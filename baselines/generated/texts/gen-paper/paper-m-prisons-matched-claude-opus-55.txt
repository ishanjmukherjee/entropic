Few areas of European human rights law have generated as much institutional activity, and as little durable change, as the regulation of conditions of detention. For more than three decades, the Council of Europe has built an oversight architecture for prisons that is, by any comparative measure, dense and sophisticated. The European Committee for the Prevention of Torture and Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CPT) conducts periodic and ad hoc visits to places of deprivation of liberty across the continent. The European Court of Human Rights has developed an extensive jurisprudence under Article 3 of the Convention, culminating in a series of pilot and quasi-pilot judgments addressing structural overcrowding and inadequate material conditions. The Committee of Ministers supervises the execution of those judgments under Article 46. Alongside these regional mechanisms, national preventive mechanisms established under the Optional Protocol to the UN Convention against Torture, ombuds institutions, and domestic courts have each assumed a share of the oversight function. Yet the problems these bodies were designed to address have proved remarkably persistent. Overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, insufficient out-of-cell time and deficient healthcare recur in report after report, often in the same states and sometimes in the same institutions.

This article examines one feature of that architecture that has received insufficient scholarly attention: the practice of formally closing oversight processes while the underlying problems remain unresolved. I call this phenomenon closure without resolution. It occurs most visibly when the Committee of Ministers adopts a final resolution ending its supervision of a group of cases on the basis that a respondent state has adopted general measures, such as new legislation, a domestic compensation scheme or a preventive remedy, even though the conditions that gave rise to the violations have not been durably remedied and, in some instances, have subsequently deteriorated. The Italian experience following Torreggiani v Italy is the paradigmatic example. Supervision was closed in 2016 after a temporary reduction in the prison population and the introduction of domestic remedies, yet overcrowding returned to levels that, by many accounts, approach or exceed those that prompted the pilot judgment. Similar dynamics, though differing in their details, can be observed elsewhere. Closure without resolution is not confined to the execution process. It also appears, in subtler forms, in the cyclical character of CPT reporting, in the acceptance of action plans as evidence of compliance, and in the tendency of domestic remedies to convert systemic failures into individualised compensation claims.

The argument of this article is threefold. First, I contend that closure without resolution is not best understood as a series of isolated failures of political will or institutional capacity, although both play a part. It is a structural consequence of the way European prison oversight has come to define success. Oversight bodies have increasingly measured compliance by reference to the adoption of measures rather than the achievement of outcomes, a shift that reflects both the legitimate constraints of subsidiarity and the practical difficulty of sustaining supervision over open-ended structural problems. Second, I argue that this conception of compliance has distinctive costs in the prison context. Because detention conditions are acutely sensitive to fluctuations in criminal justice policy, sentencing practice and public finances, measures that produce improvement at one moment offer little assurance of continued conformity with Article 3. Closure based on a snapshot therefore risks legitimising arrangements that are inherently unstable. Third, I suggest that the fragmentation of oversight across multiple bodies, often presented as a source of resilience, can exacerbate rather than mitigate the problem, since each institution may reasonably treat continued monitoring as the responsibility of another.

The article proceeds as follows. Part II sets out the institutional framework of European prison oversight and the legal bases on which each mechanism operates, with particular attention to the rules governing the closure of supervision by the Committee of Ministers. Part III develops the concept of closure without resolution and distinguishes it from related phenomena, including non-compliance, partial compliance and so-called creative compliance. Part IV examines a selection of execution processes concerning prison conditions, tracing the reasoning that underpinned decisions to close supervision and the subsequent trajectory of conditions on the ground. Part V considers the normative implications, assessing the justifications commonly advanced for closure, among them subsidiarity, institutional economy and the avoidance of indefinite supervision, against the protective purpose of Article 3. Part VI proposes a set of reforms, including outcome-based closure criteria, mechanisms for the reactivation of supervision, and more formalised channels between the CPT, national preventive mechanisms and the execution process. The article concludes by reflecting on what closure without resolution reveals about the limits of procedural oversight as a means of securing substantive rights for people in detention.
