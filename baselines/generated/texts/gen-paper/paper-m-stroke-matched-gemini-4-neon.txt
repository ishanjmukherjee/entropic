Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term adult disability and mortality worldwide. While advancements in acute medical management and thrombolytic therapies have significantly improved survival rates, individuals entering the chronic phase of stroke recovery—typically defined as more than six months post-event—face substantial ongoing health challenges. Chief among these is the elevated risk of recurrent cerebrovascular events, which carry a markedly higher rate of mortality and severe functional decline than initial strokes. Hypertension is the most prevalent and potent modifiable risk factor for both primary and secondary stroke. Despite the widespread availability of antihypertensive pharmacotherapy, blood pressure (BP) control remains suboptimal in a significant proportion of stroke survivors due to factors such as medication non-adherence, resistant hypertension, and complex comorbidities. Consequently, non-pharmacological interventions, particularly structured physical exercise, are increasingly emphasized as essential adjuncts in secondary stroke prevention guidelines.

Regular physical activity is well-established as an effective strategy for lowering resting BP and improving overall cardiovascular health. A key physiological mechanism underlying the chronic antihypertensive effects of exercise is post-exercise hypotension (PEH), defined as a sustained reduction in systolic and/or diastolic BP below resting baseline levels following a single bout of exercise. In both normotensive and hypertensive adults, PEH can occur within minutes of exercise cessation and persist for several hours, thereby reducing the daily hemodynamic load on the cardiovascular system. Furthermore, the magnitude of acute PEH has been shown to correlate strongly with the long-term resting BP reductions achieved through chronic exercise training. Understanding the acute hemodynamic responses to different exercise modalities is therefore critical for optimizing exercise prescriptions aimed at BP management in high-risk clinical populations.

To date, the majority of research investigating PEH and cardiovascular adaptations in stroke survivors has focused on aerobic exercise, such as treadmill walking, overground gait training, or stationary cycling. However, individuals in the chronic phase of stroke recovery frequently experience profound neuromuscular impairments, including hemiparesis, spasticity, muscle atrophy, and reduced muscular strength, which significantly limit functional mobility and independence. Resistance exercise (RE) is uniquely suited to address these neuromuscular deficits and is strongly recommended by major clinical guidelines to improve muscle strength, gait speed, balance, and the capacity to perform activities of daily living post-stroke. Beyond its well-established musculoskeletal benefits, emerging evidence in healthy and hypertensive non-stroke populations suggests that RE can also elicit meaningful acute reductions in BP, comparable in some instances to those observed following aerobic exercise.

Despite its clear functional benefits, the cardiovascular safety and acute hemodynamic effects of RE in stroke survivors remain insufficiently characterized. Historically, clinicians have exercised caution when prescribing RE to patients with cerebrovascular disease due to concerns regarding the pronounced acute pressor response—characterized by transient, sharp spikes in BP—that can occur during high-intensity isometric or dynamic contractions. While appropriate breathing techniques and moderate-intensity protocols can effectively mitigate these intra-exercise surges, there is a notable paucity of data examining the recovery period immediately following RE in this population. Specifically, it remains unclear whether individuals with chronic stroke—who frequently present with impaired autonomic regulation, blunted baroreflex sensitivity, increased arterial stiffness, and complex antihypertensive medication regimens—exhibit a typical PEH response after a single session of RE, or whether their post-exercise BP response is attenuated or paradoxical.

Addressing this gap in the literature is essential for refining evidence-based exercise prescriptions that safely and effectively target both neuromuscular rehabilitation and secondary cardiovascular prevention. Therefore, the primary objective of the present study was to investigate the acute systolic and diastolic BP responses following a single bout of moderate-intensity resistance exercise compared to a seated non-exercise control condition in individuals in the chronic phase of stroke recovery. We hypothesized that a structured, supervised RE session would be hemodynamically well-tolerated and would elicit a statistically significant post-exercise hypotensive response relative to both resting baseline and control values. The findings of this investigation aim to inform clinical practice by clarifying the short-term cardiovascular impact of RE in this vulnerable population.
