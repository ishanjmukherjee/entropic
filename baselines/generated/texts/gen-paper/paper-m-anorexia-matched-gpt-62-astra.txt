“I want to recover, but I do not know who I would be without anorexia.” There is no contradiction in this thought. A person can recognize that an illness has narrowed her life and still find it difficult to imagine that life continuing, as hers, in its absence. Yet the thought poses a problem for the philosophy of psychiatry. If it expresses a conflict between the person and the illness, why should the prospect of relief from the latter threaten her sense of the former? If, instead, anorexia nervosa has become part of who she is, on what grounds can anyone distinguish a commitment she has reason to preserve from one she might welcome help in relinquishing? Both interpretations capture something important. Neither, on its own, makes adequate sense of what is at stake in recovery.

Anorexia nervosa is a serious psychiatric disorder whose effects can extend across a person’s bodily experience, relationships, and practical life. Its relationship to self-identity is often approached through the clinical concept of ego-syntonicity: some features of the disorder may be experienced as consonant with a person’s values or aspirations rather than as unwelcome intrusions. This concept marks an important contrast, but consonance is not yet identity. A person may endorse a practice without taking it to define her; conversely, she may experience a practice she now repudiates as inseparable from the person she has become. Indeed, the question “Who would I be without it?” may become most pressing precisely when endorsement has begun to weaken. An account framed solely in terms of what the person currently wants will therefore miss a central dimension of her predicament.

By self-identity, I mean neither numerical identity over time nor a fixed set of traits that makes someone the person she really is. The concern here is with practical self-understanding: the descriptions under which a person recognizes her actions as intelligible, connects her past to her present, and envisages a future in which she can participate. On this account, an illness need not be valued in its own right to acquire a role in sustaining identity. It may, for example, come to organize what counts for someone as evidence of perseverance. Relinquishing an illness-related practice can then involve two distinct difficulties: acting against an established habit and losing a familiar way of recognizing in oneself a quality one still values. The difference matters because the second difficulty cannot be resolved simply by pointing out the costs of the first.

This paper argues that the relation between anorexia and self-identity is best understood by distinguishing the roles an illness has come to play from the authority a person grants it in deciding how to live. That distinction leaves room for variation. Not everyone with anorexia experiences the disorder as part of who they are, and the same person may describe it differently across settings or stages of illness. Nor is its place in a life determined by solitary reflection. A family that comes to interpret every disagreement as the illness speaking may inadvertently make it harder for the person to be heard in any other capacity. Yet treating every avowed commitment as authoritative merely because it is sincerely expressed presents a corresponding danger: it overlooks the possibility that the person is trying, without yet succeeding, to acquire a different standpoint.

The discussion proceeds by examining two familiar ways of speaking about anorexia: as an alien force from which the person must be distinguished, and as an aspect of the self whose significance others must acknowledge. I consider what each description allows a person to say, as well as what it can prevent others from hearing. I then develop an account of identity change that brings bodily habits and social recognition into conversation with narrative approaches to the self. The practical question is not whether recovery restores a self that existed before illness or requires the invention of an entirely new one. It is how a person can find enough continuity to make a changed life recognizable as her own, without continuity becoming a demand that she remain as she is.
