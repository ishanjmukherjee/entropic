The kettle whistle shook me while I
read the gas card twice – your mother's
name still on the lease, the letter
folded in your winter coat you left
behind the spare room door. The bus
swerved on Jasper, splashed my jeans,
and I thought you'd laugh, you always
laughed when plans fell through.

You wound the cord around that lamp,
the one we stole from Rick's garage,
its plastic shade cracked from the move,
it flickered like a pulse. I stood there
holding both our winter gloves,
wondering which pairs you'd take.

The kettle whistles different now – I
let it scream until the neighbour
thumps the wall. Your bus pass
expired yesterday, I found it
tucked behind the microwave. The
lamp cord hangs straight down,
untwisted, cold. I bought new bulbs
but haven't changed the shade.

Your bike still chained outside the
co-op, rust blooming through the
lock. The cashier asked me Tuesday
where you've been – I said you're
siccing big in Calgary, the lie came
easy as your name off my tongue.

I walked past Rick's last night, the
garage door yawning dark, that same
old oil stain like a map of nowhere.
My pockets full of nothing heavy
now, the glove drawer empty, the
kettle quiet, the cord straight down –
I left my spare key on the sill
where yours used to gleam.
