My father kept a ferry
Where the river bent like a knee.
He charged a penny going to town
And brought you home for free.
He said, “A man might spend his last
On flour and a little tea,
But that’s no cause to leave him there
On the bank across from me.”

Oh, pull away, my darling,
Pull the oar and let her run.
There’s a light in the ferry window
Long after the setting sun.

My brother worked the early shift
When they built the dam at Lee.
He bought my mother a reading lamp
With the first of his weekly fee.
She sat and read by a steady light
Where the wick once smoked the wall,
And I lay listening half the night
For the shoals below the mill.

Oh, pull away, my darling,
Pull the oar and let her run.
There’s a light in the ferry window
Long after the setting sun.

The day they closed the iron gates,
Dad brought the ferry in.
He hung the oars above the door
On two old pegs of tin.
By June the water reached the sill,
By fall the chimney stone,
And the ferry bell on our new front step
Was all we could bring home.

My daughter likes to row the lake.
She takes me out at dawn.
“Where did you live when you were small?”
I tell her, “Row straight on.”
And when we reach the middle,
Where the swallows dip and skim,
I ring that bell above the blue
As if to call for him.

Oh, pull away, my darling,
Pull the oar and let her run.
There’s a light on the ferry water
Where the morning meets the sun.
