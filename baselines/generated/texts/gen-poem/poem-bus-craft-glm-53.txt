The number nine threads east on Chicago Avenue,
eleven o'clock, the driver's window cracked
to let the heat out and the moths in.

A woman boards with a laundry bag
and two quarts of orange juice,
drops her coins and gathers them one at a time
while everyone mercifully looks elsewhere.

Under the blue fluorescents a man in coveralls
sleeps through the stops the driver calls,
his hard hat upside down on the seat beside him,
holding nothing.

Across the aisle two nurses
argue softly about a dog,
whether it ate the whole ham or only the ends.
The taller one laughs into her sleeve.

Pigeons roost under the viaduct
as we pass. A boy with headphones
mouthes every word to a song
no one else can hear.

The bus leans at Ashland,
a shopping cart parked at the shelter,
one wheel spinning slower and slower.

Outside a bar with its plywood window
a man raises his glass to us,
to whom exactly, unclear,
and the nine pulls away, its sign
still promising a destination
that no longer exists.
