They were never soft, those hands,
not even when they cradled me,
a newborn weight he balanced
like a cup filled to the brim.

I learned the map of them by heart:
the white scar where a chisel slipped,
the thumbnail, split and ridged,
the knuckles swollen up like knots in pine.

They smelled of motor oil and soap,
of cut grass, coffee, cold December air.
They knew the language of a stubborn bolt,
the patient art of coaxing seeds to rise.

I remember them on the steering wheel,
ten and two, steady through the rain,
and later, guiding mine across the dash:
easy now, just feel the road.

They never said the things he couldn't say.
Instead they fixed the hinge, the leaking pipe,
the bicycle chain, the broken kite,
the porch step I had told him nothing of.

Now they rest on the blanket's edge,
thin-skinned, spotted, trembling slightly,
and I hold one the way he held me once,
careful, as if it might spill.

And I see my own hand wrapped in his,
the same square palm, the same blunt fingers,
and I understand at last
what he was building all those years.
