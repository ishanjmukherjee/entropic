The café on Via dei Giubbonari opens at seven.
You arrive with flour on your sleeve
from the bakery where you hold your night shift,
say nothing about the ovens,
ask if I've eaten.

The man at the counter counts coins
slowly, as if each centesimo
had lived a small life.
You order two caffè lunghi
and one cornetto, split it
with a butter knife you brought from home.

Outside, a dog barks at a motorino.
You tell me your sister's wedding dress
caught fire on a radiator
and they married anyway,
smelling of burnt lace.

At the corner table, an old woman
feeds a pigeon from her purse.
The waiter lets her.

You take my wrist, turn it over,
press your thumb to the blue veins
as if checking the time.
Then you begin reading aloud
from yesterday's newspaper,
the horoscope section only,
yours declaring "a stranger will
change your afternoon."

The pigeon hobbles closer.
You tear the last corner of cornetto,
hold it out on your open palm.
