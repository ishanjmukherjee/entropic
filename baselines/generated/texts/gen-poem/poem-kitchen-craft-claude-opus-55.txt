At twenty to five in Hibbing the furnace kicks on
and my father fills the thermos from the percolator,
holding the cap in his teeth.
The radio on the breadbox reads the shift changes,
nine below at Chisholm, a Lutheran funeral at ten.
He has already cut the bread. The knife
lies on the board in its crumbs.
My mother in her housecoat counts pills
into the lid of a mayonnaise jar
and keeps her back to the window,
where the streetlight shows snow
filling the bed of his truck.
He screws the cup down, then a quarter turn more.
In 1921 the company moved half the town
a mile south on timbers and steel wheels,
the houses, the hotel, a church with its bell,
because the ore was under it.
Her grandmother's stove rode the whole way with the fire banked.
He wipes the counter with the flat of his hand,
sweeps the crumbs into his other palm,
and stands holding them
while the truck idles in the drive
and the thermos ticks on the table, taking the heat.
