The kettle starts its low complaint
before the window's fully gray,
and steam unthreads itself in loops
that drift toward the cupboard's sway.

A drawer of spoons that never match,
a knife worn thinner than it was,
the table with its one short leg
we steady with a folded cause:

a letter no one answered, creased
to fit the gap, and holding still.
The onions sweat. The butter browns.
The radio hums on the sill.

Here flour settles into seams
no sponge has ever fully cleared,
and every scar along the board
remembers someone who was here.

My mother's hands, my grandmother's,
the same slow turning of the wrist,
the pinch of salt that isn't measured,
the recipe that won't exist

on any card, in any book,
but lives the way that warmth survives:
passed hand to hand above the stove,
the quiet liturgy of lives.

And when the dishes dry at night,
and all the burners click to black,
the kitchen keeps its patient hum,
and waits for morning to come back.
