In the feed store on Route 9, my father
counted nails out of a bin by feel,
his thumb going over each head
like a man reading a letter twice.

At five o'clock he'd come in
from the machine shed, wash at the sink,
and the water would run gray
longer than seemed possible.

His knuckles were the size of walnuts.
One thumbnail was split and dark
from the hay baler, summer of '71,
and he never had it fixed.

He could lift the front end of a Rototiller.
He cracked walnuts in his palm for us
on Sunday, or shelled peanuts
one at a time, slower than eating them.

The night the electric fence shorted out
he stood in the yard in October
holding the wire with his bare hand
to find the pulse of the fault,

and I stood at the window
watching that hand open and close,
open and close, in the dark,
like a man learning something
he would never tell.

Now his hands lie on the blanket
at the home outside Anamosa,
fingers curled around nothing,
and still they hold it.
