His knuckles are maps of the county
where I grew up, cracked earth

under the nails, Laconia dirt.
At five a.m. he holds the flashlight

for me, steady, while I drop
the worm on the hook. His hands

smell of gasoline and the bread
he kneaded before we woke.

Last Tuesday I saw him pause
above the lake water, tracing

with two fingers the line
where the ice gave way in '67,

then cup the cold and drink.
He holds the plane iron still

against the grain, making curly
shavings that fall like the skin

of peeled apples into his boots.
His hands have throbbed all night

thinking about my sister's letters
though he never opened one.

Now he lifts the thermos,
the cap dented once by a

hydraulic jack in the mill,
and pours coffee into the snow.

The steam rises between us,
his thumb pressing the lid,

the nail split clean down
the middle, holding on.
