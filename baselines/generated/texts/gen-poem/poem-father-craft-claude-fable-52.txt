Quarter to six at Gariahat, the tube lights still on,
my father lifts a hilsa by the gills
and presses its belly with his thumb
the way he presses a mango, or my forehead for fever.
The fishmonger says twelve hundred.
My father says nothing and puts the fish down.

His nails are cut square with a blade, not a clipper.
The left thumbnail grew back ridged
after a tram door on Rashbehari Avenue,
and he uses it now to open envelopes.

He picks the fish up again. Eleven hundred.
The boti stands upright between the monger's feet
and the fish goes onto it, head first,
scales flying up onto my father's wrist, his watch,
the hair on his forearm.
He does not brush them off.

He has never once eaten hilsa. The bones, he says.

He counts notes out of a folded handkerchief,
wetting a finger for each,
takes the parcel, the newspaper already going dark,
and hooks its jute string over two fingers.
With the other hand he finds the back of my collar
and steers me down the wet lane between baskets,
one scale on his knuckle
showing white each time we pass under a light.
