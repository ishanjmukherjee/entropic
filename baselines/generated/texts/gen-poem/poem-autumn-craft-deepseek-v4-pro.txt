Across the railroad siding in Hokkaido
a boy unwinds a scarf and hangs it
on the rusted chain-link fence by the freight dock.
It is half past four. The last of the sun
flattens against the warehouse tin
like a thumb on a bruise.

Two men in quilted vests
are stacking persimmon crates inside the open car.
One counts under his breath—nijūgo, nijūroku—
the other wipes his forehead with the back of his glove
and leaves a smear of newsprint.
The fruit gives off a thin medicinal sweetness
that troubles the air but does not warm it.

An empty bottle rolls in the gravel
each time a gust hits the lot,
nudging a flattened cigarette pack
with the blue and white of the Hokkaido map
still legible on the foil.

The boy’s hands are chapped. He cups them
and blows, and the sound is not a whistle
but the low hoot a glass gallon jug makes
when you strike the rim.
Nobody looks up. The scarf stirs once
and hangs dead. A crow lands on the coupling
between two tankers, folds its wings
exactly once, and stays there.
