The city bus at midnight exhales its brakes,
a tired sigh of air and rubber,
its windows glowing like aquarium glass
where twenty strangers float
in temporary suspension.

Blue light flickers on empty faces—
the night nurse texting her mother,
the dishwasher studying his hands,
the insomniac counting stops
until home might feel possible.

At every corner, the doors gasp open,
swallowing someone new into the warmth,
vending machine hum and plastic seats
that remember thousands of backs,
the center aisle a runway for
swaying ballet of the standing.

The driver negotiates the dark
in the mirror's narrow theater,
watching lives embark and dissolve,
his hands steady on the wheel
that steers through wet asphalt
reflecting streetlights like fallen stars.

Someone is always leaving,
someone just arrived,
and in between, the bus moves
through the city's overnight pulse,
carrying the weightless burden
of human silence and diesel dreams.

It knows every pothole by name,
every bus stop's lonely bench,
how to cradle the night shift
and the dancers and the ones
who have nowhere else to be awake,
delivering them gently
into the thin gray promise
of another day.
