At half past four the lift bridge in Duluth
rises for a laker riding low with taconite,
and the horn comes over the water twice,
once from the hull and once back off the hill.

I am carrying storm windows up from my father's basement.
Each frame is numbered in grease pencil
in the hand he had in 1987,
when he still crossed his sevens.
Number four goes over the sink.
Number nine has a cracked pane he taped in an X
and never replaced, and the tape has gone the color of tea.

The screens come down. In their corners
a summer of moths, which I knock out
against the porch rail the way he knocked his pipe.
By spring the ore in that ship will be a fender in Toledo.

Down the block a neighbor rakes maple leaves
into the gutter, where the city truck will vacuum them
Tuesday, if it doesn't snow.
I turn the last brass button on number four.
It sticks where it always stuck.

The bridge settles back onto its road.
Behind the new glass the kitchen light clicks on by its timer
and lays out on the counter for me
his reading glasses, a jar of screws,
my own two hands in his gloves.
