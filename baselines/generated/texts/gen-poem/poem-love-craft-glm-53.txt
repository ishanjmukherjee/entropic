The kettle clicks off at 5:47,
and you are still at the table
with the crossword folded to a square,
pencil behind your ear like a carpenter.

I set out two cups before I remember
you take yours black now—
since March, since the doctor,
since the word we don't use at breakfast.

Outside, the neighbor kid
practices free throws in the alley,
ball against backboard, ball against rim,
the clatter of someone getting good at failing.

You look up. You ask
whether I fed the cat.
The cat died three years ago.
I say yes, I fed her.

You nod, you drink,
your hand finds the sugar bowl
you swore off, the spoon
clinking twice against ceramic.

The kid's ball quits.
A bus drags its brake on Halsted.
You push the crossword toward me—
so I can be good at failing.
So someone will sit close while I do it.
</think>Soup later, you'll say, meaning noon,
meaning you'll stand at the stove again
though the doctor said rest.

I take the pencil from your ear
and write, in the crossword's last square,
the name of our street.
Eight letters. You check it.
It fits.
