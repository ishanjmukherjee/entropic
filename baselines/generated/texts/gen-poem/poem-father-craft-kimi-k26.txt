Galveston, 1987. Six a.m.

The ferry horn sounds. My father
rubs the steering wheel with his thumb,
back and forth, back and forth,
the vinyl worn to cloth.

His hands hold the paper sack
from the donut shop on Seawall Boulevard,
grease darkening the white
where his fingers press.

A scar runs the valley
between thumb and wrist,
a gill from the table saw
in the garage in Houston
where I was not allowed.

Now he peels the orange
he bought for me,
the peel coming off in one piece,
the oil shining his nails.

He separates the sections,
arranges them on the brown bag,
counts them, nudges one closer.

The ferry lists. He steadies the sack
with the hand that holds his coffee,
the rim of the cup chipped
where his lip will find it.

I take the orange. He watches
the water, the tankers
aligned for the harbor,
their hulls the red of old brick.

His fingers drum the wheel again.
The horn sounds back,
from somewhere we cannot see.
