The bakery on Károlyi utca opens at five.
You are already in the queue.
I watch flour settle on your wrists,
the same unbleached flour they bag
by the kilo and sell to restaurants
that do not open for breakfast.

A man carries out trays of buns.
The steam makes one window
a country you could get lost in.
You buy two. Hand me one.
The apricot inside is still too hot
to taste. We eat on the steps
of a church locked until seven.

Your sleeve catches the wind.
The Danube is not visible from here
but you insist you can smell it,
the gas station on the corner,
someone's diesel starting.
A cat crosses the square twice,
the same cat or two cats.

By six the light has changed
enough to show dust on the railings.
You pinch a crumb from my collar.
The baker now arranges cakes
in tiers, each circle smaller
than the one beneath, until
the top holds only a single
marzipan rose, pale as dough,
untouched by any tongue
since the ingredient laws changed.
