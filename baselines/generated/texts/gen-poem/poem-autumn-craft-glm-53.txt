The cider press behind the feed store in Norwich
still has last year's pulp dried to its gears.
A man in a canvas coat works the crank,
and the windfalls go in bruised, come out brown.

Four o'clock. The school bus idles
at the corner of Turnpike and Main,
its exhaust stitching a seam of diesel
into the cold.

A woman carries a ladder to her apple tree,
climbs it in Gardener's clogs, and picks
the top fruit that the deer couldn't reach.
One drops. She lets it lie.

At the coop, three hens have stopped laying.
The farmer's wife carries them by their feet
to a stump behind the barn—
the axe already there, already wet.

Down by the river, someone has stacked
a wall of wood so precise
the splitting maul leans against it
like a pencil at the edge of a sentence.

The cider man hands a paper cup
to a boy on a bicycle, no money asked.
They both watch the foam settle.
The boy drinks, spits a seed into his palm,
pockets it, and pedals off
toward the last of the light,
one hand raised, holding nothing.
