Ten to five on Lake Avenue, Duluth,
and the window over the sink is black
except where the lift bridge holds its two red lights.
She fills the kettle from the cold tap,
sets it on the back burner, the one that still catches.
The matchbox says Diamond. Three left.

On the table: his thermos with the lid off,
a pencil worn to the ferrule, the gas bill
with a phone number on the back in her hand.
She rinses the thermos though it is clean.
She shakes kibble into the steel bowl by the door.
The dog has been buried since March
under the lilac, where the ground thawed first.

A boat sounds for the bridge, one long, two short,
and the bridge sounds back.
The kettle starts its ticking.
She takes down two cups, puts one back,
takes it down again and stands it
upside down on the drainboard.

Out on the water the freighter's deck lights
slide across the window frame, a thousand feet of them,
taking as long as the kettle takes.
Frost on the inside of the glass.
The kibble in the bowl, the steam
reaching the cold pane and stopping there.
