They were rough as bark, those hands of his,
cracked at the knuckles, maps of years—
each scar a story he'd dismiss
with half a shrug, a small begrudging smile.

I remember them at the kitchen table,
palms flat on wood like roots of oak,
holding a coffee cup with steadiness
that steadied me without a word.

They fixed what broke—the hinge, the fence,
the bicycle chain, my torn jeans,
threading needle awkwardly, intent,
a surgeon in a flannel shirt.

Once, they caught me when I fell
from the apple tree, fast as grace,
then pretended they done nothing much,
as if such magic were routine.

Now my own hands look like his—
the same ropy veins, the same broad palm—
and when I mend my daughter's broken toys,
somehow it is his hands that work.
