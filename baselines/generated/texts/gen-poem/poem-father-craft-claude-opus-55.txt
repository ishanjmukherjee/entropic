Ten past six on Bayway Avenue, the refinery flare
still burning against a sky the color of dishwater,
and he'd have the Buick's hood up before the coffee finished,
the feeler gauge fanned open on the fender,
each blade stamped with its thousandths of an inch.
He'd slide the thirty-five between the tip and the strap
and pull it through, feeling for drag
the way my mother checked a sheet for damp.
His left thumbnail was black, always,
from a truck door in Perth Amboy, 1974.
The radio on the back step gave the Turnpike,
the Goethals, the Outerbridge, a tanker fire in Linden
that nobody we knew was in.
He wiped each plug on his undershirt
and threaded it by hand, four turns, five,
until the threads took, and only then the wrench,
a quarter turn, no more, he said, you'll strip the head.
When the funeral home asked us for a photograph
my brother brought one of the engine.
Nobody argued. It was a good engine.
The gauge is in my kitchen drawer with the batteries,
the thirty-five blade bent a little at the tip
where he pressed it, every morning,
toward the flare.
