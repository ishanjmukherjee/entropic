The tram from Borgo presses a yellow bulb of light
down the viale, 10:42 p.m., its bell a single dull clank
before the doors fold shut. A man two rows ahead
sets a persimmon on the empty seat beside him,
the fruit too heavy for the season, its skin the color
of a stoplight held too long on red. No one looks.
The poles sway. A teenager in a tracksuit
draws a fish in the fog on the window glass
with the worn-down heel of a plastic sandal.
The fish floats there, bodiless, then fills with neon
from a shuttered pharmacy sign. The persimmon
rolls a quarter-turn when the tram brakes
and the man traps it with his forearm, not his hand,
the way you block a child’s chest at a sudden stop.
A woman’s shopping bag tips over.
Onions. She stacks them back one by one
on her lap without lowering her chin, as if
she were dealing cards for a game she intends to win.
The bell clanks again. The fish sweats glass.
The fruit sits where a fruit should not be, whole,
warmed by the body that did not eat it,
holding its shape against the hum of the wire.
