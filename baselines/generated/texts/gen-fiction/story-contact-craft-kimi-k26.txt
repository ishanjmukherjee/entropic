The ridge was cold. David Chen pressed his forehead against the spotting scope's rubber cup and watched his daughter braid grass stems below, her knees sinking into mud that hadn't thawed properly. She'd stopped asking questions twenty minutes ago. He'd stopped offering answers.

"Binoculars won't work for that distance," she said, not looking up. "Mrs. Patterson said."

"Mrs. Patterson wants me to use calibrated instruments." The scope mount wobbled on its tripod legs, one stabilizer losing grip in the earth. He'd borrowed it from the community college, filled out the form, received the indifferent nod from the clerk who'd seen him through three divorces, two job losses, none of which registered on her face. "This is the instrument."

Kara tested the braid's tension against her palm, the way her mother used to check pie crust. She was twelve. She was impossible. She was the only person the court couldn't deny him, weekends, custody determined by his work schedule, which had never been stable, which was now this: monitoring equipment on a state wildlife ridge for a project that had lost its funding in March, that he continued without pay because Mrs. Patterson in her last email had used the word critical, and David Chen had never once in his life been critical to anything.

The coordinates arrived Tuesdays. He had found the first anomaly himself, a thermal bloom where no bloom should exist, below the reservoir where the beavers had drowned in '19. Then others began reporting similar readings, scattered through the county, never staying still long enough to photograph. Amateur skywatchers, a retired veterinarian, a deputy's wife who logged frequencies on her ham radio. The Facebook group had thirty members now. They called themselves the Tuesday Club. David never posted.

"You're not even pointing at the sky," Kara said.

"It's not in the sky."

She came up the slope sideways, boots slipping. The braid hung from her belt loop, already browning. At the scope she didn't ask permission. She was always taking things now, his phone chargers, his good pen, his cigarettes though she didn't smoke, hoarding objects against some future she wouldn't name.

Through the eyepiece she saw northwest over the valley, the water treatment facility blinking its aircraft warning, radio towers beyond that transmitting static to no receiver he could identify.

"There's nothing," she said.

"Move left forty degrees."

"I see gravel. I see—" Her body went still. He'd seen that stillness once before, age six, the emergency room, doctors saying sometimes they seize like this, though she hadn't seized, she'd simply gone elsewhere, inaccessible, and returned forty minutes later asking for apple juice.

"What is it?" He reached for the focus ring, brushing her hand.

She stepped back. The scope wobbled. "I saw an elk."

"Elk don't—"

"Dead. I saw a dead elk lying in the gravel." She was already moving downslope, boots finding purchase where his hadn't, the braid catching on a thorn bush and tearing free without her noticing.

He couldn't find it. Forty minutes searching through the eyepiece, the focus at every depth, heat distortion from the afternoon ground and birds crossing his field, power lines, nothing with the bulk she described, nothing dead or living in that quadrant of valley.

For dinner they had peanut butter sandwiches in the truck because he'd forgotten the stove. She sat against the passenger door, knees drawn up, eating little, tearing the crust into soft pellets she deposited in the cup holder.

"Your mother worried," he said, though the sentence had no destination. He wasn't allowed to speak about her mother, court order, protection order, Kara in the courtroom that day twisting a paper clip until it broke, the sound audible during his testimony. The worry had been evidence. The worry had been enough.

"I don't want to go home," she said.

"Your grandmother's?"

"Anywhere. Tomorrow I have the thing. The diversity presentation." She laughed, one syllable, not mirth. "They assigned me Chinese. I'm Korean. They don't—" She stopped. More crust, more pellets. "Mrs. Patterson chose me. Because of you. Because everyone knows."

He'd known too. The emails Mrs. Patterson sent, increasingly familiar, David, if you're available Saturday, there's been another sighting, knowing he was always available, that Kara came with him because no alternative existed, because her mother had relocated to Oregon with no forwarding, because systems designed for children's safety deposited them in trucks on ridges with fathers who pointed expensive lenses at gravel and waited for permission to become necessary.

"Tomorrow's Tuesday," he said.

"Everything's Tuesday with you."

The night sky held ordinary stars. They slept in the truck bed with the shell door cracked for air, Kara in his sleeping bag, him in her childhood blanket, too short, feet exposed. At some hour she rose to urinate behind a ponderosa. He didn't offer to accompany her. She'd stopped accepting his accompaniment at ten, resumed briefly after the court, abandoned it again.

He found her in the morning at the scope, looking through.

The elk lay where she'd described. Distance and angle made it visible now, or it had moved, or appeared, curled fetally, the anthlers not branching correctly, too many points, the body lacking the correct proportions, the visible eye reflecting instrument light not as an elk's eye would.

"The Tuesday Club thinks it's alien," he said.

"Nothing's alien anymore." Kara's voice didn't sound like morning voices should, cracked with sleep. "Everything came here already. We're the thing that doesn't belong." She rotated the focus. The body in the scope sharpened, or the blur became interpretable as intention, as waiting. "It looks like it's breathing."

The club's coordinates arrived at 6:47 AM, forty minutes early, locations scattered through three counties now, including this ridge, this quadrangle, David Chen's name appended to the distribution list as origin point, as she who found first, as critical finally, the word Mrs. Patterson had never attached to his actual employment, his monitoring, his weekends on the ridge his daughter would remember or not remember, braided grass already browning in the cup holder.

Kara handed him the scope. Her fingers left oil on the adjustment ring. She descended to where the gravel slope began, where the not-elk waited, walking not running, braiding her hair as she went, a single plait she could only complete with her mother's hands, with his hands too thick, with whatever emerged from the valley's thermal distortion to meet her halfway, to continue the work of making and unmaking that required no permission, no explanation of what any moment meant, no reconciliation with what watched her coming, what had never been far, what kept arriving.
