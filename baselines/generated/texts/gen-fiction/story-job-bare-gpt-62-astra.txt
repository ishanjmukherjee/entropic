On her first morning at the county lost-property office, Nina arrived wearing one black shoe and one navy shoe. She noticed in the lift. For the remaining three floors she stood with her feet crossed, as though waiting to be photographed beside a horse.

The man training her was called Geoff. He wore a cardigan with a burn hole over the heart and did not look at her feet.

“First thing,” he said, unlocking the grille, “never suggest the answer.”

He took a green umbrella from the rack. “Someone says they’ve lost an umbrella. You don’t say, ‘Was it green?’ You ask what colour.”

“What if they don’t remember?”

“People remember all sorts of things instead.”

At nine fifteen a girl came in for a flute. She couldn’t name the manufacturer or describe the case, but said if you opened the little pocket there would be three loose raisins and a note from her mother saying DON’T FORGET FLUTE. There were four raisins. Geoff let her have it.

At ten, a cyclist claimed a set of false teeth. He put them in to prove ownership, smiled beautifully, then took them out again because, he explained, he didn’t like to cycle in them.

By lunchtime Nina had forgotten her shoes.

She had been out of work for eleven months. During that time her sister had started paying for lunch whenever they met, doing it early, on the way back from the toilets, so there could be no argument. Nina had come to hate the sight of her sister getting up from a restaurant table. Now, eating an egg sandwich at a desk with her own extension number, she sent a message: Friday lunch? My treat.

At two Geoff went downstairs to argue about shelving. At two minutes past, an elderly woman in a yellow raincoat approached the counter.

“I left something on the number six,” she said. “A voice.”

Nina waited.

“My husband’s voice. In a machine.”

“A recorder?”

“That would be the word.”

There were eight recorders in the database. Nina asked when it had been lost.

“Tuesday. Unless today is Tuesday.”

“Today is Thursday.”

“Then Tuesday.”

One recorder had arrived on Wednesday morning, listed as having been found at the bus depot. Nina fetched it in its clear plastic bag.

“Could you describe what’s recorded on it?”

The woman considered this. “A man saying the word ‘screwdriver’ rather more often than you’d wish.”

Nina put in the office headphones and pressed play.

A drawer opened. Metal implements knocked together. A man said, “Not that screwdriver. The little screwdriver. If I’d meant the great bloody screwdriver I’d have said.”

Then a woman’s voice, unmistakably the one across the counter: “Well, you married me. You could have had Pauline with the toolbox.”

The man laughed until he coughed.

Nina stopped the recording. The woman had unbuttoned her raincoat. Under it she wore a jumper darned at both elbows in different reds.

“He was teaching me to mend the kitchen radio,” she said. “He thought I ought to learn a few things.”

Nina slid the claim form across the counter and showed her where to sign.

“Did you mend it?” she asked.

“Oh no. We ruined it. Is there much on there after that bit?”

“The counter says forty minutes.”

“Good,” the woman said. “I’ve never got past the screwdriver.”

She tucked the recorder into an inside pocket and patted it twice. At the door she turned back.

“One of your shoes is blue, dear.”

When Geoff returned, Nina was entering the claim. Under identifying detail she had typed contents of recording, which seemed enough.

“Any trouble?” he asked.

“None.”

At four thirty a boy came in looking for a brown glove. There were seventeen brown gloves, none of them pairs. Asked for a distinguishing feature, he said, “It’s the left one.”

“That narrows it down,” Nina said, and laid out nine.

He tried them one at a time. The eighth fitted. He pulled a second glove from his pocket and held his hands up together. The gloves were different shades of brown, with different stitching at the wrists.

“Ah,” said Nina.

“No, that’s them,” the boy said. “They’ve always been like that.”

She gave him the form.

On the bus home her sister replied that Friday would be lovely. Nina booked the little Turkish place near the station. She would get there ten minutes early and give the waiter her card.

Across the aisle, a tired-looking man was feeding his daughter pieces of peeled orange. The girl dropped a segment. It landed on Nina’s navy shoe, leaving a bright wet crescent on the toe.

“Sorry,” the man said.

Nina bent to pick it up. For the first time that day she looked at her shoes side by side without moving either foot. Both needed polishing. The black one, she saw now, had begun to split along the seam.

“Never mind,” she said, and rode on to her stop.
