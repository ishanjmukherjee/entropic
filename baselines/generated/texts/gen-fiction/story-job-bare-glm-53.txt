The elevator smelled like lemon cleaner and something else Maya couldn't identify—coffee, maybe, or the faint industrial tang of new carpet. She checked her badge for the fourth time. The photo was terrible, her hair flattened by rain from two weeks ago when HR had taken it, but the name was right: Maya Reyes, Accounts Coordinator, Floor Six.

She'd been unemployed for four months before this. Four months of sending resumes into what felt like a void, of rehearsing answers to interview questions in her bathroom mirror, of telling her mother that yes, things were looking up. So when the offer came, she'd almost cried on the phone, which she then worried about for three straight days.

Floor six was quieter than she expected. A long hallway lined with plants that were clearly plastic but arranged with enough care to suggest someone cared about the illusion. She found her desk in an open area near the windows—a monitor, a chair, a small welcome card with a candy bar taped to it. Glad you're here! — The Team. No names, but it was something.

"You must be Maya." A woman appeared beside her, holding two mugs. She was maybe fifty, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair. "I'm Denise. I sit across from you. Fair warning, I talk a lot, but I also know where everything is, so it evens out."

Maya took the offered mug. Coffee, cream, no sugar—Denise had somehow guessed right. "Thank you. For this. For—"

"Don't thank me yet. The printer on this floor is possessed. Come on, I'll show you where the supply closet is before Carol hordes all the good pens."

The morning moved in pieces. A login that took three attempts. A meeting she sat in on where everyone discussed a client deliverable using acronyms she silently wrote down to look up later: DSO, the Meridian account, Q3 numbers. At one point the manager, a soft-spoken man named Hargrove, asked if she had questions, and she asked one, and it turned out to be a reasonable question, and several people nodded as though she'd said something intelligent. She exhaled for the first time in an hour.

At noon, Denise waved her toward a group heading out. "Lunch crew. We're getting Thai. You're coming. This is not optional, but it's also free, so nobody complains."

Six of them crowded into a booth at a place five blocks south, and Maya learned things that no onboarding document would have told her: that the Thai place gave extra spring rolls if you asked for Priya by name; that Hargrove had once fallen asleep in a client presentation and everyone pretended not to notice for exactly one year before bringing it up; that the building's sixth floor used to be legal, and that was why the supply closet had a weird brass doorknob and what appeared to be a dramatic floor safe nobody had ever opened.

"Seventeen years I've worked here," Denise said, spearing a noodle. "I've decided the safe has nothing in it. That's sadder than if it had money in it. An empty safe, locked all this time for nothing. Like most things, honestly."

"Debbie downer," muttered a young man from marketing named Felix, and Maya laughed, actually laughed, and it startled her how easy it was.

The afternoon was harder. There was a spreadsheet she was supposed to update, and it referenced systems she hadn't been given access to yet, and for forty minutes she sat there feeling the old panic rise—the fear that she was an impostor, that they'd realize their mistake, that four months of unemployment had been the universe's correct assessment of her. She almost emailed Hargrove to confess her confusion.

Instead she asked Denise.

"Nobody gets access on day one," Denise said, already typing. "It's a whole ritual. You have to fill out a form. IT loses the form. You fill it out again. By Thursday you'll be in. Until then, do what everyone does." She leaned over and pointed at the screen. "Make it look like you're working. That's the real first job. The second job comes later."

"There's going to be a second job?"

"There's going to be a lot of jobs, honey. Today is just the one where you learn where the bathroom is."

By five o'clock, Maya had updated what she could, faked what she couldn't, eaten half a candy bar, and learned where the bathroom was. She rode the elevator down alone, watching the floor numbers fall. Outside, the rain had stopped, the streets streaked and shining under the streetlights.

She took out her phone. Her mother would want a full report, wanting to hear that it had all gone perfectly, and Maya knew she'd end up giving her mostly the truth: the meeting, the lunch, the spreadsheet, the kind woman across the desk. She would leave out the forty minutes of quiet panic, mostly because it no longer felt like the whole story. It felt like one small part of a day she had, against her own expectations, survived.

She turned her badge over in her pocket. The terrible photo, the correct name.

She'd bought a monthly train pass on the way in. Not a single-ride ticket. A monthly.

Maya walked toward the station, and for the first time in months, she wasn't rehearsing anything.
