Keith Brannigan was holding the photocell up to the window as if it might be fogged, and Ronald, who had been asked not to touch anything, was rubbing the brass of the old pedestal with a sock.

"It comes on by itself," Keith said. "When the light drops. You won't have to do a thing."

"I don't do anything now."

"No. Well."

Pat called up the stairwell that the soup was on, and Keith called back that he wouldn't, thanks, the boat was at four. Ronald turned his wrist over. He wore his watch face-in, the way his father had, so that checking it looked like checking a pulse. Ten past two. The boat would be at four if Colin Hesketh had got his engine back from Pengelly's, and Colin hadn't, because Pengelly was waiting on a gasket from Plymouth and had been since Tuesday.

They ate in the kitchen anyway. Pat had put out the bowls with the blue rim, which came down at Christmas and for the man from Trinity House, and Keith held his with both hands under the chin. He wore his wedding ring on his right hand. Pat looked at it once and then at the bread.

"You'll have done a lot of these," she said.

"This is my eleventh. Twelfth. Longships was the first."

"And the keepers," Pat said. "Where do they go?"

"Pat."

"I'm only asking."

Keith tore his bread into small, even pieces and didn't eat them. "One went to his sister's in Redruth. One bought a caravan. There was a fellow at Godrevy took up the organ."

"Ronald doesn't play anything."

"I can play," Ronald said.

"He can't."

Keith laughed, then stopped, as though he'd been caught at something. He asked what would happen to the cottage. Ronald said they'd sell it to somebody from Bristol who'd put a hot tub where the coal bunker was. Keith asked how much, did he think, roughly, and Pat said she'd no idea, and Ronald said nobody would want it in February, and Keith said no, he supposed not, and put a piece of bread in his mouth at last.

The phone went at half three. It was Colin. Ronald listened, said right, said tomorrow then, said no, it's no bother, and hung up. When he came back Keith was standing with his coat already on and his bag on his shoulder, and Ronald told him, and Keith took the bag off and set it down by the door and stood looking at it.

"I'm sorry," Ronald said, though it was nothing to do with him.

"No," Keith said. "No, that's all right."

Pat went to find the spare blankets. She took the ones from the cedar chest, not the airing cupboard, and Ronald heard the lid go and knew which ones they were.

At ten to five he went up. He had not decided to; his legs did the stairs the way they had done them for nineteen years, and on the ninety-first step, where the paint was worn to the iron in the shape of a boot, the beam came round and went across him through the slot in the wall. He had not heard it start. There had always been the business of it before, the click and the hiss and his own hand. He stood on the step with one palm flat on the cold rail and let it come round again, and again. Then he went down.

Pat had made up the settee and gone to bed early, which she never did. Keith was sitting at the kitchen table with the telephone pulled over to him on its long flex, the receiver tucked between his ear and his shoulder. He wasn't dialling. Ronald could hear the tone from the doorway, thin and steady.

"I was going to ring home," Keith said.

"Go on, then."

"It's late for them."

It was a quarter past eight. Ronald filled the kettle and stood with his back to the table while it boiled, so as not to watch. Behind him the tone stopped, and there was a long gap, and then the receiver went back in its cradle without a word having been said into it.

"Do you get used to it?" Keith said. "The noise."

"What noise?"

Keith opened his mouth and then gestured, vaguely, at the window, the wall, the whole of the outside.

"Oh," Ronald said. "That."

He made two teas and put one in front of Keith, who thanked him and wrapped both hands around it the same way he'd held the soup. Then Ronald took the log down from the shelf by the barometer. The last entry was in his own hand from the night before: Lit 16:49. Wind SW 4. Vis good. Under it there was nothing, and there would be nothing, because there was no need, the man had explained all that in the morning with a diagram.

Ronald uncapped his pen. Keith watched him across the table and said nothing.

Lit 16:51, Ronald wrote. He looked at it. Then he put Wind SW 5, which was a guess, and blotted it with the side of his hand, and left the book open on the table to dry.
