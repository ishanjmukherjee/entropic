The laminator was already jammed when Elena found it, four degrees too hot, the carrier melted around a temp badge for someone named Rajid. She pried it open with her thumb, felt the heat ridge her nail bed white, and left both badges in the drawer beneath the phone list. She'd been shown the drawer two hours earlier by a woman whose own badge, dangling from a braided lanyard, read only "Volunteer" on the masking tape beneath the clip.

The volunteer—Gwen, she had said, though whether that was the name on some other tape Elena never learned—had demonstrated the coffee system by running a full pot down the sink. "The good stuff," she'd said, of the sink, as if this explained something. The building had three floors, 2 and 3 for counseling and drug court, the basement for copy machines and the laminator and a two-person office Elena would share with M. Torres, who was not present and whose desk displayed a photo of a small dog on a boat.

Elena was meant to replace someone. This had not been said. It was in the desk itself, center drawer squeaking out to reveal the ghost arrangement of someone's former necessities: a pewter paperclip in the shape of a fish, three cough drops fused to their wax wrappers, a rubber band sprung into a perfect zero. She left them undisturbed and opened the left drawer instead, deeper, where new file folders were stacked with the elastic bands still on, sharp enough to catch.

The first call came before she'd found the phone list. "Family services," she said, the greeting Gwen had whispered to her while pouring coffee down the drain.

A man asked if this was where he got the paper signed. His voice carried the hollow particular to speakerphone, though he was holding the receiver; she could tell by how he cupped his breath.

"Which paper," she said, not asking.

"The one for the thing. The class."

She had no list of classes. The phone list, when she found it in the melted-badge drawer, showed only extensions, no names corresponding to numbers, no titles. M. Torres's extension was 114, her own was 115, and 116 rang out to a storage closet she'd passed on Gwen's abbreviated tour, empty except for a roll of carpet pad and a fan missing its cage.

"I'll transfer you," Elena said, and pressed 116, and let it ring twelve times before the man hung up.

At eleven-thirty Gwen returned with a Greek salad in a plastic clamshell that wanted to stay shut. She wedged it open with a stapler. "You're not eating," she said.

"I didn't bring anything."

"There's a vending machine. By the stairs." Gwen spoke with her fork aimed at the carpet pad storage closet, though the stairs were the opposite direction. "M&Ms gets stuck. You have to hit the left side, not the front."

"I'll remember."

Gwen blinked, something wet gathering at the corner of her left eye, not quite tears, more the automatic response of a body consulted too often on matters it refused to resolve. "Torres is at a conference. Through Thursday. You're meant to shadow, but."

"I'll manage."

"There's a binder." Gwen gestured with lettuce. "Orange. Somewhere."

Elena found it at three o'clock, beneath the desk, kicked inward and buckling under someone's heel. The cover page, in eleven-point Calibri, read "Intake Procedures (DRAFT 2016)" and the second page was blank, and the third contained a flowchart in which every box terminated at an octagon marked "DISPOSITION PENDING," with no arrows leading out.

The man with the paper called back at four-fifteen. She recognized the breath, the way he held the receiver.

"Family services."

"Yeah, I called before. About the class."

"The class," she said, and opened the orange binder to a random page, which showed a line graph of something rising and not falling, years 2014-2018, no y-axis label.

"For my kid. The judge said."

She found the phone list in the melted-badge drawer. Ran her thumb down extensions. 101, 102, 103. All numbers she had not been assigned, would not answer, could not guess at. 114 was Torres, absent through Thursday. 115 was herself. 116 the storage closet.

"What day," she said, "is the class."

"Tuesday. It was supposed to be Tuesday." The man's voice frayed at the edges, what thread remained pulled by something beyond her naming. "They said call this number. They said you'd have the list."

She had no list. She had a flowchart terminating in octagons, a line graph rising toward nothing, a stapler that smelled of Greek dressing, a desk arranged for someone who no longer sat there, a laminate of Rajid, still warm when she'd pried it free.

"I'll transfer you," she said, and pressed 116, and this time she did not hang up, only held the receiver against her shoulder while the storage closet rang, twenty times, thirty, the open mouth of the missing fan cage staring from the desk photo where the small dog on the boat had once been, or perhaps still was, the frame angled away from her now, something she had done or not done, arrangement and rearrangement, the paperclip fish waiting in the center drawer for a use it no longer required.

The man was still there at ring forty-seven. She could hear him breathing.

"Sir," she said, into the connection, but into nothing, the word meant for one of them or neither, and outside the basement window, which she had not known was there, a woman in a green jacket bent to speak through the grate, her mouth moving with instructions Elena couldn't hear, her hands empty of anything that might be offered, waiting only, as the storage closet rang and rang, for whatever walked down the stairs to need hitting on the left side, not the front, the M&Ms released into the tray below with a sound like punctuation.
