Elias Graves was up to his elbows in the gears of the clockwork mechanism when the knocking started. It was a slow, deliberate thudding, dull against the heavy iron door at the base of the tower, directly below the massive pedestal that held the rotating lens. He withdrew his hands, wiping the heavy machinery grease onto a rag that used to be a bedsheet.

He walked down the spiral stairs. His joints popped in time with the descent.

When he pulled the lever to unlatch the door, the wind pushed it inward, and with it came a woman holding a dead gull by its feet. It was Marian Vance. She had hired out of Portland three months ago to paint the cottage and ended up staying, mostly in the cottage’s spare room, sometimes reading mail over his shoulder while he slept.

"I found this down by the jetty," she said. She held the bird out. Its neck hung limp, swinging in a slow arc above the threshold. "It's a fulmar."

Elias looked at the bird, then at her hands. They were stained with white lead paint. "The oil spill up north?"

"It isn't oiled," Marian said. "Look at the feathers. They're clean."

"You shouldn't bring it in here," Elias said. He did not step aside to let her pass. "Mites."

She lowered the bird, resting the tips of its tail feathers against her rubber boot. "I wanted you to see it."

"A dead bird is a dead bird, Marian."

Behind her, the gray water of the cove chopped against the rocks, throwing spray over the breakwater. The light above them was a steady, silent weight. It was two hours until sunset.

"I was thinking about the boat," Marian said. She shifted her weight from one foot to the other. "The Ellen Louise came by this morning. They were running without their running lights. Three miles out."

"I'll log it."

"I already logged it," she said. "In your book. On the desk."

Elias tightened his grip on the rag in his hand. He had been the keeper of the North Point Lighthouse for twenty-two years. He had logged every ship, every storm, every wreck that had scraped the teeth of the outer reef. The logbook was leather-bound, its spine cracking, its pages smelling of salt and lamp oil.

The Ellen Louise was a lobster rig out of Stonington. Elias knew the captain, a quiet man named Alva who drank peppermint schnapps and kept his pots far from the shoals. Alva never ran dark.

"Why did you write it?" Elias asked.

"Because you were asleep," Marian said. She dropped the bird into the grass just outside the door. She wiped her hands on her trousers. "And because I thought if I wrote it down, it would make it real."

"What are you talking about?"

"The boat was dragging," she said. "It wasn't just sailing. It was heavy in the water. Like it was towing something at the stern."

Elias looked past her, out to the horizon where the water met the sky in a sharp, colorless line. He thought of the logbook. He thought of Alva's face, the way his jaw was set, the way he never looked a man in the eye when he was lying.

"I have to trim the wick," Elias said. "And start the clockwork."

"I'll cook," Marian said. She stepped closer, her shoulder brushing his as she squeezed past him into the narrow entryway of the tower. "I bought flour. Real flour, not the stuff in the tin."

She moved up the stairs. Her footsteps echoed, a rhythmic padding that faded into the hum of the wind.

Elias stood in the doorway a moment longer, looking at the spot on the water where the Ellen Louise would have passed. He walked back up the steps. He bypassed the logbook on the desk, bypassed the kitchen where Marian was already lighting the stove. He climbed until he reached the lamp room, the glass walls showing him the full circle of the darkening sea.

He reached up and turned the brass valve. The kerosene hissed, and then there was light—a bright, blinding flare that caught the prisms and shattered the dusk into a thousand geometric pieces.

He looked through the brass sighting tube of the telescope mounted to the railing. He scanned the quadrant where the boat had been. The sea was empty. The swell was rising, a slow, rolling pulse coming from the deep water.

He closed the brass shield over the telescope lens.

He went down to the kitchen. Marian was kneading dough on the pine table. She didn't look up, but she was humming, a low, tuneless sound that vibrated in her chest.

"Did you lock the door?" she asked.

"It's locked."

"Good."

She punched the dough down, the heels of her white hands sinking into the pale mass. Elias sat in the chair by the window. He watched the dark outside compress against the glass. He didn't speak about the boat again. He didn't ask why she had logged it. He just listened to the hiss of the lamp-burner upstairs, the steady, mechanical rotation of the lens, and the sound of the sea collapsing against the rocks below.
