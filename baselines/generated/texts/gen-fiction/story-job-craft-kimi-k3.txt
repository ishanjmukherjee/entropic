The badge caught on the lanyard again. Lena Morales pulled it free and held the door for a man who didn't look up from his phone, then stood at the elevator bank watching the floors light up in no pattern she could use.

"You're pressing it wrong," said the woman beside her. "You have to hold it."

The woman had a paper cup with a lid that didn't fit right, coffee darkening the seam. She held the down button with her thumb, the nail bitten to nothing. "First day?"

"Is it that obvious?"

"You smiled at the lobby."

The elevator came. They rode in silence to eleven, where the woman got out and turned left without a goodbye. Lena turned right because the offer letter said suite 1140 and the arrows pointed that way.

Her new manager was named Dale Bookman. He met her at the reception desk carrying a cardboard box. "You'll have to excuse the—" He set the box on a chair. It held a ficus, a mug wrapped in a sock, three hard drives. "We're moving you to the corner office. Temporarily. Until we figure out the situation with Karen."

"Karen?"

"You'll meet Karen." He said it the way you'd say a dental appointment.

The corner office had two windows and a whiteboard someone had tried to erase and given up on—half a flowchart, the words "Q3 alignment" visible under the ghost of something else. There was a desk, a chair, a second chair facing the desk with a coat draped over it. A woman's coat, camel-colored, belt trailing.

"That's not—" Dale picked up the coat, folded it over his arm, put it down again. "Just leave that."

He showed her the login. He showed her the shared drive, which was organized in folders named FINAL, FINAL_2, and USE_THIS_ONE. He told her lunch was usually twelve-thirty but flexible. He told her the bathroom key was on a lanyard by the kitchen because of an incident he didn't describe. At no point did he say what her job was.

"You'll sync with Karen," he said, from the doorway. "She'll give you the download. She's been—she's great. You'll love her."

The office filled up slowly. A man named Pete nodded at her from the kitchen while the microwave ran. A woman named Ruth asked if she was the new Karen and then said "sorry, sorry, the new—you know what I mean" and left before coffee finished brewing. Someone's radio played low behind a closed door, the kind of station that apologizes between songs.

At eleven-fifteen, an email arrived from an address that was just numbers. Subject: Welcome. Body: Per my last. There was nothing previous.

At twelve-forty, the coat was gone from the chair.

Lena ate her sandwich in the kitchen. Pete came in, opened the fridge, closed it. "You're sitting in her light," he said, not looking at her, and took his yogurt back to his desk.

She found the bathroom key on its lanyard, a green plastic coil stretched pale from use. The hallway was longer than it looked from inside. Coming back, she passed the corner office and heard Dale's voice through the door, low, and then a woman's, lower. She kept walking. Her desk—the other desk, the one outside the corner office, which she'd understood at some point was actually hers—had a sticky note on the monitor now: Karen wants to see you.

She stood outside the corner office and knocked.

"Yeah."

The woman from the elevator sat behind the desk, the bitten thumb resting on the space bar, the bad lid gone from her cup so the coffee steamed freely. The coat was on the back of her chair. On the whiteboard, the half-flowchart had been erased, all of it, even the ghost.

"You're Karen't," Lena said, and heard it wrong the moment it left her.

"I'm Karen." She didn't smile. "You're in my seat. But that's not your fault. Sit down."

Lena took the second chair, the one that had held the coat.

"So." Karen turned the monitor slightly, not toward her, just away from herself. "Here's where we are. Dale's told you about the transition?"

"He said I'd sync with you."

"Sync. Right." Karen looked at the whiteboard like something was still written on it. "Here's what I need you to do first. Make a list of everything you touch this week. Everyone who emails you, every folder you open. Names, times."

"Is that standard onboarding?"

Karen picked up a pen, clicked it, put it down. "You're going to be great here," she said, in the voice of the dental appointment. "You have a good face."

From the outer office came the microwave's hum, then its beep, then Pete saying something to Ruth that ended in a laugh too quick to mean anything.

The afternoon went where afternoons go. Lena opened folders. USE_THIS_ONE contained seventy copies of the same spreadsheet. At four-thirty she wrote her list on the back of the offer letter printout: Dale (badge, login, coat), Pete (microwave, light), Ruth (sorry), Karen. She stopped. She added: Karen (bad lid, bitten thumb, whiteboard clean). She did not know why she wrote it that way, in evidence.

At five, Karen came out with her coat on. "Leave the list on my desk."

"Will I see you tomorrow?"

Karen stood in the doorway doing something with the belt, looping it through, not threading it right. "You should keep that bathroom key," she said. "Don't give it back to anyone. Even if they ask nice." She looked at Lena then, full on, for the first time since the elevator. "Especially then."

She left. The radio behind the closed door went off between one song and the next, and the floor went quiet in a way that had weight to it, the hum of a fridge in an empty house.

Lena sat at the desk that was hers, in front of the office that wasn't, and read the email again. Per my last. She opened a reply window and typed Happy to help and deleted it and typed Could you clarify and deleted that. Through the window the light over the parking lot had changed, and cars were backing out one by one, brake lights, turn signals, everyone negotiating the exit in turn.

On the whiteboard tray sat the green coil of the bathroom key, though she could not remember putting it there.
