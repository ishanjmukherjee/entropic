The first time the boy signaled, Tobias Ferrow mistook it for a fishing boat in trouble.

It was a November night, wind coming hard off the Atlantic, and he had been up in the lantern room wiping salt from the inside of the glass when he saw it: a small, uneven light across the bay, near the cluster of houses at Carrick Point. Three short. Three long. Three short. Then nothing. Then again.

He had the radio in his hand before he understood that the light wasn't moving. It was coming from a window. Someone had learned the one piece of Morse that everyone learns, and was using it the way children do, because it was the only thing they knew how to say.

Tobias watched for twenty minutes. The signal came and went, patient, insistent. Finally he took the old signal lamp from its hook, the brass one nobody had used since the war, and pointed it across the water. He sent slowly, the way he would to a nervous trawlerman: R U OK.

A long pause. Then, jerky and uncertain: Y E S.

Then: H I.

He laughed out loud, alone in the tower, the great lens turning beside him and throwing its beam out over the black sea. HI, he sent back.

The boy's name was Danny. Tobias learned this the next night, and the next, letter by laborious letter. Danny was nine. Danny had a book from the library about codes. Danny could see the lighthouse from his bedroom and had wondered for a long time whether anyone lived in it.

I DO, Tobias sent. SINCE 1971.

ALONE?

He considered that one. WITH THE LIGHT.

They kept it up through the winter. Danny got faster. Tobias got gentler with his spacing so the boy could keep up, then less gentle as he didn't need to. They spoke about weather, mostly, because weather was the thing Tobias knew best, and Danny wanted to know everything: why the fog came in on warm days, how far the beam could reach, whether Tobias had ever seen a shipwreck.

ONCE, Tobias sent. 1979. ALL SAVED.

BECAUSE OF U?

BECAUSE OF THE LIGHT.

U R THE LIGHT, Danny sent, and Tobias had to sit down on the iron stairs for a while before he answered.

In spring, Danny's signals came later, then less often. Tobias understood. There were bicycles in the world, and friends, and boys did not stay nine. He kept the brass lamp on its hook and checked the window at Carrick Point every night anyway, the way he checked the barometer, the way he checked the oil.

Some nights there would be a light. HI. STILL THERE?

ALWAYS.

Danny was twelve when he wrote that his father had left. Fourteen when he signaled, misspelling it badly, that he had been in a fight. Sixteen when the messages stopped altogether, and Tobias told himself this was the proper order of things.

The letter from the Lighthouse Board came in the autumn of 1996. Automation. A sensor would decide when it was dark. A machine would turn the lens. Tobias Ferrow, after twenty-five years, would be thanked for his service and given a small pension and a flat in town.

He did not argue. He had seen it coming for a decade, every station along the coast going dark of people, one by one. On his last night he climbed the stairs at dusk the way he always had, wound nothing because there was nothing left to wind, and stood in the lantern room watching the light come on by itself.

He looked across the bay out of habit. The window at Carrick Point was dark. Of course it was.

He was halfway down the stairs when the color of the night changed.

He went back up. There, across the water, not from the window but from the shore itself, from the end of the stone pier: a light. Strong and steady, not a child's torch. And the hand working it was quick and sure.

HI. STILL THERE?

Tobias took the brass lamp from its hook for the last time. His hands weren't as steady as they had been. LAST NIGHT, he sent. THEY AUTOMATED ME.

A long pause.

I KNOW. READ IT IN THE PAPER. CAME HOME FOR IT.

Then: I'M AT THE NAVAL COLLEGE NOW. NAVIGATION.

Then, slower, every letter given its full weight: I WANTED TO BE THE LIGHT TOO.

Tobias stood in the lantern room with the great lens turning beside him, no longer needing him, and the beam swept out over the water and passed across the small figure on the pier, lit him for an instant, and moved on.

U ARE, Tobias sent. SEE U ON SHORE.

He went down the stairs, and for the first time in twenty-five years he left the door open behind him.
