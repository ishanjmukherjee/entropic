The house smelled like her—lavender and old paper—and Martha realized it would be the last time she'd ever smell it.

"Where do we even start?" her brother Dan asked, standing in the doorway of the kitchen with his hands in his pockets. He'd driven six hours from Chicago and arrived with takeout coffee and an apology for being late, as if lateness mattered anymore.

"The attic, I guess. Save the hardest for last." Martha had been here a week already, sleeping in her childhood bed, avoiding the boxes in every room the way you avoid eye contact with someone who knows your secrets.

They climbed the narrow stairs single file. The attic light was a single bare bulb, and Dan had to duck under the sloped ceiling. Forty years of their mother's life sat in cardboard and steamer trunks, in hatboxes and grocery bags taped shut with labels in her careful cursive: Christmas, misc. Kids' school stuff, KEEP.

"Keep," Dan read aloud. "Keep for what? We're the end of the line, Mart."

Dan always cut to the wound. Martha hated it and needed it.

They started with the school boxes. Report cards, every one kept. Martha's third-grade art project, a construction paper turkey with a wattle made of a traced hand. Dan's spelling bee certificate, third place, 1979. Their mother had underlined his name in red pen, the way she underlined everything, as if documents might escape if not pinned down.

"She saved my detention slips," Dan said, unfolding a yellowed slip. "Why would she save these?"

"Because they had your name on them."

He went quiet. Martha knew he was doing the thing he did—jaw working, eyes fixed on nothing—her brother's version of grief, which looked like anger preparing for an argument it couldn't have.

The afternoon moved in shifts, like weather. One box would undo them—the one with their father's reading glasses, still folded, still waiting on a side table thirty years after the funeral—and they'd retreat to separate corners, Dan sorting nails and screws into jars, Martha going through linen, until they could be in the same airspace again.

Around four, Martha found the cigar box.

It was tucked behind the Christmas decorations, and she nearly missed it, but the gold lettering caught the light. Inside: not cigars, but photographs. Dozens of them. Black and white, mostly. A young woman on a beach, laughing, one hand thrown up against the sun. The same woman on a motorcycle, hair wild, mid-laugh. The woman dancing in a kitchen with a man who was not their father.

"Dan. Come look at this."

He crouched beside her. "That's Mom."

It was and it wasn't. Their mother had been a careful woman, a list-maker, a pot-scrubber, a woman who took the bus and clipped coupons and sang in the church choir. This woman on the motorcycle looked like she owned the wind.

"Who's the guy?"

Martha turned the photo over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting that was almost their mother's but looser, younger: July 1964, Rehoboth. J. said yes!!!

"J. said yes," Dan repeated. "Said yes to what?"

"Before Dad. This is before Dad." Martha sorted through the rest. The man—J.—appeared again and again. Then the photos stopped, abruptly, like a door closing.

At the bottom of the box, a ring. Not their father's ring, not her mother's wedding band, which Martha already wore on a chain around her neck. This ring was older, its band thin, a small pearl set like a period at the end of a sentence.

They sat on the attic floor among the ruins and held the evidence of a woman they'd never met.

"She had a whole life," Dan said. "Before us. A whole life we knew nothing about."

"Isn't that what mothers do? Fold themselves in half so they can fit inside the house?"

Dan laughed, but it came out wrong, and then Martha was crying, and then, for the first time since the funeral, since the diagnosis, since the phone call that started everything, Dan was crying too. They sat shoulder to shoulder against a trunk marked Winter clothes and wept for their mother and for the girl on the motorcycle and for themselves, children at fifty-two and fifty-five, orphaned at last.

When the bulb flickered, they wiped their faces and got back to work.

They kept the cigar box. They kept the turkey and the detention slips and the reading glasses. They kept the pearl ring, and Martha slipped it onto her thumb, where it fit perfectly, as if it had been waiting.

By nightfall the attic was sorted into piles—donate, discard, keep—and the keep pile was bigger than either of them expected, because it turned out that clearing out a house has nothing to do with clearing anything out.

Downstairs, Martha made tea in the dented kettle. Dan called out from the living room, "Found her record collection. Anything good?"

Martha looked at the boxes around her, at the purple late-day light on the wallpaper her mother had hung in 1982, and said, "Probably. She was full of surprises."

She heard the needle drop, heard a scratch, and then guitar—a sound too wild for the careful woman she'd known, and exactly right for the one she was only beginning, too late, to meet.
