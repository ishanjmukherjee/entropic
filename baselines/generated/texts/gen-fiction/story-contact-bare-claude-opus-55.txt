The signal was found by accident, which is how most important things are found.

Mara Okafor was twenty-six and three years into a doctorate she had begun to suspect was pointless. Her task was to catalogue the slow wobble of distant pulsars, the tiny irregularities that might betray the passage of gravitational waves. It was patient, invisible work. Most nights she watched the data scroll and thought about quitting.

The anomaly was in a source designated PSR J1903+0327b, a dim and unremarkable star nobody had looked at closely in a decade. Its pulses arrived on schedule, but the gaps between them were stretching and shrinking in a way no physical process could explain. The variation was tiny, a few billionths of a second, and it followed a pattern.

It took her eleven months to be sure. One. Two. Three. Five. Seven.

The primes.

There was a press conference. There were congressional hearings and a cover of Time and a brief, strange period when strangers recognized her in grocery stores. Then the world learned what Mara already knew. The message was slow.

Not slow the way a dial-up modem was slow. Slow the way continents were slow. The sender, whoever they were, changed their modulation once every three hundred and eighty days. One symbol per year, give or take.

"That's not a conversation," a senator said at the third hearing. "That's geology."

The funding lasted six years. By then the primes had given way to something more complex, a sequence that might have been a grid, though nobody could say what it was meant to fill. The big observatories moved on to faster, more fashionable mysteries. Mara took a teaching job at a small college in Vermont and kept a dish of her own, a secondhand twelve-meter antenna she rebuilt with grant money, her students, and once with the proceeds of selling her car.

Every year, on or around the first of March, she recorded one new symbol.

She married. She divorced. She buried her father and then her mother. Her students grew up and some of them came back, gray at the temples, to help her recalibrate the receiver. The world forgot, remembered on anniversaries, and forgot again. A popular theory held that the signal was a natural phenomenon after all, some exotic magnetic resonance. Mara never argued with it. She just kept writing the symbols down.

In her fortieth year of listening, the grid resolved.

It was a picture, built line by line, one line per year, and it had been coming down toward Earth since before the pyramids. The top rows showed a star: wide, orange, with seven worlds drawn in careful circles around it. The fourth world was marked. Below it was a figure, if you were generous with the word, a shape that branched and rebranched like a river delta or a bare tree in winter. Mara stared at it for a long time before she understood that it was a self-portrait.

Below the figure was a frame. It was the same width as the image above it and outlined in a careful border, and it was empty.

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the cold control shed with a thermos of bad coffee and the picture glowing on her screen, and she thought about what it meant to build a message like this. You would have to know that no one alive at the start would be alive at the end. You would have to teach your children to keep sending it, and they would have to teach theirs. Every year, one more line. You would have to believe, across a hundred generations, that somewhere out there someone was patient enough to listen.

And then you would leave a space and wait.

It wasn't a greeting. It was an invitation: Here is us. Now show us you.

In the morning she called the few people who still returned her calls. Some of them were impressed. A few were moved. All of them, gently, pointed out the arithmetic. A reply of equal complexity, sent at the same rate, would take longer than recorded history. Mara was seventy-one years old.

"So it won't be me," she said. "It was never going to be me."

They spent the spring arguing about what to send. The committee was mostly former students now, and their students in turn. Some wanted mathematics. Some wanted music. One young woman wanted to send nothing but the image of a human hand, open, palm forward, drawn at the scale of a fingerprint so that it would take a thousand years to finish.

In the end they agreed to begin with what the sender had begun with, so that the reply would sound like an echo.

On the first of March, Mara stood in the shed while the transmitter warmed. Outside, snow was falling on the dish. She thought of a creature like a winter tree, on a world circling an orange star, holding its own vigil and listening for a change in the dark.

She pressed the key.

One.

Then she sat down to teach the others how to wait.
