The attic ladder springs under Mara's hand, and for a half-second she is thirteen again, knees flat in knee-worn jeans, swearing through gritted teeth that she will not come down until Saturday. She hears the lock click, the old catch seize in the throat of the house.

"Jesus Christ!" Roman's voice, three flights down. "You better not have locked yourself in."

The catch moves before she has to call him. She hasn't lost the knack, she just hasn't had to use it in years.

She steps onto plywood and It happens. Her shoulders know it before the rest of her. The air is packed with heat and something inert, mothballs maybe, cardboard dried out years ago. Her mother's things, organized in laundry baskets and milk crates, stacked at eerie cable to a wholeness that tells her their mother was sure something would be salvaged eventually.

Roman mounts the ladder half-way, flashlight on his phone swinging. "Looks like she's finally moving out."

"I brought the truck."

"Oh, good." He pulls himself into the square of light, breathing swift through his mouth. He is bald now, though two years ago she thought wiry. He wore it badly, as a man might wear some unexpected gift from a much younger relative, not wholly unwanted but impossible to place. He reaches for the nearest crate.

"Don't—" Mara starts.

He lifts the lid. Inside, newspaper wrapped cups the shape of a baby's head. Painted eggs, Ukrainian or imitation-Ukrainian, boxed in tissue. Photographs in sandwich bags. One of her, six, hair in a ragged bowl that might or might not have been her own idea. Roman doesn't pick it up.

"We said pictures first," she says.

"That was Tuesday." He pries the flaps of another box. "This is Sunday. On Sunday, I touch things."

She crosses to the dormer window. The glass is offset like a pupil. Their mother's room had brass knobs, pale blue walls. She thinks of the last time she'd been out here, before the cancer even, when she'd walked past the shut door and caught herself saying through it, I'm sorry, with no idea why.

Roman pulls three mugs loose and looks into each before setting them down, like he doesn't understand why anyone would own so many. On the back of one he reads the label: Occupational Therapy. "Should have kept one for you."

"I hated her job. She was only happy there. She hated being home."

"Then why did she stay home for twenty years?" He seals the box and peels off masking tape. The house's freezer hum arrives in the attic every time a truck passes, a drafted rumor of a refrigerator somewhere.

Mara opens the next basket. Doctors' reports folded like letters. Drawings. Half-cut winter house plans their father never built, before he was their father, before he was gone. The skeleton worry of a person. "You remember summer, when we took the Mercury to Virginia?"

"The radiator."

"Yeah. Motel overflowed."

"I got the refund." He moves to a bundle of spooled garden hose coiled near the chimney wall, rust-holed and swaddled. "Own damn fault."

"We could drive it out again if we took everything we could carry."

"Which car would you use?"

"The Rav-4." She checks her phone. The listing had said the yard work at Syracuse wasn't terribly involved. Roman from Tripoli Romano has always been privy to possibilities even she would have dismissed. "Or whatever you're still paying for."

Guitar strings hang across the gable slope, tied there in some earlier attempt at music. He reaches up and breaks one with his thumb. The sting is sharp. "I'm not selling."

"Didn't say sell."

"You were going to." He rests his hand on the nearest basket before she can lift it. "Mom wanted us to keep the house."

"She said that once. When I had kids. We were supposed to bring them here for Christmas."

"You didn't have to bring them here. You just couldn't sell it if you lived here first."

"We can't live here, Roman."

"Twenty years is a long time to be out." He begins counting things with this finger and choses something heavier. "It's a shame we're down today. I could do with a cigarette."

There's no fireplace here but a mouse trap maybe. Dust like shaved pencil lead on the trap's spring. It has never triggered anything.

Across the cores of the house they're small again, short and fast, and the room at the foot of the stairs smells of butter and vinegar. Mara catches herself watching the landing for one balled skirt hem or bare foot and calms immediately. Nobody is coming up. She is almost glad of the heat. Roman checks the clock on the wall—there shouldn't be a clock up here, and when he catches his own eye, he looks away first.

"Listen," Mara says. From beneath the eaves where she kneels, she can see him pivot to the boxes before him, choosing, weighing. His hands are their mother's. "We should get the fridge emptied before—"

"You didn't call me on the way up." He tears through a plastic bag's knotted loop without taking anything out. "You just— showed?"

"I stopped to get coffee. I thought you'd be there."

"Fucking figures." He stops, a box of their mother's unsent Christmas cards balanced on his boot. "I brought you a key one year. I thought—never mind." He sets that box closer to the ladder, where the light is worse and its contents more like faces poking through fog.

He doesn't say what had stopped him that year, but the house holds memory of a phone call longer than anyone made anymore. Mara can't access it from where she crouches. She closes the basket filled with their histories along the edges of their grandfather's handwriting. Everything in here seemed so amendable, save a wanting. She opens the one beneath it. No paperwork. Just pillowcases folded up like the start of a sheet-walled tent. Smells of soap and grass.

"Take those," she tells him.

He is looking toward the eaves where their parents must have kept something once. Shadows below the roof boards interpose like the bars of a stairwell window. "I'm taking what we missed."

"Fine. Then take the hose. There might be a market for it."

"There's always a market," he says, and somewhere down the flue, the sound of glass or something smaller breaking, purposeful, hidden in the slot between wall boards. She doesn't ask about it. Let the house give itself up. Something has been kept in the structure for so long, it stands to reason not all of it would come out alive.

Mara opens an envelope and finds her name on the outside in ink, dated December 1995. Inside: a thumbnail scrap of blue construction paper and a key.

Roman glances at her then and neither of them moves. His own palm hangs open near the basket, unclosed. What had been tucked away in a pocket, not a letter. Not the answer she comes home for like an eaten meal. The two of them watched their mother fold into narrowness every Saturday, watched her ribs seam away from shirring—why couldn't their father have stayed until the end of one lecture? There was always a reason, always four walls to blame for one wanting a little bit more air.

Roman says, "If you won't come down by dinner tomorrow, at least make the call."

"I told you I haven't thought that far ahead."
