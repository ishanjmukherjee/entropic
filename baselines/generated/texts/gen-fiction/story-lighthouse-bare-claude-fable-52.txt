Every evening at dusk, Marta Oyelaran climbed the one hundred and forty-two steps of the Kestrel Point light, and every evening she counted them, though the number had not changed in thirty-one years.

The counting was a habit left over from her first winter, when she had been young and frightened of the dark stairwell and the way the wind made the tower hum like a struck glass. Now the counting was simply company. Forty: the landing where the paint blistered. Eighty-eight: the narrow window that faced the mainland, where on clear nights she could see the village lights scattered like spilled salt. One hundred and forty-two: the lamp room, and the great lens waiting in its brass cradle.

The light had been automated eleven years ago. A man from the coastal authority had come out on the supply boat with a box of electronics and an apologetic expression, and in an afternoon he had made her unnecessary. The lamp would turn itself on. The lamp would report its own faults by radio. She was welcome to stay on as caretaker, he said, at reduced pay, until such time as the cottage was needed for something else.

It had not been needed for anything else. So she stayed, and she climbed the stairs, and she polished a lens that no longer required her.

On the last night of October, the fog came in early. Marta watched it from the kitchen window as she washed her supper plate: a gray wall moving over the water, swallowing the skerries one by one. By the time she had dried her hands, the sea was gone entirely, and the world ended at the garden fence.

She climbed. Forty. Eighty-eight. At one hundred and ten she noticed that the stairwell was darker than it should have been, and by one hundred and forty-two she knew why.

The lamp was out.

She stood for a moment with her hand on the cold rail. The lens sat dark, an enormous faceted eye with nothing behind it. Below, the automated panel showed a row of small green lights, each one insisting cheerfully that all was well.

She tried the reset. She tried the breaker in the cupboard. She tried the radio, which gave her a soft hiss and nothing more, and she remembered the gale two weeks back and the antenna she had meant to check.

Then she heard it, low and far off: a ship's horn.

Marta had been unnecessary for eleven years, and she found that she had been waiting the whole time to stop.

The old oil lamp was in the storeroom under a canvas sheet, where she had put it herself in the days when the electric light was new and no one trusted it. The reservoir was dry, but there was paraffin in the shed for the cottage heater. She made the trip twice, a can in each hand, one hundred and forty-two steps each time. Her knees had opinions about that. She did not consult them.

It took most of an hour to seat the lamp, trim the wick, and work out how to disengage the motor so that the lens would turn freely. When she touched the match to the wick, the flame caught low and blue, then rose yellow, and the lens took that small fire and threw it out into the fog as a long white blade.

But the lens would not turn on its own. The clockwork that had once driven it had been removed years ago to make room for the motor.

So Marta put her shoulder to the brass frame and walked.

Around and around the lamp room she went, slowly, pushing the great lens ahead of her. One flash every twelve seconds was the Kestrel Point signature. She counted her steps to keep the pace, as she had always counted. The horn sounded again, nearer. Then again, farther north, and she understood that it had turned.

She walked until the windows went from black to gray. She walked until the fog thinned to rags and the skerries came back, wet and gleaming, with the sea breaking white across their teeth. Beyond them, safely in the channel, a rust-streaked freighter was making its slow way toward the harbor.

It sounded its horn once more, three short blasts. Marta, who knew every signal in the book, did not recognize that one. She decided it meant thank you.

She put out the lamp and sat down on the floor with her back against the wall. Her legs trembled. Her hands smelled of paraffin and brass.

In a few days the supply boat would bring a technician, and he would replace some small failed part, and the green lights would be telling the truth again. He would probably not ask about the old lamp sitting in the cradle, and she would probably not explain.

But that evening at dusk, she climbed the stairs. She counted every one.
