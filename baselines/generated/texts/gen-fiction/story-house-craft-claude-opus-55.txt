Gary was holding the Electrolux hose the way you'd hold a snake you had decided not to kill.

"That goes," Linda said.

"It works."

"Nobody has used it since Carter."

He coiled it around his forearm, elbow to palm, the way their father had coiled extension cords, and set it by the back door with the other things she had said would go: the bread machine, a shoebox of cassettes, the green kitchen stool with one rubber foot missing. She had started thinking of the pile as Maybe. Gary did not call it anything. He just kept carrying things to it.

The realtor was coming Thursday. Linda had written this on the back of an electric bill and stuck it to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like Ohio, and twice now she had come into the kitchen and found the bill turned around so the Duke Energy side faced out.

"There's a Goodwill on Route 4," she said.

"There's one on Main."

"Is that one still open?"

"I don't know," Gary said. "I don't go to Goodwill."

She did the bathroom alone. Their mother's things were easy, almost nothing: a jar of Pond's with a fingerprint dried into the surface, two lipsticks worn down to slants, a hairbrush she held for a while and then put in the bag. On the edge of the sink, in a juice glass, were two toothbrushes. One was pink and dry. The other was blue, and the bristles were splayed, and when she touched it, it was damp.

She stood with her thumb on it. Downstairs the screen door went, and went again.

In the kitchen Gary had the freezer open. It was the old upright in the utility room, the one that sounded like a truck idling, and he was standing in its breath with his hands in his pockets.

"I was going to do that," she said.

"I know."

She came and stood next to him. The shelves were full of margarine tubs and Cool Whip containers and quart Ziplocs laid flat and stacked like files, each with a strip of masking tape and their mother's printing in black marker. Chicken & rice, Mon. Ham & beans, Tue. Goulash, Wed. The dates ran down the left side of each strip. October. November. On the bottom shelf, December, and two marked January, the marker gone faint because she must have been pressing less by then.

"Jesus," Linda said.

"She did one a day. After the second round. She'd sit on the stool." He nodded at the Maybe pile. "She couldn't stand that long."

Linda counted the top shelf without meaning to. Some of the October spaces were empty, the frost holding the rectangle where a bag had been.

"You've been eating these."

"They're good."

"Gary, some of these are from August."

"It's a freezer."

She reached in and took a tub. The cold went straight to the bone of her finger. Stew, Fri, and a date in September, a week before Linda had flown in for the first time, the week their mother had said on the phone that it wasn't worth the ticket, that it was mostly sleeping, that Gary was here.

"We have to clear this out before Thursday," she said.

"Why?"

"Because people open freezers, Gary. They open everything. They'll open the medicine cabinet."

He looked at her then, and she put the stew back in its rectangle of frost.

They worked the rest of the afternoon in different rooms. She did the linen closet, the sheets gone yellow along the folds, a Christmas tablecloth with a burn mark shaped like a pear. He did the garage, and she heard him dragging something and stopping, dragging and stopping. At five he came in and ate a bowl of something standing at the counter, and didn't offer, and she didn't ask.

When he went out for cigarettes, she went back to the freezer.

She was not looking for anything. She took the bags out shelf by shelf and set them on the dryer, meaning to count them, meaning to have a number to say. Behind the January ones, flat against the back wall, was one more bag, smaller, the tape on it longer than the others and folded over at the end where the writing had run out of room.

Linda — chili, the way you used to like it, if you come.

No date.

She read it twice. The frost on it had a thumbprint in it, larger than their mother's.

When Gary came back she was putting the last of the bags back where they had been, matching each one to its shape in the ice. He stood in the doorway with the carton under his arm.

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing. I counted."

"How many?"

"Enough," she said. "Through about March, if you skip a day."

He set the cigarettes on the dryer. "There's one in the back."

"I saw it."

"I didn't know if you'd want it on the plane."

"It wouldn't keep," Linda said. She shut the freezer, and it shuddered and caught and went on running, and after a minute he went and turned the electric bill around.
