"That's forty-one," Renee said, and dropped another margarine tub into the contractor bag. "Forty-one, Curtis. With lids."

"She used them."

"For what?"

He was on the stepladder with his head in the cabinet over the refrigerator, handing things down without looking: a fondue pot, a bag of birthday candles, the manual for a microwave two microwaves ago. "Soup," he said. "She sent people home with soup."

Renee wrote FOOD BANK on a box and ran the tape gun across it. She had been in the house since Tuesday and had done the kitchen drawers, the linen closet, and the bathroom, where she had filled a grocery sack with prescription bottles and read each label before it went in. She kept a list on a legal pad. Next to dehumidifier she had written $40? and next to china, look up pattern.

"The woman from Howard Hanna is coming Saturday at ten," she said. "She wants the hall clear so she can photograph."

"Saturday."

"I fly out Sunday. I told you that."

"You told me." He came down the ladder holding a Pyrex dish with a strip of masking tape on the bottom. Their mother's capitals: RENEE. He turned it so she could see, then set it on the counter between them.

"I can't take that on a plane."

"I'll mail it."

"It'd cost more to mail than it's worth." She picked it up anyway and looked at the tape. She put it in the box with the china, then took it out and stood it on the counter again. "What's in the back bedroom?"

"Boxes. Her sewing stuff. I'll do it."

"I've got two days, I can just—"

"I'll do it, Renee."

In the basement the dryer buzzed. Renee looked at the linoleum. Curtis folded the stepladder, leaned it against the refrigerator, and went down the stairs, pulling the door shut behind him.

She was wrapping saucers in the Plain Dealer when a key turned in the side door. A boy of about eleven came in, the key on a shoelace around his neck. He stepped out of his sneakers and set them on the rubber mat next to a pair of work boots, toes to the wall.

"Is Curtis here?"

"He's downstairs. Who are you?"

"Devin." He pointed with his thumb. "From across." He looked at the boxes, the bag, the open cabinets. "Are you the one in Tucson?"

"I'm Renee."

"She said you have a pool."

"It's the complex's pool. It's not mine."

"She said she was going out there. She said when it got cold you were going to have her out there and she'd be in the pool on Christmas." He pulled the key off over his head and put it in his pocket. "She showed me on the map."

Renee folded newspaper around a saucer and then around the same saucer again. "Does your mother know where you are?"

"She's at work till six. I come Thursdays."

"Do you want something to eat?"

"Curtis makes grilled cheese. Thursdays he's home. The other days he doesn't get home till after, so I just go to my house." He went through to the den. The television came on, a laugh track, then something with engines.

She put the saucer down. She walked up the hall with the legal pad against her chest, past the bathroom, past her mother's room with the stripped mattress, to the door at the end. There was a line of orange light under it. Something behind it ticked the way a space heater ticks. She stood there long enough for the engines in the den to give way to a commercial for a personal injury lawyer, and then she walked back.

Curtis was in the kitchen. He had seen the sneakers. He had his truck key out and was slitting the tape on the food bank box.

"That's counted," she said.

"It's one can." He took out a tomato soup and found the opener in the drawer she had already emptied and refilled with things to donate. He got the skillet down off the pile by the door. The butter was his; she understood now that the butter and the bread and the half gallon of two-percent she had been stepping around since Tuesday had not been their mother's.

"Does he know it's being sold?"

"He's eleven."

"That's not what I asked you."

"Saturday at ten," Curtis said. "I heard you." He laid the bread in the pan.

From the den Devin called to ask if his mom could have some of the soup again, if there was extra.

Curtis turned the burner down. He went over to the contractor bag, pulled the mouth of it open, and put his arm in to the elbow.

"The lids are at the bottom," Renee said.
