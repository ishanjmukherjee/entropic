"Not the shackle," Marlene said. "Go through the body. Shackle's hardened, you'll be here till lunch."

Curtis moved the grinder down half an inch. The lock was a brass Master, the kind sold in two-packs, and it took about forty seconds and dropped to the concrete in pieces. Marlene nudged them against the wall with her shoe.

"Ninety-one days," she said. "We send three letters. Third one's certified. Nobody signs." She held out the clipboard. "Print, don't sign. Corporate can't read signatures."

He printed CURTIS BOYD in capitals, the S tipped backward like something leaning out of the wind.

"Pruitt, Lorraine," Marlene read. "Ten by ten. You want to roll it up or should I?"

He had his shoulder under the door before it caught at knee height, and it went up the rest of the way without a sound from him. Inside there was a mattress on its side in a plastic bag, a recliner with a bath towel over the headrest, a floor lamp, and a sewing machine cabinet the green of a hospital corridor. Liquor store boxes were stacked four high along the left wall. The one on top said XMAS – FRAGILE in marker.

"We don't go in," Marlene said. "Photos from the threshold. What you can see from here is what the bidders see."

"What if there's something in the back?"

"Then somebody gets lucky. Guy last spring got an outboard motor." She gave him the tablet. "Get the dresser. People like a dresser."

"Drawers are full."

"You can tell that?"

"The way it's sitting."

She looked at the dresser a while. "Huh. The last one couldn't tell a couch from a loveseat. He lasted nine days. The one before that, I think his girlfriend didn't like the hours." Her phone buzzed against the clipboard. She read it, turned it face down. "You got family around here?"

"A sister."

"That's nice. Close?"

"Twenty minutes."

"I meant—" She waved it off. "Do you do church? Some people do church."

"No."

"Because Sundays are dead. It's one person, you'd just sit. You could bring a book. I've been doing every Sunday since March."

He took eleven photos. In the ninth the flash caught a coffee can on the shelf above the boxes, a Folgers can with a strip of masking tape across the lid, and he lowered the tablet and then raised it and took the picture again from a little to the right, where the lamp shade was in the way.

"That one's blurry," Marlene said, looking over his arm. "Doesn't matter. Nobody bids on coffee."

She hung the company lock on the hasp herself. It was red, round, with no shackle to speak of. The key had a red plastic tag, and she threaded it onto the ring with the others.

In the office she had him type the listing while she ate crackers out of her coat pocket. Household goods. Recliner, dresser, mattress, sewing machine, misc. boxes.

"Put antique," she said. "For the sewing machine."

"It's a seventy-four."

"How do you know that?"

"They all are, in that color."

"Put vintage, then." She leaned in to check his spelling. "Goes live Friday at nine. She can still pay up till the hammer. Four-twelve, plus forty for the lock you just cut. Cash or money order."

"Do they ever?"

"Once. A woman came in with eleven hundred dollars in a bank envelope, crying, hugged me across the counter. Then her next month bounced." She brushed crumbs off the keyboard. "You get so you don't look at the stuff. First year I looked at all of it."

Her phone went again. This time she took it out to the lot and stood between her car and the dumpster with one hand flat on top of her head. On the monitor, camera six showed the second-floor corridor in gray, the red lock a darker gray. He watched that instead.

She came back in with her purse already on her shoulder. "I have to be somewhere by three-thirty. It's an hour. It isn't Sunday, but they said today." She was looking at the keys, not at him. "Gate code's on the sticky. Six o'clock you flip the sign and pull the till, that's all. Can you do that? You can call me. Don't call me unless it's a fire."

"I can do that."

"You're coming back tomorrow."

"Yes."

She put the ring on the counter between them and kept two fingers on it a moment. Then she was in the car, backing out too fast, braking for nothing.

He sat. The ring had fourteen keys. He turned it until the red tag was on top.

The phone rang at ten past four. He let it go twice.

"Northgate Storage."

"Yeah, I got a letter?" A woman's voice, a television going behind it. "About two-fourteen. Is Marlene there? I usually talk to Marlene."

"She stepped out."

There was a pause on the line. The television laughed.

"Well, who's this?"

He closed his hand over the keys.

"I'm new," he said. "It's my first day."

"Well, congratulations," she said. "Is there anybody there who knows anything?"
