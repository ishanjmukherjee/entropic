The key stuck in the lock the way it always had, and Elena had to jiggle it twice before the deadbolt finally turned. She stood aside to let her brother pass through first, some old habit of deference she couldn't shake.

"Smaller than I remembered," Tom said, his voice hollow in the empty hallway.

Their mother's house had shrunk in their absence, or they had grown—Elena couldn't decide which. She set her bag on the newel post and stared at the staircase, at the thumbnail groove worn into the wall where she'd braced her hand every morning before school for twelve years.

"Where do we even start?" Tom asked.

"Kitchen, probably. She always kept the important papers in that drawer under the phone." Elena moved automatically, her body remembering the path through narrow doorways. The green linoleum tut-tutted under her shoes, the same worn track between stove and sink and refrigerator.

The kitchen smelled like rust and something sweeter underneath, cinnamon maybe, from some last baking that no one had finished. Elena pulled open the junk drawer and the chaos of it made her throat tighten—rubber bands around wadded tinfoil, takeout menus from restaurants that had closed a decade ago, a broken compass Tom had mailed her from a summer camp and she'd somehow lost and their mother had apparently found.

"She saved everything," Tom said, not quite an accusation.

"She lost Dad's ring in the garden once and spent three days sifting through dirt with a colander. You remember?" Elena found a manila envelope marked IMPORTANT in her mother's rounded script. "She couldn't help it. Keeping things was how she loved."

Tom opened the refrigerator, then shut it quickly. "We should've come sooner."

"You were in Portland. I was in Rochester. We talked about it."

"We talked about a lot of things."

Elena didn't answer. She opened the envelope—insurance documents, the deed, a letter from her mother's sister dated 1987 that had nothing to do with anything practical. She set it aside and moved to the cabinets, began lifting out the mismatched drinking glasses, the set of stoneware plates with the blue rim that had survived every dinner of their childhood.

"I'll take these," Tom said suddenly. "The plates. Maya always wanted—" He stopped. "She thought it was funny, the way Mom would pile them too high at Thanksgiving. The gravy situation."

"Your ex-wife wanted Mom's plates."

"Almost-ex," Tom corrected automatically. Then: "Fine. Yes."

Elena wrapped three glasses in newspaper and set them in a box. The silence stretched between them, familiar and uncomfortable, the particular quiet of two people who had shared too much history and handled it poorly.

They worked through the afternoon in parallel, not together. Tom took the living room, sorting through books and photograph albums with the efficiency of someone who had practice leaving places. Elena climbed the stairs to her mother's bedroom and stood in the doorway for a long moment before she could enter.

The bed was made, hospital corners, the quilt pulled tight enough to bounce a quarter. Elena opened the closet and the smell of her mother's perfume, something department-store and powdery, rose out like a ghost. She sat down on the carpeted floor and looked at the organized rows of clothing, sorted by color, by season, by some logic only her mother had understood.

She found the dress her mother had worn to Elena's college graduation, navy blue with a collar that was slightly wrong for the decade. The funeral dress, black wool with a moth hole in the hem she'd never mentioned. The sundress with lemons on it that Elena had always secretly hated.

She was still sitting there when Tom found her, holding the lemon sundress in her lap.

"Hey," he said.

"She wore this to your wedding."

"I know. She got wine on it during the speeches. Dad wouldn't let her go change, he thought it was funny, the way she kept trying to hide it behind her champagne glass."

Elena hadn't been at the wedding, not really. She'd been in the bathroom for most of the reception, arguing on the phone with a boyfriend who would disappear two months later. She'd missed this detail, this small scene of her parents laughing together. She had so many memories of her mother alone, widowed, slowly folding in on herself like a piece of paper being put back in its envelope.

"I should've visited more," Elena said.

"I should've called more."

"We said that at the funeral too. We're establishing precedent."

Tom sat down beside her, knees cracking, too close and not close enough. He picked up the funeral dress, inspected the moth hole. "Remember when she tried to teach us to sew? I made a pillow for Show and Tell and the stuffing came out during my presentation. Looked like a cotton massacre."

"She patched your jeans until you were sixteen. You were humiliated."

"I was. I also never learned to do it myself." He set the dress down carefully, smoothing the sleeve. "Maya did all my buttons. My hems. I don't know how to sew a button, Elena. I'm thirty-four and I don't know how to sew a goddamn button."

Elena reached over and took his hand. He startled, then gripped back, hard enough to hurt.

"We can't keep everything," she said.

"I know."

"But we can keep some things. The plates. The blue-rim ones. And I'll take the glasses, and—" She stopped, looking at the closet, at the accumulated weight of a woman's careful, particular life. "We can figure out the rest."

Tom nodded, not letting go. They sat together on the carpet as the afternoon light moved across the bed, shifted, thinned. Downstairs, a floorboard settled with the particular sigh of old wood cooling. Their mother's house, full and empty, waiting to become something else.
