The badge didn't work. Mara held it against the reader a third time, watched the light blink red, and felt the particular heat of being observed by a lobby full of people who belonged there.

"New?" The security guard didn't look up from his crossword.

"Is it that obvious?"

"They never activate them until noon. Nobody knows why." He buzzed her through. "Fourth floor. Don't take the left elevator."

She took the right elevator, which smelled faintly of oranges, and spent the ride rehearsing her own name as though she might forget it. Mara Okafor. Junior cartographer. She had said those words to her mirror that morning with a toothbrush in her mouth, and they had sounded like someone else's.

The fourth floor of the Hallwell County Survey Office was not what the website had promised. The website had shown glass walls and people pointing at screens. The reality was a long room with carpet the color of weak tea, filing cabinets taller than she was, and a single enormous table covered in paper maps, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, a stapler, and what appeared to be a brick.

"You're the new one." A woman emerged from between two cabinets, sixty or so, with reading glasses pushed into gray hair and a pencil behind each ear. "I'm Delphine. I'd shake your hand but I've got graphite all over me. Did you take the left elevator?"

"No."

"Good. Smart. Come on."

There was no orientation. There was no folder of benefits paperwork, no laptop waiting with a sticky note of passwords. Delphine walked her to a desk beside a window, cleared a stack of survey books off the chair, and said, "That's yours. The drawer sticks. Theo will get you logged in when he's back from the field, which could be an hour or could be Thursday."

"What should I do until then?"

Delphine considered her for a moment, as if the question were more interesting than Mara had meant it to be. Then she went to the big table and came back with a rolled sheet, which she spread across the desk.

It was a map of the county's northeast corner, hand-drawn, dated 1961. The linework was beautiful: every creek feathered with tiny strokes, every hill shaded by someone with patience and a steady wrist.

"We're digitizing the old sheets," Delphine said. "Software does most of it. But the software doesn't know when the old surveyors were wrong, or lying, or drunk. Compare this against the satellite layer when you get access. For now, just look at it. Tell me what bothers you."

She left. Mara looked.

For twenty minutes nothing bothered her at all, and she began composing the email she would send her sister: They gave me a sixty-year-old map and no computer. I think I've made a mistake. She had left a decent job for this one. A boring job, with a working badge and a manager who sent calendar invitations for everything, including, once, a meeting to discuss having too many meetings. She had left because she'd spent four years making maps that told delivery trucks where to turn, and had begun to suspect she'd forgotten why she liked maps in the first place.

Then she saw the road.

It ran along the eastern edge of the sheet, a thin double line labeled Pruett Lane, and it crossed Sallow Creek at a point where the contour lines bunched tight together. She traced them with a fingernail. Forty feet of drop on either bank. Nobody built a road there. Nobody could have, not without a bridge, and there was no bridge symbol, only the road sailing serenely across a ravine as if the surveyor had simply willed it over.

She found Delphine at the table, eating crackers over a plat of the fairgrounds.

"Pruett Lane," Mara said. "It can't cross there."

Delphine's eyebrows went up. She came and looked, and then she laughed, a short delighted bark.

"Eleven years I've had people look at that sheet. You know how many caught it on the first day?" She tapped the ravine. "It's a trap street, sort of. Old Hallwell himself drew it. If another company copied his map, the impossible road would show up in theirs, and he'd know. Except the county commissioners believed it, and in 1964 they budgeted to pave it."

"What happened?"

"A very embarrassing site visit." Delphine brushed crumbs from her sleeve. "Half this job is finding out what's really there. The other half is finding out why somebody said otherwise. Most people only like the first half."

Mara looked back at the little double line, drawn so confidently over nothing.

"I think I like the second half," she said.

"I thought you might." Delphine pulled a pencil from behind her ear and held it out. "Mark it. Then find me the others. There are six more on that sheet."

At noon, somewhere below, her badge quietly began to work. Mara didn't notice. She was on her third.
