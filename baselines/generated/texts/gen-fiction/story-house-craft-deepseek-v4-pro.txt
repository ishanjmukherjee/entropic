The dust ruffle wouldn’t give.

Linda had her shoulder against the box spring, one hand reefing on the polyester. It was sewn to something. She let go and a puff of motes hung in the air between her and the baseboard.

“You want the pickle forks,” her brother called from the kitchen.

“What?”

“The forks. The tiny ones for pickles. You said.”

“I said oyster forks.”

“Same thing.”

She sat back on her heels. A sneeze that had been threatening for half an hour finally broke, and she let it go into the crook of her elbow. Through the wall she heard a drawer slide shut, then another.

“They’re in a box,” she said.

No answer. She ran her thumb along the seam where the ruffle met the mattress ticking and found the knot of thread. Her mother had tacked it down by hand. A whipstitch, tight and even. Linda worked her nail under it.

After a minute she heard footsteps cross the dining room and stop at the bedroom door.

“I’m taking the radio,” Michael said.

He was holding the little transistor from the kitchen windowsill, the one with the flour-dusted dial. Their mother had kept it tuned to the AM farm report every morning for forty-two years, long after any farm figured in her life.

“Okay,” Linda said.

“The batteries are corroded. I’ll have to open it up.”

“Okay.”

He didn’t leave. He stood in the doorway turning the radio over in his hands, popping the battery cover with his thumbnail and pressing it shut again. Pop, click. Pop, click.

“You’re keeping the bedroom set,” he said. It wasn’t a question.

“I don’t know. Maybe.”

“It’s maple. That’s good wood.”

The thread snapped. Linda pulled the ruffle free and a slide projector fell onto the carpet, a brown Kodak carousel with a cracked lid. She must have dislodged it from under the bed. A carousel tray was still attached, half-full of slides.

Michael stepped into the room and picked it up. He depressed the advance lever and something ratcheted inside.

“Still works,” he said.

“Don’t point it at the wall.”

“Why not.”

He set the radio down on the dresser and lifted the projector to the foot of the bed, squinting through the viewfinder. The fan whirred on. A white square hit the floral wallpaper.

“Turn the lamp off,” he said.

“We’re supposed to be clearing.”

“Turn it off.”

She clicked the lamp and the room went dim. The square on the wall flickered and then there was their father, squinting into the camera with a stringer of bluegill held at chest height. His free hand was shading his eyes. The fish scales caught the light and threw it back.

“She kept that one in,” Michael said.

He advanced the tray. Click. A Christmas tree with a bent angel. Click. Linda at maybe eleven, gap-toothed, holding a cat she didn’t remember.

“How many trays are there.”

“Just this one. The rest are empty slots.”

He clicked faster. Their mother at a card table, a half-played hand of solitaire frozen in front of her. A lawn chair. A Pontiac with a dented bumper.

Then a slide that was darker than the others, taken indoors without a flash. A man sitting on a couch, elbows on his knees, face blurred slightly by a slow shutter speed. He had a mustache and a short-sleeved shirt with a pen in the pocket.

Michael’s hand stopped on the advance lever.

Linda looked at the man’s shirt. The pocket had a flap with a snap. Her father had never worn shirts with snaps.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. The projector fan hummed. Dust moved in the beam of light.

“Is that Uncle Walt,” Michael said, knowing it wasn’t.

Uncle Walt had been dead since 1984 and he’d never had a mustache.

Linda walked to the projector and pressed the select button. The slide lifted out of the gate and slipped back into the carousel. The wall went white.

“The next house,” she said.

“What about it.”

“The next house we had. On Monroe Street. I remember that couch.”

She had come home early from school once, stomach flu. Walking up the driveway with her house key out. She had seen a car she didn’t recognize parked at the curb. When she came in the house was quiet in a way that made her stop in the vestibule. Then her mother’s voice from the living room, a tone she’d never heard, and a man’s voice answering. She’d backed out and sat on the front steps for an hour, her stomach forgotten.

“She kept it under the bed,” Michael said.

“She kept everything under the bed. That’s where things went.”

He reached down and unplugged the projector. The fan wound down like something letting go of a breath.

Linda picked up the dust ruffle from the floor and folded it once, twice, into a neat square that would go into a garbage bag.

“There’s a Goodwill box started in the living room,” she said.

Michael didn’t move. He was holding the slide carousel now, turning it slowly in his hands so the cardboard mounts clicked against each other.

“I can take it,” she said.

“Take what.”

“The bedroom set.”

He set the carousel on the dresser. The batteries in the radio had leaked a white crystalline crust that had crept into the battery compartment seam. He picked at it with his fingernail.

“You’d have to rent a truck anyway,” he said. “For the maple.”

“I know.”

“I can help you load Saturday.”

Through the window the late afternoon had flattened into a gray that made it impossible to tell what time it was. Linda reached over and clicked the lamp back on, and both of them blinked.

In the kitchen, the faucet dripped once into a pan left in the sink. From the floor above, the furnace kicked on and a vent somewhere whistled.

“I’m keeping the pickle forks,” Linda said.

Michael almost smiled. “Oyster.”

“Right.”

He lifted the radio from the dresser and pulled the battery cover off entirely, dropping it into his shirt pocket along with the crusted batteries. The empty compartment smelled faintly of copper.

Linda stood up, dust ruffle in one hand, and walked past him to the hallway. She stopped at the linen closet and pulled the chain for the overhead bulb. Shelves of sheets, pillowcases, a stack of blankets in plastic bags. She began taking them down, arms full of fabric that smelled of cedar and nothing else. Behind her she heard her brother go back to the kitchen and open another drawer.
