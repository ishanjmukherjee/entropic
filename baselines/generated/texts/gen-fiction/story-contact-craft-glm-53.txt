The signal came in on a Tuesday, and Doreen Plunkett had already put her hearing aids in backwards, which is why she heard it at all.

She was seventy-four and worked the front desk at the radio observatory two nights a week, mostly because the young engineers didn't answer the phone and somebody had to. The facility sat in a fold of hills outside Socorro, and Doreen had been taking messages there for eleven years. She knew the difference between the dryer tumbling wrong and the dryer tumbling wrong because something was interfering with the instruments. It was in her hips, somehow, the feeling.

That night the graduate students were all in Albuquerque at a wedding. Doreen was alone with the coffee maker and the(phone log when the interference came through the landline—not through the speakers, through the actual phone, a sound like crickets slowed down a thousand times, a sound with a shape to it.

She should have called Dr. Okafor. She wrote his number on a sticky note every shift and stuck it to the monitor so she wouldn't have to open the drawer. She looked at the note. Then she picked up the receiver and listened for another forty minutes, because the sound was doing something to the backs of her eyes, something adjacent to remembering.

At 2:15 she called her son Gary in Tulsa. He worked days at a grain elevator and answered with the TV on.

"Ma. It's one in the morning here."

"I need you to tell me I'm not crazy," she said. "Or tell me I am. Either one."

"Is it the truck? Did the truck break down again?"

"The truck is fine."

"Because I can send money, but I can't—"

"The truck is fine, Gary. Somebody's talking to me." She heard herself say it and it sounded like the onset of something. She knew how it sounded. Her sister had started the same way, at sixty-eight, and ended up in a facility near Pueblo that smelled of pee and hand lotion.

He was quiet. She could hear a commercial for something.

"I'll come out," he said finally. "I got vacation."

"I don't need you to come out."

"Well, I got it anyway. Might as well."

He arrived Thursday in a rented sedan, too new, the seatbelt cutting across his neck wrong so he kept pulling at it. He'd brought a cooler with bologna and a jar of pickles his wife had put up. He didn't ask about the sound. He fixed the hinge on the screen door at her apartment, replaced a washer in the bathroom sink, and stood in the kitchen eating pickle spears over the sink the way he'd done at fourteen.

"I'm not staying past Sunday," he said.

"I know that."

"You need anything from the store."

"I need you to take this seriously."

He put the pickle jar down. It was the first time in maybe nine years she'd said anything like that to him, and the want in the room rearranged itself around the word.

The observatory had, by Friday, noticed. Dr. Okafor called twice, and a woman from somewhere called three times, and Doreen gave them what she'd written down—times, durations, her descriptions of the shapes the sound made. She wrote them out in cursive because she'd never learned to type properly, and a young man from the lab came and photographed the pages with his phone like they were artifacts.

"They want you to come in," the young man said. "They want you to listen again. Under, uh, conditions."

"What conditions."

"Instruments. Recording. They want to know what you're hearing that the machines aren't."

She looked at him. He had a beard like an underside of a porch and couldn't hold eye contact.

"The machines are hearing it fine," she said. "That's not the problem. The problem is what it's saying."

Nobody in the room had wanted that sentence. She watched it move down the table, and it landed on Dr. Okafor, who put down his pen and went to the window and looked at the hills like he'd left his ticket in the car.

Gary drove her in the sedan. He sat in the lobby during the sessions with a vending machine sandwich. They ran the sound four times, five times, ten. On tape and through headphones and once through a speaker array that was frankly too loud, and everyone watched her face while she listened, which she did not care for. She felt like her sister in Pueblo. She did not tell Gary that part.

"Don't tell me what you're hearing, just tell us if it's the same," they kept saying, and it was, and it was, and on the eleventh run it wasn't.

"There's an answer in it now," she said. "It stopped about a minute ago. It answered."

That was when things got past her. Men she didn't know, a trailer with equipment, a folding table with coffee she wasn't offered but the visitors were. Her pages of cursive, laminated by then, or photocopied, passed hand to hand. Somewhere in the_SYS the phrase "the event" started and stuck. Within two years there would be documentaries, and in all of them there would be a woman at a front desk, archival footage, a still photo of the wrong woman actually, another Doreen Plunkett, a schoolteacher in Ohio, and neither of them ever got it fixed.

The decoding took eleven days and needed Doreen for six of them, and what came out at the end—if it was what came out at the end, and Dr. Okafor to his death said the math was a mirror, we were hearing our own signal returned, that all of it was reflection—was a number and a place. Coordinates in the Pisces region and a count: 1.

One what, everyone wanted to know. One alive. One coming. One of us, one like you. The arguments outlived everyone who'd been in that trailer.

Gary left on Sunday like he'd said. He hugged her at the car with one arm, the other holding the cooler, and she felt the whole weight of his childhood in it, the bus stops, the hospital when he was nine, the door he'd kicked in at sixteen. He didn't say the thing he wanted to say. She didn't say hers.

"You'll call," he said.

"If there's anything to call about."

"There's always something to call about, Ma."

He drove off with the seatbelt cutting his neck. She watched the sedan all the way to the county road.

The signals degraded within the year, scattered, stopped. They never came again, or they came constant and we lost the ear for them—Dr. Okafor's position, later, in a paper nobody assigned to undergraduates anymore. Doreen gave two interviews and refused the third. She went back to two nights a week at the desk for another six years. She kept answering the phone.

Gary came out every Thanksgiving, then every other year, then less. When she died the observatory sent a card with forty signatures she couldn't have read anyway. He found the cursive pages in a shoebox with the pickle jar's lid, kept for no reason he could name, and he kept them too, in the truck of the sedan, and then in a closet in Tulsa, and he never once played them for anyone, which she had asked him for, exactly, in a note he did not show his wife.
