The lamp had been burning for sixty years before Marcus arrived, and it would burn long after him. That was the first thing the old keeper told him on his first night at Gannet Point, words delivered like a warning, or perhaps a benediction. The man's name was Edwin, and he stayed only three days to teach Marcus the work before taking the boat to the mainland and retiring to a cottage in Ballymoor where, rumor said, he grew tomatoes and never once looked at the sea.

Marcus was thirty-two and had come to the lighthouse the way people come to most remote places—not fleeing anything dramatic, but worn down by the accumulation of ordinary disappointments. A failed engagement. A job in an office where the fluorescent lights hummed at a pitch that seemed to lodge behind his eyes. His mother's slow dying over two winters. When he saw the notice for a keeper at the Commissioners of Irish Lights, he applied before he could talk himself out of it.

The work was simpler than he'd imagined and more demanding than he'd expected. The lamp itself needed almost nothing from him—a great rotating eye, its mechanism precise as a clockmaker's heart. But the tower wanted constant tending. He polished the lens until he could see his own distorted face wavering in the glass. He scraped rust from the railing that circled the lantern room. He kept the log, recording wind speed and visibility and the passing of ships, their names and nationalities, guessing at these last from their lights when they passed in darkness.

The loneliness, which everyone had warned him about, arrived differently than promised. It did not come as an ache in the evenings, though the evenings were long. It came instead as a gradual thinning of his need for speech. By the second month, he found himself going three days without speaking aloud, and when he finally said something to the kettle—there now, there—his own voice startled him, hoarse and unfamiliar, the voice of a stranger who had wandered into his house.

He began to read aloud in the evenings, just to keep the sound of language near him. He worked through a shelf of books Edwin had left behind: maritime law, mostly, dry as old bones, but also a surprising collection of poetry. He read Yeats to the sea on nights when the fog came in thick and the foghorn sounded its low mournful note every thirty seconds, and the poems seemed to answer the horn, or perhaps to argue with it.

In spring, a family of seals took up residence on the rocks below the north side of the island. Marcus watched them from the gallery with the binoculars that hung by the door. There was one pup, born late, that the others seemed to tolerate rather than welcome, always at the edge of the huddle. Marcus named it Brendan, after the navigator monk, and took to narrating its small adventures in the log: Brendan fished the shallows at low tide. Brendan argued with a gull and lost. He knew it was against the spirit of the log, which was meant for weather and ships, but no one read the log but him, and later, he would think that this was when the book became something else—a record of a life rather than a record of conditions.

The storm came in October, the worst in thirty years the mainland papers said afterward. Marcus spent one whole night in the lantern room, not because the lamp needed him but because he could not bear to be away from it. The wind hit the tower like something with intent. Spray from waves breaking against the rocks rose past the windows, higher than the tower, so that for moments the light swept through water and made the whole storm glow, a slow rotating illumination of chaos. He watched a fishing trawler come around the point at two in the morning, running before the storm, and saw itsRunning lights pass safely westward, and he felt something he had no adequate word for. Not pride. Something more like purpose, or the relief of discovering that purpose was possible.

After the storm, he found damage on the gallery and spent a week repairing it, and when he radioed the mainland for supplies, he asked also for seeds. By the following summer he had a small stubborn garden in the lee of the keeper's cottage, growing potatoes and kale and nasturtiums that did nothing useful but which he defended fiercely to the visiting supply man, who had raised an eyebrow.

Years passed, and they passed strangely, both slowly and all at once. The lamp was automated in his ninth year—the notice came with apologies and restructuring plans—but Marcus was kept on as attendant, his duties reduced but his presence deemed valuable. He was glad of it, in his quiet way. The light still turned. He still recorded Brendan's descendants in the log, though by now the seals were simply seals again, and he simply the man who watched them.

He was old when a young woman came out on the supply boat, an engineer with questions about the old mechanism, and she stayed for tea and asked him why he'd stayed. Marcus considered the question for a long time, long enough that she shifted uncomfortably in her chair. Then he told her about the trawler in the storm, and about the thinning of speech, and about how some people are made for crowds and some for corridors, and how a lighthouse is a corridor between sea and shore, darkness and morning, and he had simply made his home in the passage.

She didn't fully understand—her face said so—but she wrote something in her notebook, and after she left, Marcus climbed the tower in the blue hour when the first stars appeared, and lit the lamp by hand, the old way, though the automation would do it within minutes anyway. He did it because Edwin had shown him how, sixty years and one lifetime ago, and because some things, once entrusted, want to be tended.

The light turned. The sea held its old darkness. Somewhere out past the point, ships moved safely through the night, and Marcus stood at the rail, keeping watch, which was, in the end, the whole of it.
