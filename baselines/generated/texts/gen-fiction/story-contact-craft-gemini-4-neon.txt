Ray slid a folded paper towel under Sandra’s mug before the brown ring could set in the laminate. On the monitor between them, a column of four-digit integers stalled, blinked twice, and began scrolling again from the bottom of the glass.

“You didn’t tell Ken about the bearing on Four,” Ray said.

Sandra didn’t look up from the apple she was quartering with a pocketknife. “It’s not the bearing. It’s the grease.”

“It’s making that sound again. Like a shopping cart.”

“Then put grease on it.” She pared a strip of skin off a wedge, the blade catching the fluorescent light from the ceiling tube, and dropped the peel into the metal wastebasket by her heel. The trash liner had slipped down inside the rim.

Ray leaned his hip against the edge of the desk. The thermal printer on the filing cabinet gave a short, dry rasp and fed out two inches of gray paper. Neither of them reached for it. Four minutes earlier, Ray had sent a thirty-second carrier wave with a prime-number cadence toward a dark patch of sky six degrees off the ecliptic. The return had arrived forty seconds ago, clean of background hiss, with the primes reversed and a fifth integer tacked onto the end that hadn’t been in his packet.

“I’m going to have to log the dish time,” Ray said. “Ken checks the meter on Fridays.”

“Today’s Wednesday.”

“He checks it early when he’s going to Reno.” Ray picked up a blue Bic from the blotter and clicked it twice against his thumbnail. “If I put it under maintenance, he’ll ask why we were pointed at nothing.”

Sandra chewed, her jaw working slowly. A small brown bruise sat on the skin of the remaining half of the apple. “We weren’t pointed at nothing.”

“You know what I mean.”

She wiped the knife blade on the thigh of her jeans, folded it shut, and set it beside the keyboard. On the screen, the column stopped again. A second window opened on the left side of the monitor—one Ray hadn’t written into the diagnostic script—and began drawing a series of nested triangles in green phosphor.

Ray’s hand stopped clicking the pen. He looked at the triangles, then at the wall clock above the corkboard, where a yellowed shift schedule from April was held up by three pushpins.

“Four minutes,” he said, his voice flat. “That’s inside the belt.”

“Three minutes, fifty-two seconds,” Sandra said. “It’s moving.”

Ray stepped around the desk toward the landline bolted to the cinderblock wall. He had already rehearsed the phrasing in the hallway while the coffee was dripping: Ken, we’ve got a non-terrestrial transponder with a structured reply, I’m holding the raw tape. He didn’t want to sound excited. Excited people got moved to the day shift at the main office, where there were no windows and the carpet smelled like damp wool.

He lifted the beige handset. There was no dial tone. He tapped the plastic cradle twice with his index finger, then followed the coiled cord down behind the filing cabinet.

The clear plastic jack lay on the linoleum, six inches from the wall outlet, next to a dead moth.

Ray stayed crouched by the cabinet for a second, his knees popping. When he stood up, he was holding the end of the cord between his thumb and forefinger.

“The line’s out,” he said.

Sandra was leaning forward, her elbows on the desk, watching the triangles flatten into parallel lines. “Yeah.”

“It’s not out, Sandra. It’s unplugged.”

She reached over and tapped the spacebar. The parallel lines cleared, and a new string of characters—not integers this time, but the phonetic alphabet from the station’s weather-band loop—began to print across the top of the screen, one letter every two seconds.

Ray looked down at the spiral notebook lying open beside her elbow. He hadn’t noticed it before because she’d had her forearm over the spine. The top of the page was dated Monday. The lines were filled with her small, slanted handwriting: timestamps, azimuth readings, and, at the bottom, a list of three-word phrases she’d keyed into the transmitter. Water is wet. The door sticks. My sister’s name.

“Monday,” Ray said.

“Sunday night, really,” Sandra said. “After you left. The dish drifted off the calibration star and picked it up on the side lobe.”

“You’ve been talking to it since Sunday.”

“It’s been talking to me. Mostly it just repeats the syntax back until it gets the spacing right.” She took a sip from her mug, her eyes never leaving the glass. “Yesterday it asked about the hum.”

“What hum?”

“The transformer out by the gate. It could hear it in the carrier.”

Ray looked at the plastic jack in his hand. The little locking tab was intact. If he plugged it in, Ken would be on the gravel road in forty minutes, and by noon there would be men in windbreakers stepping on the linoleum, asking who had touched the keyboard first.

“You can’t keep it in a notebook, Sandra,” he said.

“I’m not keeping it.” She picked up the last quarter of the apple and bit into it. “I just didn’t want to explain the grease to Ken yet.”

On the filing cabinet, the thermal printer rasped again, longer this time, the paper curling over the metal lip and dropping toward the floor. Ray didn’t move to catch it. He let the phone cord slip from his fingers; the plastic jack hit the baseboard with a small, hollow click.

Sandra swallowed, wiped her fingers on the paper towel under her mug, and put both hands back on the keys.
