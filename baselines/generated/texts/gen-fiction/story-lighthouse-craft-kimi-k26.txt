The bulb burned out at 2:47. Eleanor heard the motor stutter before the light died, a sound like a man clearing his throat in another room. She was already awake, sitting at the kitchen table with her sister's letter unopened since Tuesday. The coffee had gone cold in the cup she held with both hands, though she did not drink it.

She climbed the spiral stairs without turning on lights. Her knee clicked on the fourteenth step, same as always. At the service room she opened the cabinet where they kept spares, fingers finding the wrapped bulb by shape. The old one came out hot enough to hiss against her damp palm. She set it on the metal shelf and did not suck the burn.

"Need any help?"

Mitch stood in the doorway in his undershirt, suspenders hanging at his hips. He'd lost weight; she could see the notch where his belt had settled for fifteen years, now three holes past.

"Go back to bed."

"I heard it quit."

"It's done now." She held the new bulb up to the socket, aligning the pins by touch. The twist, the catch, the brief flare of contact before steady light. She turned the occulting mechanism by hand to watch the beam cut through the black window, sweep, darkness, sweep, darkness.

Mitch stayed in the doorway. From below, the foghorn's compression throbbed against the foundation. She'd forgotten to check the diesel level.

"Your sister write?" he asked.

"Yes."

"She okay?"

"Yes."

Mitch pulled his suspenders onto his shoulders, a gesture of dressing without purpose. He'd sold the boat after the funeral, though he still took the skiff to the mainland Tuesdays for supplies. Tuesdays were his drinking day, the only one he allowed. Last week he'd returned with the wrong bulbs, standard domestic wattage, twenty in a box. They used four a season. She'd put them on the shelf without comment.

"You coming down?" he asked.

"I'll check the generator."

"The gauge said half."

"When?"

He worked his jaw. "Evening."

She descended past him, close enough to smell the metallic residue of sleep, the faint iodine of the soap he used. At the landing she stopped. "The coffee's cold if you want it."

"I'll make fresh."

"Suit yourself."

The diesel tank stood in a concrete room off the base of the tower, painted green, sweating condensation. She knocked the gauge with her knuckle: quarter tank, the needle sticking, same problem since 1983. She recorded the reading in the log with her left hand, the right in her pocket. The numbers resembled her sister's handwriting. They had been taught together at the same table, copying the same sentences.

She did not open the letter. She knew it contained an offer, a plan, a train schedule. Esther had sent five such letters since the spring, each one slightly more specific, as if longitude and latitude would make the destination real. Portland. A room already rented. A doctor who asked no questions until you were ready to answer.

Eleanor filled the diesel from the jerry can, two gallons, enough for four days. The fuel smelled of something rotting underwater. She wiped the neck of the can with a rag and hung it on its hook.

Mitch was in the kitchen when she returned, not making coffee but sitting with her cold cup before him. The letter lay between them, still sealed.

"Quarter tank," she said. "I'll fetch more Thursday."

"Jimmy Fenn carries it now. Price went up."

"I know the price."

"He'd deliver, probably. For extra."

"I don't mind the skiff."

Mitch turned the cup in its ring of condensation. His thumbnails were split from the line, from reef knots pulled dry. "Letter from Portland," he said.

"Yes."

"Cold there, I hear. This time of year."

"Warmer than here."

He looked at her then, eyes the color of fish gone to opacity. "You always said you hated cities. The noise."

"I did say that."

She picked up the letter, feeling the thickness of folded pages, the scrape of something enclosed. A ticket, perhaps. The doctor's address. She put it in her apron pocket with the burnt hand.

"That bulb's the last of the shipment," Mitch said. "Three months since we ordered."

"I'll check with Fenn. He trades in all sorts."

"He said January, last I asked."

"Then January."

She opened the refrigerator, not hungry, needing the light and the machine hum. A wrapped package on the second shelf: halibut, Wednesday's catch, his first in six weeks. She'd watched him from the tower window, the skiff listing, the line slack, then the forty minutes of nothing after the strike, just his arms working. She'd turned the beacon to calculate the intervals without watching.

"Fish for supper?" she asked.

"If you want."

"Potatoes are soft."

"Fenn had turnips. I forgot to get any."

"I'll manage."

She closed the refrigerator and stood with her back to him, counting the revolutions of the beacon above, the vibration through the cabinet knobs. Seventeen seconds between flashes. She heard his chair scrape but did not turn.

"I'll trim the wicks," he said. "Before light."

"Thank you."

His footsteps retreated down the passage, heavier than climbing. The warped door to the lamp room resisted, then gave, squealing on the hinge she'd asked him to oil. She stood in the kitchen darkness, waiting for the fractional increase in brightness when he opened the service door, the glimpse of his silhouette against the Fresnel lens, rearing like a figure through ice.

She touched the letter through her apron. Agnes, it said on the front, her given name, which no one used anymore, which Esther alone remembered from the papers their mother signed. Eleanor she had chosen herself, fourteen years old, from the name of a queen in a water-stained book, a woman who had fled one island for another, who had written letters her husband burned unopened.

The light turned above. She went to the window to watch it complete its arc, catching the froth at the reef, the empty mooring where Fenn's supply boat would come, would go, the mainland a smudge only visible in certain weathers when certain things were true.

Her hand in the pocket closed on the envelope. She did not open it. She did not need to. The diesel would run four days. The halibut would keep until tomorrow. When Mitch descended, she would be in their bed, feigning sleep, her back set toward his weight settling on the mattress, toward the smell of his hands, of paraffin and mineral oil, of everything that kept the dark at distance, one rotation at a time.
