The letter was from a girl who signed herself Deirdre, and Merrill read it a third time before the kettle went.

Dear Mr. Halstead. I am doing a project for school about the lighthouse at Point Vanuer and my teacher said someone still lives there. My question is what do you do all winter and also does it get lonely. You can write back or not.

No address. The envelope had come inside the supply crate, forwarded from the Coast Office, so someone there had read it before he had. He made his tea and stood at the window with the mug against his sternum, the way he did every morning, and looked at what there was to look at: the channel, gray going on gray, a trawler crabbing north with its gulls.

There had been a woman named Ruth for eleven years. That was the thing the school project would not find in any book. She had kept the second watch, and after her sister took ill in Duluth she had gone to Duluth, and that was six winters ago now, and the letters had gotten shorter and then there was one that said she'd met a man named Carl who had a good pension and a house with gutters that worked. Merrill had written back, I'm glad the gutters work, and had never mailed anything again.

He answered the girl on the back of a tide chart.

Dear Deirdre. In winter I scrape thesalt off the glass, which takes an hour when it freezes. I wind nothing anymore because the mechanism is electric, but I check it. I read. There are four hundred and eleven steps. I know because I counted them once for a man writing a pamphlet. The pamphlet came out and the number was wrong in it.

He read it over. He added: It does not get lonely the way you would think. It gets quiet in a way that has weight to it, and after a while you carry the quiet around even when you go to town, and people can see it on you, a little.

He mailed it when the tender came with the diesel and the mail sack, and the pilot, a heavy man named Borys, handed the sack up and said, "Your light was late last Tuesday. Two minutes past sundown by my watch."

"You can't see sundown from the water with cloud like Tuesday."

"I saw it," Borys said. "Two minutes."

"Then have them check the sensor log."

"Sure," Borys said, and looked at him longer than the sentence needed, and went down the ladder.

The sensor log was fine. Merrill had been in the lamp room with the door shut, sitting on the stool, because the Tuesdays were hard since the Tuesday that had been six winters of ago, and sometimes on Tuesdays a man sat down. He wrote nothing about that either.

The girl wrote back in October. A real envelope this time, blue, with a horse sticker in the corner.

Dear Mr. Halstead. Thank you for answering. My teacher read part of it out loud. I got an A minus because I didn't do a poster like you were supposed to. I have a follow-up question. Have you ever saved anybody?

He sat with that one for two weeks. The kettle went, the glass got scraped, a bulk carrier came through at 3 a.m. and he logged it, hull down, riding high, probably empty, going somewhere to fill up.

In November of the fourth winter he had gone out on the walk after an ice storm, standard procedure, chip the glaze off the railing before a man could go over on it, and he had gone over on it. The drop was forty feet onto the rocks and he had caught the lowest rung of the service ladder with one wrist and hung there for a while. He had thought, hanging: not yet, which was strange, because he could not think who the sentence was addressed to. Then he had climbed, one-armed, badly, and made the walkway, and sat there bleeding through his sleeve, laughing in a way that had frightened him more than the fall.

I have not saved anybody, he wrote. The light has. Boats do not hit this point, though before the light they did, and there is a list of wrecks at the historical society in town with the names of the drowned, and painter's row at the cemetery is mostly them, young men, all in a row like birds on a wire. I have not saved anybody. I have also not drowned anybody, which is a thing to say for a job, I suppose. Do you want to be a lighthouse keeper?

I don't know what I want to be, the girl wrote. My mom says that's normal for thirteen. I want to know things. Did the keeper before you save anybody?

There had been a keeper before him, Aldous Pike, forty years on the light, and Merrill wrote back the truth: He pulled two boys out of the water in 1958 and then never went in the water again, not once, not even his feet, for forty years. I asked him why once and he said "Why does a person do anything twice?" He died in the spring, in a bed in Marquette, and I went to the funeral. There were eleven people. Nobody knew what to say about a man nobody knew what to say about.

After that the letters came less from her and more from him, and the thing he never wrote, ever, in any of them, was what he was doing: which was checking the tide charts every year against the ones from before Ruth left, to see whether he'd counted the steps wrong, some year, some watch. Four hundred and eleven every time. He kept counting anyway.

In March the tender came and Borys handed up the mail and said, "Coast Office says the light's going automatic in June. Full remote. You'll be reassigned or retired, they'll send the paperwork."

Merrill took the sack. "How late was it Tuesday?" he said.

"One minute," Borys said. "Getting better."

The June tender came with two technicians and a crate of electronics, and they worked three days, and one of them, the younger one, asked him, "What do you do all winter, stuck out here?"

Merrill looked at him. "I answer my mail," he said.

There was one letter left in the box after the light went dark for good, addressed in a hand that was neater than the girl's had become, as if the girl had grown up all at once. He read it standing on the dock with his two duffel bags at his feet.

Dear Mr. Halstead. I got into Michigan State. My mom cried and then said don't tell anyone she cried. I have one more question and then I'll leave you alone, I promise. On the day you leave, is the light on or off?

He sat on his duffel bag and wrote back on his knee with the boat waiting, and what he wrote was:

Off. That's how I'll know the difference.
