Dennis had the wrapper of the sandwich spread flat on the console like a placemat, and he was eating over it carefully, because Ruth had told him twice in eleven years about crumbs in the keyboards.

"Run it again," she said.

"I ran it four times."

"Run it again with the other offset."

He wiped his fingers on his jeans and ran it again. The printer in the corner was the old dot-matrix one that nobody had been allowed to throw out, and it started its grinding, left to right, left to right, while the two of them watched the paper come up out of it in a pale strip.

It was the same. It was the same as the last four times, and it was the same as the thing Walter had built in 1999 on the kitchen table in Socorro with graph paper and a pencil he sharpened with a paring knife. The counting numbers along the top. The elements. The little stack of the double helix. The figure of a person, arms at the sides, like a gingerbread man somebody had stepped on. The dish underneath it.

"It's ours," Dennis said. "It's just ours coming back. It's a reflection, or it's a bounce off something, or it's a — I don't know. Ruth, it's ours."

"It's not a bounce. A bounce is twenty-six years round trip from nowhere. There's nothing out there to bounce it."

"So it's a hoax."

"Go ahead and call Pruett and tell him it's a hoax."

He didn't. He folded the sandwich wrapper in half and then in half again.

She tore the strip off the printer and laid it next to the framed copy on the wall, the one from the press kit, with Walter's name in small type at the bottom and somebody else's name in larger type above it. She held the strip up against the glass and moved it until the rows lined up.

The counting numbers were the same. The elements were the same. She went down the helix with her thumbnail.

Beside the little figure, in the original, there was a vertical bar and a binary number. The height of the transmitter species, expressed in wavelengths. Walter had argued for that for a week. Nobody else on the committee had wanted it. He'd said, they'll want to know how big we are, Ruthie, that's the first thing you'd want to know.

She counted the bits in the strip, and then she counted them again.

"What," Dennis said.

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing with your mouth."

"Do you have a calculator that isn't on a computer."

He found one in the drawer under the coffee maker, a solar one with a cracked face, and she stood under the fluorescent light so it would turn on. She typed, and divided, and stood looking at it.

The number in the original came to five feet nine inches. She knew that without the calculator. She had watched him write it in. She had said, you're five seven, Walt, and he'd said, it's an average, it's a species, it isn't me, and she had let it go because the kids were in the bath and the water was running cold.

The number on the strip came to five feet seven and one quarter.

"Is it bad?" Dennis said. "Is it like, a threat."

"It's a height."

"Whose?"

She put the calculator down on top of the folded sandwich wrapper.

"Did they change anything else," he said. "Ruth. Did they change anything else."

"No."

"Just that."

"Just that."

He got up and went to the window, which looked at nothing, the dish lit from underneath out in the dark and the gravel lot and his truck with the camper shell. He'd been saying since August he was going to put in for days so he could drive to Amarillo and see the baby. He said, to the window, "Average height for a man is five nine. I looked it up once. For my license."

"It's lower now. Worldwide."

"I don't think it's five seven."

"No."

"So they got it wrong."

She didn't say anything. She was looking at the little figure, which was the same, the same squared-off head, the same arms. Walter had wanted to give it hands. The committee said hands were wasteful, too many bits. He'd drawn them in on her copy anyway, at home, in pencil, three fingers each, and she didn't know where that copy was. She thought it might be in the garage in the box with the tax returns. She thought her daughter might have thrown that box out when they did the garage.

"I have to call Pruett," Dennis said.

"I know."

"I have to call him right now, Ruth, it's been forty minutes."

"Go ahead."

He picked up the phone on the wall and then put it back down and picked up his cell instead, and went out into the hallway with it, and she heard him say Mr. Pruett, sorry, sorry, it's Dennis Kowalski, at the array, and then the door swung shut on its slow arm.

She sat in his chair. It was warm. She smoothed the strip flat on the console with the side of her hand and took the pen out of the cup, the blue one that skipped, and in the margin next to the bar she wrote the number in feet and inches, small, and then she sat there with the pen against the paper, not pressing, while out in the hall he said no, sir, no, I don't think it's a bounce.
