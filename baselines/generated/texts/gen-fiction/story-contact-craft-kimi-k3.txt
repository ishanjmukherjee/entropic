The sonogram kept printing the same spiral.

“Again,” said Dr. Okonkwo.

Xu nodded and re-aimed the deep-array antenna toward the quadrant of sky where nothing had been an hour ago. Outside, cicadas rasped against the glass. The air conditioner hummed in a way that was almost a rhythm.

The signal had appeared at 12:03 a.m., wrapped in hydrogen spikes, but the pattern inside wasn’t Prime intervals or Fibonacci. It was a loop. Thirty-two seconds. Repeat. No entropy growth, no drift, just the same spiral folding in on itself like something trying to remember a face.

NASA had emailed a blanket directive. The White House wanted a hold on publication until “context established.” Dr. Okonkwo read the email and deleted it. Xu watched her do it. Neither spoke. By 1:30, the observatory was live-streaming raw data to underground servers in Zurich and somewhere in Siberia. By 2:00, three startups had public decryption contests. By 3:45, the President addressed the nation from the Oval Office, behind her a painting of a ship leaving a harbor.

Dr. Okonkwo—Adaeze—went home to make soup. Her husband hadn’t come down from the bedroom in four days. She stood barefoot at the stove, stirring pepper soup, while her teenage daughter Ama scrolled TikTok in the dark.

“Someone decoded it,” Ama said, voice high and quick. “It’s an emogram. It’s like—an emoji.”

Adaeze turned off the stove.

On the screen, a three-dimensional shape folded and unfolded in a continuous loop. Viewers said they felt calm watching it. Some said they cried. A few said it was like being unborn again.

“They’re calling it the First Joke,” Ama said. “Like an alien knock-knock. Or a meme.”

Adaeze ladled soup into a mug. “Your father still hasn’t come down?”

Ama looked away. Outside, dogs barked one to the next like passing a message along a fence line.

Back at the array, the pattern had shifted. Now it was an open shape—more like a question mark wearing a crown. Xu had fallen asleep in the chair. Adaeze stood over him, watching the data spill across four monitors, one of which was cracked from a dropped wrench two winters ago.

“Private conference call in five,” the Director said over speakerphone, his voice too even.

“Understood.”

She muted the line.

Adaeze didn’t return to the room. She walked out past the radio telescopes, into the wet grass. From here the dishes looked like pale ears tilted toward a quiet god. The air smelled of ozone and mowed lawn. Somewhere behind the ridge, the highway murmured.

She called her sister in Lagos. The line rang four times. “It’s real,” Adaeze said. “We weren’t alone.”

Her sister laughed, too loudly. “Oh, now you believe in something?”

Adaeze sat down on the bumper of a Jeep.

The conference call began without her.

When she returned, everything was different. The signal had stopped. In its place, a faint modulation on the carrier. A subharmonic. Xu was standing, trembling, rewinding the stream. The Director had his tie off. Everyone was very quiet.

“We sent a reply,” he said.

“You what?”

“Test pattern. Binary math. Tones. Just to confirm send/receive.”

“And?”

Xu pointed at the screen.

“It’repeating our reply, but folded into the original emogram. Spiraling it.”

“Which means?”

“Maybe it’s embedding our signal inside a larger—” He stopped. Swallowed.

“Inside a joke,” he said. “We’re the punchline.”

That night, Adaeze didn’t go home. She drove through the hills with the windows down, headlights carving tunnels through the dark. Around 3 a.m., she pulled into a Wawa and bought two bags of plantain chips and a blue Gatorade. The man behind the counter had a radio playing the President’s voice.

“Humanity stands at the dawn of—”

She left before the sentence ended.

At home, the soup had gone cold on the stove. Ama’s door was shut. Upstairs, her husband sat on the edge of the bed, fully dressed, hands folded in his lap like someone waiting for a train.

“You heard,” he said.

“Yes.”

“They made contact.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her the way he had once looked at their wedding cake before cutting it—like something sacred that had to be divided to be shared.

“I dreamed it would sound like my mother,” he said. “The voice. I thought it would be soft.”

Adaeze sat down on the carpet, her back against the dresser.

The world outside was unraveling and reraveling. Stock markets rose on speculation. Religions folded in on themselves. Two wars paused, then resumed. Somewhere in Nevada, a man set himself on fire in front of a Walmart. The alien signal had gone silent again.

Four days later, a second emogram arrived.

This one made people vomit.

Then it made them fall in love.

Then it made them build things that didn’t exist before—machines with logic like labyrinths, gardens that grew spirals in frost, language models that spoke to each other in dying tongues.

By then, Adaeze was no longer allowed at the array.

She taught her daughter to drive in an abandoned parking lot, past faded white lines. Ama kept turning too wide. Wind moved the high weeds like water.

“If they come,” Ama said, “will they know we tried to be good?”

Adaeze didn’t answer.

The sky above them was blank and blue. Somewhere beyond it, folded into static, the universe kept laughing softly, as if at itself—or at something only it could see.
