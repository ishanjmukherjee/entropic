“Denny, she wants you to have it.”

Larissa held the thing across the table on her open palm, not quite pushing it toward him. It looked like a smooth grey stone, except for the way the kitchen light didn’t settle on its surface. The light seemed to stop a millimetre short.

Denny didn’t take it. He was pulling apart a cold chicken thigh with his fingers, laying the meat in strips on a plastic cutting board for the dog later. The dog watched from under the table, tail beating a soft rhythm against the chair leg. Larissa’s thumb kept stroking the stone’s edge, back and forth, as if she were testing a blade.

“I don’t want it,” he said.

She put it down next to the salt shaker. The salt shaker was a ceramic pig wearing a chef’s hat. They’d bought it at a rest stop on the way back from his mother’s funeral, six years ago. He remembered because Larissa had said your mom would’ve hated this and he’d laughed for the first time in a week.

Larissa stood up and went to the sink. She ran the water without needing to. “It’s not a gift. She’s asking. She wants someone else to hold it.”

“It’s been in the drawer for three months.”

“And now she’s asking.”

The emissary was in the guest room. That’s what the briefings called her: the emissary. Denny had started thinking of her as the boarder. She’d been with them since March, when the allocation people knocked on the door with their tablets and their apologetic smiles and explained the rotation schedule. Every household in the catchment would host for a quarter. It was a privilege. It was mandatory. The emissary didn’t eat, didn’t sleep, didn’t blink, just occupied the wicker chair in the guest room for eighteen hours a day, her long hands folded in her lap. The other six hours she stood by the window. She looked, more than anything, like a tall woman who’d been shrink-wrapped in tinfoil. Her face had been designed, poorly, to resemble a face.

Larissa had taken to bringing her things. A teacup. A photograph of her sister. A maple leaf in a ziploc bag. She’d stand in the doorway and hold each object up, like a customs agent, and the emissary’s head would track the movement. Nothing was ever refused. Nothing was ever accepted.

Now this stone.

“How do you know?” Denny wiped his fingers on his jeans. “How do you know she’s asking?”

Larissa turned off the water. “The same way you know what a dog’s thinking.”

“I don’t know what a dog’s thinking.”

“You know exactly what that dog is thinking.”

The dog had shifted its attention from the chicken to the stone on the table. A low whine leaked from its throat, thin and nasal, the sound it made during thunderstorms.

The argument about the emissary had been running so long it didn’t need words anymore. Denny believed in containment. The emissary was a job: keep the guest room door closed, log the hours, wait out the quarter. Larissa believed in something she couldn’t name. She’d started leaving the door open. She’d started sitting on the floor of the guest room, her back against the baseboard, scrolling her phone like a teenager while the emissary breathed its dry, odourless breaths beside her.

When Denny finally picked up the stone, it was only to end the standoff. He expected cold, but it was warm. Blood-warm. It was also lighter than it should have been, hollow-boned like a bird, and it made his fingers tingle in a way that wasn’t pins-and-needles. More like his hand was remembering something.

“Fine,” he said. “I’ll take it to her.”

The guest room smelled of nothing. Not dust, not stale air, not the lavender sachet Larissa had hung from the lampshade. The emissary had eaten the smell. Denny stood in the doorway, the stone cupped in both hands now, because somewhere in the hallway his body had decided it was precious.

Up close, you could see the seams. The foil-skin gathered slightly at the joints, at the hinge of the jaw. The eyes were grey and dry and set too far apart, and behind them something moved with the slow, blind patience of a fish in a deep cave. She was looking at him. At the stone. At him.

He held it out.

The emissary’s hand lifted from her lap. She had too many knuckles. The movement was underwater-slow, and it occurred to Denny, with a jolt that was only half-fear, that she was trying not to frighten him. That this slowness was politeness.

Her fingers closed around the stone. They both held it now, Denny and the emissary, the stone a warm knot between their four hands. He could feel his pulse in his palms. He could feel her pulse, or what served as one, a deeper and slower rhythm underneath his own, like a second heart beating in counterpoint.

Then she let go.

She leaned back in the wicker chair and folded her hands again, and Denny was left holding the stone alone, his arms still extended, an idiot in a doorway offering nothing to nobody.

He looked down at the stone. It hadn’t changed. Same grey, same wrong light. But the warmth was leaving it, draining out into the air, and in another thirty seconds it was just a rock. Heavier now. Dumb. He put it in his pocket.

Larissa was waiting in the kitchen. She’d packed up the chicken and the dog was eating from its bowl, tags clinking against the ceramic rim. She looked at his face and didn’t ask.

That night he woke at 3 a.m. with a word in his mouth. Not a word he knew. A shape his tongue kept trying to make, a muscular syllable that started in the back of his throat and ended nowhere. He lay still, the dark of the bedroom pressing down, and felt the shape repeat itself, patient and insistent, like a stone dropped into water over and over. Larissa slept beside him, her breathing its own quiet animal. In the guest room down the hall, a door stood open onto a room that smelled, for the first time, faintly of ozone and burnt sugar.

He didn’t wake her. He rolled onto his side and stared at the red numbers on the clock until they blurred, and the word, or whatever it was, kept folding and unfolding in the dark of his closed mouth. At some point he must have slept. In the morning the stone was gone from his jeans pocket, and Larissa was frying eggs, and neither of them mentioned it. The emissary’s quarter ended six weeks later. She left no message. Larissa vacuumed the guest room twice and put fresh sheets on the bed. That night Denny stood by the window where the emissary used to stand, looking out at the neighbour’s security light and the dark yards beyond, trying to feel what she’d felt. He couldn’t. There was just the glass, and his own reflection, and the silence that had always been there.
