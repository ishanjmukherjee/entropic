"You don't tie it," Marisol said. "You fold the top over the rim and let the weight hold it. You tie it, you got to cut it later, and then you're standing there with scissors."

Dale folded the liner over the rim. The plastic was thin enough that it clung to the hair on his wrist, and when he pulled his hand back it came with him a little, like it wanted to go too.

"Good. Forty more on ten, then eleven."

The cart had a caster that wouldn't swivel until she kicked it, twice, with the side of her shoe. She did this without looking, the way you'd turn off an alarm. Dale pushed. The corridor ran past the conference rooms, frosted glass with names stenciled at eye level: Lakeview, Ridgeline, Cedar. Lakeview's whiteboard still said Q3 TARGETS?? in green marker, the question marks gone faint where someone's sleeve had dragged.

In the copy room he reached behind the fake ficus and found the switch on the first try.

Marisol was watching him from the doorway. "Who showed you that?"

"Lucky."

"Took me a month to find that. I used to do this room in the dark." She came in and took the vacuum cord off the hook and handed it to him, plug first. "Last guy, he lasted four nights. He kept saying it was the smell. Of what, I said. He couldn't tell me."

"Toner," Dale said.

She considered this. "Maybe."

Her phone buzzed in the pocket of her smock. She took it out, read it, turned it face down on top of the paper shredder, and left it there while she showed him how to wind the cord so it wouldn't kink, over the elbow and back to the hand, over the elbow and back.

On eleven the carpet was the same brown with a gray fleck, worn pale in a path from the elevators to the kitchen. Dale went down the row of cubicles emptying bins. Granola bar wrappers. A banana peel gone black at the stem. A printout with a single paragraph circled in red and the word NO in the margin, pressed hard enough to tear.

At the fourth cubicle from the window he stopped. There was a cushion on the chair now, the kind with a cutout for the tailbone, and a photo of a beagle in a Halloween hat. The monitor hung at an angle on its arm, sagging toward the desk.

He took a dime out of his pocket, fit it into the screw at the joint, and turned it a quarter turn. The monitor came level.

"We don't touch the equipment," Marisol said behind him.

"It was loose."

"Then they put in a ticket."

"They put in tickets," Dale said. He put the dime back in his pocket. "For a while."

She didn't answer that. She was looking at the beagle.

The corner office at the end had its light on. A woman sat with her shoes off and her feet up on an open drawer, eating almonds out of a bag and scrolling with one finger. Dale reached for the bin by her door.

"Leave that one, hon, I'm still using it," she said, and didn't look up.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Thanks, you guys are great."

He moved on to the next door. Marisol stood a moment longer by the corner office, then followed with the cart, and kicked the caster harder than she needed to.

At break they sat in the kitchen on eleven because Marisol said the one on ten had a microwave that tripped the breaker. She heated rice in a margarine tub with a paper towel over it. Taped to the microwave door was a sheet of copy paper, the corners curled and the tape gone amber: PLEASE COVER YOUR FOOD. THIS MEANS YOU. — D.K.

Marisol read it while the rice turned. Then she looked at the badge clipped to his smock, the one they'd printed that afternoon, the photo with his eyes half shut.

"People never cover it," she said.

"No."

"You'd think, with a sign."

The microwave beeped. She took the tub out with two fingers and set it on the counter and didn't eat it right away. Dale drank from the water fountain, which still ran warm for the first three seconds, and he still waited the three seconds.

At two she picked her phone up off the shredder on the way back down. She read whatever was there and typed something short and put it away.

In the freight elevator, she leaned on the cart handle. The walls were lined with gray quilted pads, and someone had written Tony B in ballpoint on one of them, and under it, in different ink, who.

"Tomorrow you start on twelve," she said.

"I can do eleven."

"I'll do eleven. You do twelve. Twelve's lawyers. They don't eat at their desks, it goes fast."

"Okay."

"You do twelve and then come find me."

The doors shut. The elevator dropped with the small hitch it had always had between nine and eight, and Dale shifted his weight before it came, and Marisol saw him do it, and looked at the numbers going down.
