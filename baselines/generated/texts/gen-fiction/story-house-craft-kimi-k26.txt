The gravy boat was Margaret's. Patricia knew this because her mother had never used it—had, in fact, kept it in the china cabinet's highest shelf, behind the wedding crystal no one had wanted. She held it now, porcelain green with a painted rose, and looked at her brother.

"Doris wanted this," she said.

"Doris can want it." Brian was on his knees, fishing something from under the radiator. A cat toy, yellowed with age. Their mother hadn't owned a cat since 2003. He set it on the windowsill without comment and stood, knees clicking.

Patricia wrapped the gravy boat in newspaper and placed it in the box labeled KEEP. The marker had bled through the cardboard in places.

"There's a water stain," Brian said, "up there. By the corner."

She didn't look. "Hasn't that always been there?"

"No." He was still looking up. "Not always."

They had been at it since nine. Patricia had brought coffee from the shop on Milwaukee, two cups because Brian forgot these things, and he'd accepted his without remark and drunk it cold. The house smelled of cardboard and the particular silence of rooms without curtains. She had taken the curtains down first thing, folded them mothballed flat, and Brian had watched her do it without offering to help.

Now he was in the kitchen, opening drawers. The junk drawer, specifically, third from the left of the stove. Everyone had one. Patricia knew what was in it because she'd gone through it last week, alone, telling Brian she was just checking on the pipes. Rubber bands in a ball. A Phillips head with a striped handle. Takeout menus for restaurants that had closed. Their father's fishing license, 1987, the year he died. She had held it and not shown it to anyone.

"Found your envelope," Brian called out.

She went to the doorway. He was holding a manila envelope, nine by twelve, unsealed. She recognized her own handwriting, the address of her apartment in Oak Park, crossed out in blue pen. Return to sender. Inside, she knew, were photographs. She'd mailed them three years ago when her mother asked after them, then never mentioned again.

"Must've come back," Patricia said.

"Must've." Brian set it on the counter, not in any box. "You want it in keep?"

"Trash."

He didn't move to throw it away. She didn't move to touch it. The envelope sat between them, slightly bowed from humidity, and she thought of what it cost to mail something this size, the clerk weighing it, her own hand on the counter waiting for change.

"I'll get the bedroom," she said.

Their mother's bedroom still had the crocheted bedspread, ivory gone to the color of old piano keys. Patricia sat on the edge and the springs made their familiar complaint. The closet was half-empty already—Brian had done something in there last month, she didn't ask what—and the remaining clothes were the ones their mother had worn after, the elastic-waist pants and buttonless cardigans, the wardrobe of someone who needed help dressing.

Patricia reached back and opened the nightstand drawer. Tissue box, empty. Hand cream, still sealed. A hair elastic, stretched beyond use. At the back, a folded paper. She knew what this was too, because she'd found it last time, read it, folded it back.

She left it there. Closed the drawer.

Brian was in the hallway, carrying a box. "Basement next?"

"If you want."

"I don't want."

"There's nothing down there but Christmas."

"The fake tree," he said. "We can put it on the curb."

"The stand too?"

"There a stand?"

"Metal. Red base." Patricia stood from the bed, smoothed the spread where she'd sat. Already the gesture of someone leaving, keeping up appearances. "Last year it was in the corner. Behind the dehumidifier."

Brian shifted the box. Cardboard cut into his forearm, leaving a crease. "You remember that?"

"I remember."

Neither moved. The hallway was narrow, doors on either side, bathroom at the end with the pink tile their father had never gotten to renovating. Patricia thought of the hair elastic in the drawer, how their mother's hair had thinned to a color between gray and white, how she'd worn it back in the photos Patricia had once sent and then tried to send again, how you could hold something too long until it became a different object entirely.

"I'll get the basement," Brian said.

"I'll finish here."

He nodded, went toward the stairs. She heard him descend, the fifth step creaking, his weight on it, the sound of someone navigating a house by its particular damages.

Patricia went back to the nightstand. Opened the drawer. The folded paper was a letter, three pages, their mother's handwriting, the script loosed and wandering at the edges—a first draft, she'd realized last time, never sent, never meant to be found. The date at the top: two months before the diagnosis, though perhaps their mother had known, perhaps that was what these pages contained, a knowledge that moved through them like weather through a house, warping the doors.

She refolded it. Replaced it. Closed the drawer and opened the closet instead, took down a cardigan still smelling of department store, the tags never removed, the size wrong, something Patricia had given her one birthday when she'd stopped knowing what to give.

She sat back on the bed, cardigan in her lap, and listened to her brother in the basement, the drag of the tree against concrete, something heavy being moved, the sound of someone working without help because help had never been asked for, only accepted when offered, and not always then.

The gravy boat was Doris's. She would take it tomorrow, driving up from Champaign, and she would not ask about the letter, the envelope, the cat toy on the windowsill. She would take her dish and make her calls and report back to the others: Brian seemed fine, Patricia seemed fine, the house was emptying nicely.

The water stain darkened in the corner where Brian had pointed. Patricia watched it, waiting to see it move.
