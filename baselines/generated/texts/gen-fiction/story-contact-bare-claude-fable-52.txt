The men from the government came on a Tuesday, while I was regrouting the bathroom.

There were three of them, plus a woman from the observatory at Green Bank who did most of the talking. She asked if I was Odile Marsh, formerly Odile Tran, formerly of 14 Kestrel Road, Duluth. She asked if my father had held an amateur radio license. I said yes to all of it, grout drying on my knuckles, and asked whether he'd died owing somebody money.

"No, ma'am," she said. "Did you ever use his equipment yourself? Say, the summer of 1986?"

I had, once. I was eleven, and forbidden to touch the rig, which sat in the garage under a bedsheet like a piece of furniture in a closed-up house. One night in July, when my parents were asleep, I went out in my nightgown and switched it on. I knew the procedure from watching. You said who you were. You said "over" when you were done, and then you waited, because my father said "over" didn't just mean I'm finished. It meant I'm finished, and I'm listening.

So I pressed the key and said: "Hello. Is anybody out there? This is Odile. I'm eleven. Over."

Static answered. I waited until my feet got cold, and then I went to bed, and by the end of the summer I'd forgotten I was ever the kind of child who did things like that.

The woman from Green Bank put a tablet on my kitchen table and pressed play.

There was a hiss, and then a voice. It was a little girl's voice, thin and serious, with a Minnesota vowel in it that I'd spent my twenties sanding off.

"Hello, Odile. This is anybody out there. Over."

It played four times before I could ask her to stop.

"It's on a loop," she said. "It's been arriving for nine days, from a star a little under twenty light-years off. They're using your recording because it's all they have. You gave them eleven words and they built a sentence out of them."

"Ten," I said. "I said 'is' twice." I don't know why that was what came out. My hands were shaking, and there was grout on the tablet.

They flew me to West Virginia. I had assumed, the whole flight, that I was a curiosity, a footnote they wanted to interview before the real work began. It took two days of meetings before I understood the problem they were actually having.

A reply had been drafted. It had been through the UN, through linguists, through mathematicians who had wanted to send primes and been overruled. It was four hundred words long, and every one of them had been fought over. It mentioned peace eleven times.

"The trouble," said a tired man named Abernathy, "is that they didn't answer Earth. We've been shouting at the sky for a century. Television, radar, deliberate messages with diagrams. They ignored all of it, or couldn't parse it. They answered one transmission. Yours had a name and an invitation to respond, and it stopped." He rubbed his eyes. "Our best guess is that 'over' is the only part of anything we've ever sent that they understood. You talked, and then you gave them a turn."

"So you want me to read your statement."

"We want your voice. They'll recognize it. Well, they'll recognize something. You've aged."

"Thank you."

"I mean that it may matter to them. That time passes for us. That's information."

They put me in a booth lined with gray foam. The statement was on a stand in front of me. Through the glass I could see forty people pretending not to stare, and above them, on a monitor, the big dish tilted toward a patch of sky that looked like any other patch of sky.

I read the first line. On behalf of the peoples of Earth. It was a good line, and it wasn't mine.

I thought about the garage. I thought about how long I'd stood there with my finger off the key, and how the silence hadn't felt empty, exactly. It had felt like a held breath. I thought about someone under a different sun catching ten words out of the dark and turning them over for however long it took, until they could hand them back in an order that meant yes.

And I thought about arithmetic. Twenty years out, and twenty back. I would be ninety-one, if I was anything.

Abernathy's voice came over the intercom. "Whenever you're ready, Ms. Marsh."

I moved the statement off the stand. I saw him see me do it. He didn't stop me, though, and I've thought about that often since.

I pressed the key.

"Hello, anybody. This is Odile. I'm fifty-one now. It took a while, but I heard you." My voice cracked, and I let it. That was information too. "There are a lot of us out here. Some of us are eleven. If I'm not here when you answer, one of them will be."

I took a breath.

"Over."
