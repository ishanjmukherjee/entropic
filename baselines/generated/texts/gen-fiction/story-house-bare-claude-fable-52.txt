The key still stuck in the lock the way it always had, and Dana had to lift the door by its handle to get it open. Behind her, Neil carried a roll of garbage bags and a box of donuts, as if they were coming to help someone move.

"It smells the same," he said.

It did. Lemon cleaner, and under it the faint mustiness of a house where the windows had been painted shut since 1994.

They started in the kitchen because it seemed the least dangerous. Dana took the cabinets and Neil took the drawers, and for an hour they worked without saying much. Their mother had owned eleven wooden spoons. She had kept every twist tie that ever entered the house, sorted by color in a margarine tub.

"Why," Neil said, holding it up.

"In case."

"In case of what?"

"She never said. Just in case."

He put the tub in the garbage bag, then took it out again and set it on the counter. Dana pretended not to notice.

The freezer was worse. There were foil packets labeled in their mother's slanted capitals: SOUP MARCH. SOUP (GOOD). BREAD NEIL LIKES. Neil stood with the door open and the cold rolling out over his shoes.

"I told her I liked that bread once," he said. "Maybe 2009."

"She made it every time you came."

"I know. I don't even like it that much." He laughed, and then he wasn't laughing, and Dana reached past him and shut the freezer door.

"Leave it for tomorrow," she said.

They moved to the living room. The estate woman had told them to sort everything into three piles, keep, donate and discard, and by noon the discard pile was a single lampshade. The keep pile had spread across the sofa.

"We can't keep all of it," Dana said.

"I know."

"You have a one-bedroom apartment."

"I know that, Dana."

She picked up a ceramic owl from the mantel. It was ugly, a brown lumpish thing with one eye larger than the other. She turned it over. On the bottom, scratched into the clay, was her own name and the number 7.

"I made this," she said. "I'd completely forgotten."

"It's hideous."

"It's been on the mantel for thirty-five years." She set it on the keep pile and didn't look at him.

In the afternoon they did the bedroom, which they had both been avoiding. The bed was made. Her reading glasses were folded on the nightstand on top of a library book, three weeks overdue now, a bookmark holding her place at page 212.

Neil picked the book up. "Was it any good, do you think?"

"She'd have finished it either way. She finished everything."

"She finished that thousand-page thing about the Dutch tulips."

"And complained the whole time."

He put the book in his jacket pocket. "I'll take it back. Pay the fine."

The closet held her coats, her good shoes still in their boxes, and on the top shelf a hatbox that neither of them had seen before. Dana lifted it down and they sat on the edge of the bed with it between them.

There were no hats in it. There were report cards, a hospital bracelet so small it would not have fit around Dana's thumb, and a bundle of letters in their father's handwriting. Beneath those was a sheet of lined paper folded in quarters.

Dana unfolded it. It was a list, in pencil, in a child's laborious print. REASONS I AM RUNNING AWAY. 1. Dana gets the big room. 2. Nobody listens to me. 3. Meat loaf.

"Oh, no," Neil said.

"You were six. You got as far as the Hendersons' hedge."

"I had a sandwich with me. I remember the sandwich." He took the paper from her. At the bottom, in their mother's hand, was a note in blue ink: He came back at 5:40. I watched him from the window the whole time.

Neither of them said anything. Outside a car went by, and somewhere down the block a dog was barking at it.

"You did get the big room," Neil said finally.

"I'm older."

"By two years. That isn't a qualification."

"You can have it now," she said, and he laughed, a real one this time, and wiped his face with the back of his wrist.

They stayed until the light went amber in the front windows. They had not done half of what they'd planned. The donuts were gone, and the piles were all still the wrong sizes. Dana carried the owl out to her car and belted it into the passenger seat, feeling ridiculous.

Neil locked up. He had to lift the door by its handle to get the bolt to catch.

"Same time tomorrow?" he asked.

"I'll bring lunch."

"Not meat loaf."

"No," she said. "I'd never."

He stood on the porch a moment longer, the margarine tub under his arm, and then he came down the steps.
