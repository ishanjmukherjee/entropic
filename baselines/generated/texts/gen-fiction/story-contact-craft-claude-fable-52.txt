"That one sticks," Lorraine Sadowski said, and put her hip against the side door of the garage until it gave.

Ruth Okafor went in first. Glenn Pruitt stayed on the gravel with the flattened boxes under his arm, reading his phone.

It was a one-car garage with no car in it. Pegboard, a chest freezer, a workbench made from a hollow-core door laid across two filing cabinets. On the bench sat a Yaesu transceiver with a dish towel over it, a straight key screwed to a square of plywood, and a desk lamp with a clothespin holding the shade on. Coaxial cable ran up through a hole in the ceiling that had been sealed with what looked like bathtub caulk.

"He'd have hated you seeing it like this," Lorraine said. "He kept it nicer."

"It's fine," Ruth said. "It's very orderly."

"The nephew wants the truck. I told him, you come get it, then. He's in Dayton. You'd think Dayton was the moon."

Glenn came in sideways with the boxes. He looked at the ceiling, then at his watch. "Mrs. Sadowski, we'll give you an itemized receipt for everything. Serial numbers. You'll have a number to call."

"I have a number to call for the gas company too."

"Yes, ma'am."

"There's coffee cake. It's the Entenmann's, I didn't make it."

"We ate on the road," Glenn said.

Ruth lifted the dish towel. The dial was set to 1296.100. She'd known it would be. She had seen that number every day for five weeks, on screens in a room in West Virginia with no windows, next to a call sign that belonged to a retired pipefitter in Ross County and the same eleven characters arriving every fourth night, patient as a porch light. They had answered it from a three-hundred-foot dish. They had answered it in primes, in hydrogen, in Walt Sadowski's own fist reconstructed from an old contest recording. It took no notice of any of it and kept calling for him.

"Did he ever talk to you about what he did out here?" she said.

"He bounced it off the moon. He told everybody that. He told the man who did our gutters." Lorraine pulled a lawn chair from behind the freezer and unfolded it but didn't sit. "You people want the bookshelf? Because I can't lift it."

"Just the notebooks."

The notebooks were Mead composition books, the marbled kind, shelved spine out with the years on masking tape. 2004 through this one. Glenn set a box down and started taping the bottom. The tape gun shrieked.

"My daughter's getting married Saturday," he said, to nobody. "In Tucson."

"That's a dry heat," Lorraine said.

Ruth took down 2019. Date, time in UTC, elevation of the moon, then two columns, SENT and RCVD, in a draftsman's block capitals, the sevens crossed. The RCVD column was groups of characters she could have recited. The SENT column she had never seen and nobody had. She did not read it. She turned pages by their corners and let her eyes go soft, like looking at a stranger's mail.

"He had the stroke in March," Lorraine said. "Two years this March. He was at the Kroger. They were very good, the Kroger."

"I'm sorry."

"Eleven days at Adena and he never did wake up."

Ruth put 2019 in the box and took down the last one. March. The block capitals ran to the ninth and stopped a third of the way down the page. Below that was a blank line. Below the blank line the entries went on, every fourth night, date and time and the moon's elevation, in blue ballpoint, in a round upright cursive with closed loops, the kind they used to teach with a workbook. They went on through that spring and the next. The last was dated Sunday.

The SENT column was full.

She closed the book. On the bench the key's black knob had a pale crescent worn into it, and next to the key was a pair of drugstore reading glasses, the kind sold with a beaded chain, and a mug with a tea bag dried to the bottom.

"Is this one current?" Glenn said. He was holding the box open.

"That's the lot," Ruth said, and laid it in on top.

Lorraine was looking at the lawn chair. "You'll want the antenna too, I suppose. It's bolted through. He used the lag screws, he didn't trust the others."

"We'd need a crew for that," Ruth said. "A lift. It wouldn't be this week."

"It could be next week," Glenn said. "I can get them here Monday."

"Thursday at the soonest," Ruth said. "With the holiday."

He looked at her. He picked up the box.

In the driveway he loaded the trunk and got in and started the car and sat with his phone lit against the wheel. Ruth stood at the passenger door with the receipt book. Lorraine had not come out. Inside the garage the tape gun was still on the bench where he'd left it, and the dish towel was back over the radio, squared at the corners. Ruth wrote 1 transceiver, to follow and tore the pink copy off and went back up the gravel to wedge it in the door.
