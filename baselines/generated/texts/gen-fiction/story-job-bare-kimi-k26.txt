The elevator chimed on the seventeenth floor, and Maya's reflection in the chrome doors looked like someone else entirely. Someone in navy blazer and pressed trousers, someone with an actual leather portfolio instead of the spiral notebook she'd carried through four years of college. She checked her phone: 8:47. Thirteen minutes early. Too early. She'd circle the floor, she decided, find the restroom, check her teeth for lipstick.

The restroom smelled of lavender and something chemical, and the mirror confirmed her worst suspicion: she looked terrified. She practiced smiling, which made it worse. Someone entered, an older woman with silver hair swept into a practical bun, and Maya fled to the hallway.

She found the glass doors of Hartwell & Associates easily enough. The reception area stretched before her like a museum exhibit of corporate achievement—clean lines, abstract art, a single orchid on the desk that looked too perfect to be real. No one sat at the reception desk.

Maya hovered. She'd arrived second in her class at Michigan Law, she'd survived twenty-seven interviews, seventeen rejections, and one brutal bar exam. She could handle an empty reception desk.

She checked her phone again. 8:52. The glass doors whispered open behind her.

"Christ, I need coffee." The man who entered was tall, gray-haired, his suit obviously expensive and slightly rumpled. He held a briefcase in one hand and a bicycle helmet in the other. He stopped when he noticed Maya. "You're new."

"Yes. Maya Chen. Starting today." She extended her hand.

He switched the helmet to his other arm and shook. "Gerald Hartwell. Welcome to the asylum, Maya. You're early."

"I wanted to—"

"Don't explain. Never explain." He dropped the helmet on the reception desk and opened a door behind it, revealing a small kitchen. "Coffee? I make terrible coffee. It's my hallmark."

"Thank you, yes."

He emerged with two styrofoam cups, pressing one into her hand. It tasted burned and faintly metallic, exactly as promised. "Seventh floor conference room, nine sharp. Orientation." He drank deeply, apparently unaffected. "Don't look so frightened, Maya. I was frightened once. 1987. Walked in terrified, walked out with a job I hated and a wife I loved." He smiled, something sad at the edges. "She's gone now. The wife and the job." He picked up his helmet. "The terror's optional, I've found. You can put it down whenever you like."

Before she could respond, he disappeared through a side door marked STAIRS, coffee still in hand.

At 8:58, Maya found the seventh floor conference room. Twelve other first-years sat around a mahogany table, nearly identical in their careful professionalism. A woman at the head—sharp-featured, red glasses—distributed folders.

"Sit," she said, not looking up. "I'm Patricia Okonkwo, professional development. These next six hours will cover billing procedures, firm culture, sexual harassment protocol, and why you should never, under any circumstances, use the client elevators."

Maya sat between a young man who smelled aggressively of peppermint and a woman who immediately opened a laptop and began typing. The chair was ergonomically designed, expensive, slightly uncomfortable.

By hour three, Maya understood Hartwell's coffee. The break room offerings were worse, some kind of hazelnut-flavored punishment that coated her tongue. She stood by the window with her second cup, watching actual clouds drift past the building. Someone had written the wi-fi password on a post-it: HARTWELL1987.

In hour four, they covered fire exits. Hour five, email etiquette. The peppermint man raised his hand to ask about billable hour benchmarks. The laptop woman asked about parental leave policies, though she looked approximately twenty-three.

At 12:47, running seventeen minutes behind schedule, Patricia Okonkwo finally mentioned the actual law.

"You'll be assigned to practice groups next week," she said. "For now, familiarize yourself with the firm management system. Your login credentials are in your folders." She paused, adjusted her red glasses. "Hartwell & Associates is not a warm environment. It is not a family. It is a machine for converting intelligence and time into excellent legal work. The sooner you understand this, the more successful you'll be."

The peppermint man nodded earnestly. Laptop woman typed something.

But Maya thought of Gerald Hartwell's bicycle helmet, his terrible coffee, his optional terror. She thought of the orchid in the reception area, which she'd finally decided was real because of the single drooping petal on its lowest bloom, imperfect, alive.

She raised her hand.

"Yes?"

Maya hadn't planned the question. "What happens to the orchid when it dies?"

Silence. Patricia blinked.

"Excuse me?"

"The reception orchid. When it finishes blooming. Does someone replace it?"

Patricia's mouth compressed. "Facilities handles plant maintenance. Any questions relevant to your employment?"

Maya thought of not asking. Of folding herself into the careful shape the room seemed to require. But outside, the clouds kept moving, indifferent to seventeen floors of ambition.

"No," she said. "That was all."

Afterward, the peppermint man—David, she'd learned—cut her off in the hallway. "That was bold," he said, not approvingly. "The orchid thing."

"Was it?"

"You don't want to be memorable on the first day. You want to be competent. Invisible until proven otherwise."

"Thank you for the advice."

He nodded, satisfied, and turned toward what Maya assumed were the associate elevators, the ones non-clients used, the approved paths.

She found the stairs instead. Seventeen flights down, she counted, her new leather portfolio growing heavy, her blazer warming with the effort. At the landing between twelve and eleven, she stopped. A window looked out on an airshaft, nothing but brick and a sliver of gray sky. Someone had written on the wall in black marker: You are here.

Maya laughed, surprising herself. The sound echoed, unprofessional, unmeasured. She descended the final flights differently, lighter, leaving something on the stairs behind her without quite knowing what it was.

The lobby opened onto sunlight, afternoon heat, the city moving in its endless patterns. She had eleven hours before she needed to return. Enough time for a long walk, for dinner alone, for remembering that she was more than this building, this day, this careful costume.

At the revolving door, Gerald Hartwell caught up to her, helmet in hand, apparently having circled the building on his bicycle, having burned off his terrible coffee.

"Survived," he observed.

"Barely."

"Tomorrow's worse. They assign partners. Mine's a monster, technically brilliant, emotionally catastrophic." He held the door for her. "What's yours?"

"I don't know yet."

"Ah." They stepped into the afternoon together, the sidewalk crowded, the traffic loud. "The terror," he reminded her.

"I put it down," she said, and found it was true, or true enough.

He smiled, the sad edges still there but something else too, recognition perhaps, or simply the acknowledgment that survival continued, that first days led inevitably to second days, that orchids bloomed and drooped and were somehow replaced, the machine continuing, imperfect, alive.

"Good," he said. "See you tomorrow, Maya Chen."
