The kitchen was the worst, so they saved it for last.

Nora had wanted to start there, to get it over with, but Daniel said no. He said if they started with the kitchen they'd never finish anything else, and he was right, which annoyed her. He was usually right about things like that, the practical sequencing of grief. He'd been the one to call the funeral home. He'd been the one to find the will in the shoebox under the bed, labeled in their mother's square capitals: IMPORTANT. DON'T THROW OUT.

So they did the bedroom first, then the closets, then the garage, which took two full days because their mother had kept every jar she had ever emptied. Pickle jars, jam jars, a single jar of maraschino cherries from what Daniel estimated was 1994. They filled the recycling bin four times.

"She was going to use them for something," Nora said.

"For what?"

"Something. She always said she was going to use them for something."

Daniel held one up to the light. A dead moth had made its home inside. "Well," he said. "She did."

Nora laughed, and then she wasn't laughing, and Daniel set the jar down and waited without saying anything, which was another thing he was good at.

By Saturday there was only the kitchen. They stood in the doorway with their coffee in paper cups, because the mugs were already packed, and looked at it. The yellow curtains. The calendar from the hardware store, still on October. The radio on the windowsill that only got one station, the one that played old country songs and farm reports their mother had never once needed.

"Okay," Daniel said.

"Okay," Nora said.

They didn't move.

The drawers were easy enough. Twist ties, rubber bands, birthday candles in a sandwich bag, eleven pens and none of them working. Batteries of uncertain charge. A coupon for detergent that had expired during the Obama administration. Daniel sorted with brutal efficiency and Nora let him, because she kept stopping. She found the potato peeler their mother had used for forty years, the one with the wooden handle worn pale where her thumb went, and she couldn't put it in any of the boxes. Not Keep, not Donate, not Trash. She put it in her coat pocket.

Daniel saw. He didn't say anything about that either.

The cabinet above the refrigerator was the one neither of them could reach without the step stool, which was how their mother had liked it. Daniel climbed up and handed things down: a fondue set, still in its box. A punch bowl. A stack of Tupperware lids with no Tupperware.

Then he went quiet.

"What," Nora said.

He came down holding a recipe box. Tin, painted with faded strawberries, the hinge rusted. Nora knew it immediately. She had not seen it in twenty years.

"I thought she lost this," she said. "When we moved. She said it got lost in the move."

"I guess it didn't."

They sat at the kitchen table, at their old places. Nora on the left, Daniel by the window. The chairs were exactly as uncomfortable as they remembered. Daniel opened the box.

The cards were soft with handling, stained with butter and vanilla and things neither of them could identify. Their mother's handwriting, but younger, looser, before the arthritis made her letters so careful. Pot roast. Lemon bars. Grandma Ruth's rolls, with a note at the bottom: don't tell Ruth I changed it.

"She changed Grandma's rolls," Daniel said. "That's the scandal of the century."

Further back, the cards changed. They weren't recipes anymore, or not quite. One said Nora's birthday cake, 6th, and underneath, in different ink: too much frosting, she cried, use less next year. Another said Danny's soup for when he's sick and then a list of ingredients and then, at the bottom, he likes it when I sit with him while he eats.

Daniel put that one down very carefully on the table, face up, and looked out the window for a while.

There were dozens. A card for the casserole she'd made when their father left, with nobody ate this written across it. A card for the cookies Nora had brought to her first day of high school. She said they were embarrassing. She ate three on the way home. A card that said only Thanksgiving 2019, both kids home, and under it, nothing at all, just the date, as though that was the whole recipe.

"She didn't lose it," Nora said. "She just didn't want us to read it."

"Or she wanted us to read it later."

"Is there a difference?"

Daniel thought about it. "Probably not to her."

Outside, the afternoon was going orange. The radio was off but Nora could almost hear it, a fiddle somewhere, a man reporting the price of soybeans to no one.

Daniel slid the soup card back into the box, in its right place, and closed the lid.

"Keep," he said.

"Keep," Nora agreed.

They finished the kitchen the next morning. It took less time than either of them expected.
