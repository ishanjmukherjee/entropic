The copier jammed on page three, and Ramona decided this was a message from God, though she couldn't have said of what.

She'd been at Hessler & Brunt for forty minutes. Nobody had shown her the copier. Nobody had shown her anything, really. A woman named Deb had pointed at a desk near the window and said, "That's yours, probably," and then walked off with a manila folder pressed to her chest like a hot dish coming out of a microwave.

"Jiggler," said a voice behind her. "The little green one. You have to kind of talk to it."

Ramona turned. A young man stood there holding two paper coffee cups, one in each hand, as if he'd forgotten which was his.

"I'm Kevin," he said. "Mailroom, technically, but also I fix things when Deb breaks them."

"I haven't broken anything."

"Page three," he said. "It eats page three. It's a known issue." He set down one of the coffees on the copier, knelt, opened a panel she hadn't known was a panel, and extracted a crumpled sheet with the tenderness of someone pulling a splinter. "There. You'd have gotten it eventually."

He left the coffee. She didn't ask. She drank it at the desk that was probably hers, and it was good, and she spent the morning opening email accounts and waiting.

At eleven-fifteen, Deb came back without the folder and stood at the edge of Ramona's desk, not sitting on the corner of it, a courtesy Ramona registered dimly as Significant.

"Marcus wanted you to sit in on the Williamson call," Deb said. "But Marcus is at the dentist, so it's off. Unless—no, it's off."

"Okay."

"You came from Delcore?"

"Six years."

Deb nodded slowly, the way people nod at x-rays. "They still in the Brownlee building?"

"Moved to Mohawk Street in 2020."

"Right," Deb said, though her face said she had already known this and had been checking for something else. She looked at the window, the parking lot below, a gray Taurus pulling in. "Anyway. Lunch is at the deli or the gas station. The gas station has better egg salad but you have to get there before the ambulance guys."

Then she was gone again.

Ramona worked at Delcore for six years and on the last day hadn't cried, though her coworker Priya had cried enough for both of them, standing by the ficus with her lanyard still on. Ramona had trained Priya for a job Priya was already better at. That was the whole story of Ramona's six years, if she was honest, and she mostly wasn't.

At twelve-forty she walked to the gas station. The ambulance guys were already there, two of them at the counter arguing about softball in a low, exhausted way, and she took the last egg salad. One of them looked at her tray and then at the clock and sighed, deeply, a sigh from somewhere under his boots.

She ate in her car with the windows cracked. Through the windshield she could see the fourth floor of Hessler & Brunt, her own window, and Deb standing at it, not looking down, just standing. Holding a coffee. Kevin's other coffee, maybe. It was impossible to say from here whether Deb was looking at anything at all.

Ramona had a daughter in Akron who turned nine on Saturday. Her ex-husband, Doug, had the weekend and was taking Misty to a water park hotel, tickets already purchased, nonrefundable, a fact he'd delivered with the relish of a man reading out lottery numbers. Ramona had asked for Sunday instead and gotten Sunday, and the person arranging the schedule at Hessler & Brunt had said, "You just started, so," and Ramona had said, "I understand," and that had been that.

Back at her desk she found a yellow sticky note stuck to her monitor: Marcus—dentist—reschedule Williamson. —D. Under it, a smaller note, different handwriting, blue pen: Jiggler trick works on the printer by the kitchen too. —K.

She did the Williamson reschedule first, found Marcus's calendar riddled with gaps, and slotted the call into one of his Friday holes. It took four minutes. Then she pulled up the file Deb had been holding to her chest all morning—not hard to find, the folder name was WILLIAMSON/BELL, and it had been sitting, unshared, in the drive since March, waiting for somebody.

She read it for an hour. It wasn't complicated. It was complicated only if you'd been at the dentist, or holding it to your chest.

At four-thirty Deb appeared again, and this time she did sit on the corner of the desk, just a little, just her hip.

"You didn't have to do that," Deb said.

"It was four minutes."

"The March part?" Deb was looking at her hands. "Parts of the March part are—I had reasons."

"Okay," Ramona said.

Deb waited, as if Okay were a door she was waiting to see opened. It wasn't. It was just Okay. Ramona picked up her pen and clicked it once, and Deb stood up.

"The egg salad," Deb said, at the edge of the cubicle. "You beat the paramedics."

"There were two left when I got there."

"No. There was one." Deb almost smiled, or did something in the neighborhood of smiling. "I get there at twelve-fifteen. I've gotten there at twelve-fifteen for four years."

In the parking lot, Ramona sat in her car a while before starting it. The Taurus was leaving. Up on the fourth floor a light was on in her window—that was strange, to think of it that way, my window—though she'd turned her lamp off. Someone had turned it on, or it ran on a sensor, or it had never been off.

She drove to the phone store on Route 9 and bought a small hands-free mount for her car, the kind you can call from driving, and she called Misty, who answered on the fourth ring mid-chew.

"Guess what," Misty said.

"What."

"Dad says the water park has a lazy river AND a wave pool. Guess which one I'm doing first."

Which one, Ramona said. Guess. She missed the exit for her street and drove straight through the light, taking the long way, and Misty guessed with her, all the way home.
