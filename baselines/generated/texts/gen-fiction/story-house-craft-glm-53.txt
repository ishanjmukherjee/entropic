Doris couldn't find the box cutter, so Karen was slicing the packing tape with a paring knife she'd found in the kitchen drawer, the one with the cracked handle, and both of them were pretending this was efficient.

"Linens," Karen said, peeling back the flaps. "More linens."

"Mark it Goodwill."

"That's what I did with the last two."

The house had the smell of a house nobody had cooked in for a while. Doris had opened the back door a crack but the screen was latched and she hadn't gone to unlatch it.

They'd been at it since nine. It was now after one. Neither had suggested lunch.

In the hall closet they found the galoshes, still paired, the rubber gone gray and stiff. In the linen closet, a Hotel Barrie bathrobe, tags still on, fugitive from some Christmas fifteen years ago. Doris held it up.

"She never went," Doris said.

"Went where?"

"Barrie. Aunt Fay kept saying she should."

Karen folded the robe into the Goodwill bag without taking it from Doris's hand directly. There was a small negotiation there—the robe passed, the hanger stayed behind—and both of them let the hanger stand against the wall.

Upstairs, the bedrooms went faster. Their old room was a sewing room now, had been for twenty years, and neither sister commented on this. They boxed patterns, a pincushion with a wrist strap, two liters of rub-on letters in a plastic bin, and Doris, kneeling at the nightstand, opened the drawer and closed it again.

"Coins?" Karen said from the doorway, her arms full of curtain rods.

"Reading glasses."

"Those can go to the Lions Club. They take glasses."

"I know."

"Okay."

Karen stood a while with the curtain rods. Then she said, "There's a guy from the church coming Thursday, for the organ. If we're done in here."

"Thursday's fine."

"He needs both staircases clear."

"Then we'll clear them."

Karen went down. Doris opened the drawer again. Under the glasses, a folded paper, and under the paper a photograph in one of those little card-frame folders from a portrait studio. She did not look at the photograph front. She looked at the back, where there was nothing. She held it a long moment, and then she put it in the pocket of her cardigan, which she then zipped, which was absurd, in June, and she knew it.

The attic took them through the afternoon. Karen went up first because Doris didn't trust the pull-down ladder and Karen knew she didn't and didn't say so. The heat up there was flat and physical, like being under a hand. Boxes of the children's things, labeled in their father's blocky capitals: KAREN, SCHOOL; DORIS, SCHOOL; XMAS DECOR. They opened XMAS DECOR because it was nearest, and it wasn't Christmas decorations. It was photographs, loose, hundreds of them, sleeved in nothing, curling, and the first one Doris picked up was of her own daughter at maybe four, on the shoulders of a man neither sister had seen in nineteen years. Flannel shirt. A lake. The child was gripping his hair and laughing.

"Who labeled this box?" Doris said.

Karen had gone quiet at the other end of the attic. Doris looked over. Karen was sitting on an old trunk, not opening it, a photograph in her own hand, and her face was doing something she had clearly decided on and was now executing, which was nothing. She put the photograph face-down in her lap.

"There's more film in the canister," Karen said. "Under the eave. She kept developing film right to the end, I found receipts. Six rolls at least that are just—you know. The last year."

"Where are the rolls?"

"I'm saying she took pictures right to the end. She was at the window with that camera. The nurse told me."

"That was Scope's Falls. She wasn't taking pictures."

"Well, the nurse thought she was."

They sat in the heat with the box between them. Doris put the lake photo back, loosely, wrong side up, so that the next person to open the box would meet it that way.

"Take what you want now," Karen said, "so I don't have to make decisions about your stuff later."

"There is no stuff of mine."

"There's a trunk."

"That's her trunk."

"It's got your name on it."

Doris didn't go look at the trunk. She went down the ladder instead, slowly, and ate two crackers standing at the counter, out of the sleeve, without a plate, and the crumbs went down her front. She heard Karen up there moving things, packing, doing the work, making the decisions she was good at.

At dusk the Goodwill boxes were on the porch and the church guy had texted about the organ, early Thursday, and Karen had found the box cutter in the car, of course. They stood by their separate cars.

"The glasses," Doris said. "For the Lions Club. I put them behind the seat, your side—no, the bench. Middle of the bench, in that tin."

"Got it."

"And the rub-on letters. Saint John's might want those for the rummage sale."

"Noted."

Karen unlocked her car and didn't get in. Across the yard, the screen door had come unlatched somehow—maybe the wind, maybe the boys who took the lawnmower—and it swung and knocked, swung and knocked, and neither sister went to latch it.

"I'll take the trunk," Doris said.

Karen nodded, once, watching the door.

"I'll come Saturday for it," Doris said. "For the trunk."

But she touched her cardigan pocket then, the flat shape of it, and Karen saw her do it and looked away, at the porch, at the boxes, at the unlatched door swinging, and got in her car.
