He had the logbook open on the oilcloth before she'd got the kettle down, and he was turning the pages from the back, the way you look for a name in a hymnal.

"Those are just the old ones," Marjorie said. "The current's on the shelf by the radio. I do it in ink."

"These are fine."

"You'll want the generator hours. I've them separate."

Glenn Fortier said he'd get to the generator. He had a clipboard from the Department of Transport with a form on it, eleven boxes, and he'd filled in two: the station name and the date, 14 May 1974. His shoes were wet through. He'd come across the bar on foot forty minutes ahead of the tide instead of waiting for Hebb's boat, and she had noticed that and not known where to put it.

She set out the tea and the tin of milk. She'd blackleaded the stove on Tuesday when the Department's notice came. She'd done the brass on the lens carriage, the vent ball, the handrail all the way up, ninety-two steps, and she'd painted the sill in the watch room where the rust had come through, though the paint was still tacky and she hoped he wouldn't lean there.

"Your husband kept these," he said.

"Till sixty-six. Then they let me carry on. There was a letter about it, I can find the letter."

"His hand's very small."

"He was saving the book."

He'd stopped turning. She knew the weight of the pages well enough to tell roughly where: late fifties. She poured for him, and he didn't take it up.

"Is the fog signal logged every time?"

"Every time. Hours and minutes. They checked the oil against it."

"Twenty-third October, fifty-eight. Nine hours."

"That'd be right for October."

"Wind southeast six. Light exhibited five forty-one." He read with his finger. "One to supper."

Marjorie turned the handle of his cup toward him. "We had people. The inspector. Hebb's father, if he got caught by the water."

"It doesn't say who."

"Well, Walter wouldn't."

"There was a boat lost off the ledge that night. The Carol Ann. Out of Port Greville."

"There were boats lost most years."

"One man. They found the boat in November, up by Advocate. Not him." He turned the page forward, then back. "My mother got eleven hundred dollars from the Fishermen's Relief. She kept the stub. I've been going through her things since March."

The kettle was ticking as it cooled. Marjorie looked at the clipboard, the nine empty boxes. Tank capacity. Condition of tower. Resident keeper required Y/N.

"I'll tell you what I remember about that October," she said. "I wound the signal myself, one night. It's a weight on a chain, you'd to crank it every two hours, and Walter always did it, he said it'd have my shoulder out. But he had the truck gone till morning. Clear to Amherst and back, on those roads."

"What's in Amherst?"

"The bus."

He sat with his hands flat on either side of the book. He was perhaps thirty. He had a razor nick under his jaw, and one collar point was turned under.

"He never said who he'd driven?"

"He said he'd had to wait for the tide. That was all he said, and I had a sore shoulder, I wasn't asking." She got up and moved the milk tin for no reason. "There was a dory came in on the shingle about then. Nobody claimed it. We used it for the pots, years. It's turned over behind the oil shed, if it's any use for your form."

He went out without his jacket. From the window over the sink she could see him go around the shed and crouch where the dory lay in the dock weed, grey, the garboard sprung. He put his hand on the bow. Walter had painted the bow out in station white the first spring, two coats, and it had never quite covered; in a low light you could make out that there'd been letters. He stayed there with his hand on it. Then he pulled a dock leaf, and another, and laid them down beside him, like a man weeding.

She washed her cup. She washed his, though he hadn't drunk from it, and then was annoyed and filled it again from the pot.

When he came in the knees of his trousers were dark. He sat, uncapped his pen, and drew the clipboard over the top of the logbook.

"Tank capacity," he said.

"Two hundred gallons. It was filled in April."

He wrote it in. "Steps?"

"Ninety-two. I did the rail."

"I saw that."

"You could go up. The sill's wet, in the watch room, mind your sleeve."

"I've the tide," he said. He looked at the next box for some time, the pen not touching the paper. "Does the truck still run?"

"It's Hebb's now. He'd take you. He'd want two dollars."

"To Amherst?"

"He's never been asked," Marjorie said, and went to find the letter.
