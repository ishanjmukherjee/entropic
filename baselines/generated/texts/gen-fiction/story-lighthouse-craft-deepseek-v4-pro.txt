The postcard was between two bills when she fanned the mail across the counter, and Carl took it to the window without a word. The picture on the front was of a cow, a prize Holstein with a blue ribbon hanging around its neck. He turned it over.

Milk futures down. Bred three heifers. Thought you’d want to know. —Margaret.

The last word of the message was “know,” but she had signed it, which was how she left a door unlocked. Carl slid the card into the breast pocket of his workshirt and buttoned the flap.

“Going up,” he said.

Donna looked at the magnet on the refrigerator, a clip shaped like a lobster she had bought in Portland on a day trip with a man she no longer mentioned. “There’s a roast for later.”

“I’ll eat when I’m down.”

The spiral stair was iron, bolted into the granite, and it sang a little under his boots. Each landing had a slit window, and at the third he paused and put his hand inside his shirt, feeling the card’s corner press his sternum. The cow on the front had the dumb, placid look of an animal that doesn’t know its weight is being calculated in dollars. Margaret had written “bred” like it was a weather report, like she had not once driven six hours to sit in this kitchen and tell Donna that the lighthouse life was no life at all for a woman with a degree. Donna had agreed, the two of them forming a brief, terrifying alliance over coffee, before Margaret got back in the car and returned to a man Carl had never met, a dairy farmer named Ken whose name Carl could not say without a pause before it.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and the faint, sweet tang of kerosene that never quite aired out. He did the checks in order: fuel level, wick trim, glass clarity. The sun was still high enough that the beam was idle, the great Fresnel lens a sleeping eye. He took the postcard out and set it on the sill, cow-side up.

A boat was crossing the channel below, a lobster boat by the look of the stack and the way it sat low in the stern. Carl lifted the binoculars. It was the Miss Emily, Frank Souza’s boat. Frank was at the wheel, and beside him, braced against the gunnel, was a woman in a yellow slicker. Her hair was dark and cut short, and she was pointing at something in the water. Carl watched her arm rise and fall. Frank laughed, the sound lost to the glass but visible in the hinge of his jaw. The woman turned her head to look up at the lighthouse, and Carl saw that she was young, younger than Frank by a decade at least, and that her face was familiar in the way that all new faces on the water became familiar after a while—a person you’d nod to at the fuel dock, a niece up for the summer, someone’s someone.

He lowered the binoculars. The Miss Emily throttled down and drifted toward the harbor. Carl watched it shrink until the woman’s yellow coat was a dot, then nothing.

He checked the barometer. Tapped it twice. The needle held steady, but his left knee ached in the way it had since a fall on the rocks in ’91, and the ache was usually a day ahead of a pressure drop.

The postcard he left on the sill. At dusk he lit the lamp, the mantle blooming into its steady, hissing glow, and the lens began its slow revolution, the weight-driven clockwork ticking in the floor below. He ate the roast cold at nine, standing at the counter, and Donna had gone to bed without saying goodnight.

At eleven the phone rang. It was the Coast Guard station in Rockland, a petty officer whose voice Carl didn’t recognize. A mayday had been relayed from a recreational vessel taking on water somewhere east of Metinic. They were asking all stations to keep a watch. Carl said he would, and hung up, and stood for a moment with his hand on the receiver before he climbed back up to the lamp room.

The fog came in at two in the morning, a wet, white nothing that pressed against the glass. Carl turned on the foghorn. Its note was a single low blast every thirty seconds, and each one vibrated up through the soles of his feet. He scanned the water with the light, the beam swinging out and returning, out and returning, cutting nothing but cotton.

He thought about the mayday. He thought about the woman in the yellow slicker, whether she was sleeping in Frank Souza’s house or in a guest room with a quilt pulled up to her chin. He thought about Margaret, and the word “bred,” and the way Donna had looked at the magnet shaped like a lobster as though it were a receipt she’d found in someone else’s coat.

At four-fifteen the fog lifted just enough to show a mile of black water, and on that water, a shape. Carl put the binoculars to his eyes. It was a cabin cruiser, dead in the water, listing badly to port. No lights. No movement on deck. He called it in, gave the coordinates, and the radio crackled back that a cutter was en route. He put the binoculars down and waited.

The cutter arrived in the grey hour before dawn. Carl watched its searchlight rake the cruiser’s cabin, its deck, the empty cockpit. After a while, a small figure in an orange vest emerged on the cutter’s deck and made a gesture he couldn’t read—a hand raised, then dropped. The cutter idled for a moment longer, and then the cruiser was left behind, still listing, drifting now with the tide, a thing not worth towing in until daylight.

Carl stayed at the glass until the light was full and the fog had burned off completely. The sea was flat and empty. The cruiser was a speck to the south. He turned off the lamp, trimmed the wick, wiped the soot from the chimney with a rag. His knee had stopped aching. He picked up the postcard from the sill, looked at the Holstein’s dumb face, and then tucked it back into his pocket.

Downstairs, Donna was pouring coffee. She wore her bathrobe, the blue one with the frayed cuffs, and she held the pot slightly away from the cup as she poured, as though it might splash.

“There’s a boat adrift out there,” Carl said.

“I heard the horn.”

“Coast Guard left it.”

Donna nodded at the pocket where the card made a white rectangle against the worn denim. “Anything you need to answer?”

Carl put his hand over the pocket. Felt the edge of the paper, the rounded corner where the die-cut had gone through. Through the window, the Miss Emily was heading out again, Frank alone at the wheel.

“Not yet,” he said.
