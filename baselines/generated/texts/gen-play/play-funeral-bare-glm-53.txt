A church. Two rows of folding chairs face the audience. At center, a closed casket flanked by lilies. FRANK, 52, sits alone in the back row, tie loosened, staring at the floor. JULIAN, 48, enters from the side, still wearing his coat. He hesitates, then sits one seat away from Frank. A long silence.

JULIAN: Empty church. Figured there'd be more people.

FRANK: People showed up earlier. Paid their respects. Left.

JULIAN: In and out. Like a gas station.

FRANK: (finally looking at him) Ten years, and that's your opener?

JULIAN: What do you want me to open with? "Sorry about Dad"? You think I'm not sorry about Dad?

FRANK: I don't know what you are. I stopped guessing in 2015.

(Silence. Julian takes off his coat, folds it carefully on his lap. Very deliberate. A man buying time.)

JULIAN: Is that what he wanted? A closed casket?

FRANK: He didn't want a casket at all. Wanted to be dumped in the ocean. No ceremony, no fuss. You'd know that if you'd called him. Even once.

JULIAN: I called.

FRANK: Christmas. Two-minute calls. I know, because I was there in the room, and he'd sit with the phone on his knee afterward like it was something that had died.

JULIAN: (sharp) Don't. Don't put that on me. He knew why I stayed away. He's the one who—

FRANK: He's the one who what? Raised us? Worked two jobs after Mom left? That monster?

JULIAN: You keep his story. Keep it. But you don't get to keep mine.

(Frank stands, paces, sits back down — closer now, one seat between them gone.)

FRANK: You know what he did the last month? Couldn't hold a spoon. I fed him. Every meal, I fed my father like a baby, and he'd look at me with this... this apology in his face. Like the indignity was his fault. Like he owed me something.

JULIAN: (quietly) He did owe you something. And you collected. You got the whole estate of him. Every Sunday dinner. Every—

FRANK: You think I wanted that?

JULIAN: I think you got to be the good son without ever once asking why I couldn't be.

FRANK: (standing, angry) Because you*chose* it! The money, the fight, walking out over—

JULIAN: Say it. Say the thing.

FRANK: The business. It was a cabinet shop, Julian. Wood and glue. And you burned it down over a promotion.

JULIAN: (stands, facing him) It wasn't a promotion. It was the succession. He was handing you the shop and I found out from the accountant. His own son, Frank. The middle son. Not important enough to hear it from his mouth. Thirty years I planed wood in that building, and I learn I'm irrelevant from a man who comes to measure the break room for new lockers.

FRANK: So torch it. That's a proportionate response.

JULIAN: I didn't torch it!

(Silence. Frank stares.)

FRANK: ...What?

JULIAN: The fire marshal's report. Electrical. Faulty wiring in the back wall. I got the letter three weeks after it burned, when I was already in Phoenix, and it didn't matter anymore, because you'd already decided, and Dad had already decided, so what was I going to do — mail it to you? Knock on your door with my exoneration in a manila envelope? (beat) I kept the letter. Ten years. I brought it on the plane. It's in my coat.

FRANK: You're telling me... he knew. You're telling me Dad died thinking you burned down his shop, and you had proof you didn't?

JULIAN: He died thinking I was capable of it. And so did you. That was worse. A letter doesn't fix that.

(Frank slowly sits. Julian does not. Frank puts his head in his hands.)

FRANK: He asked about you. At the end. Third-to-last day, not lucid, and he said, "Tell Jules the wiring—" And I thought he was confused. Dreaming about the fire. I told him it was okay. I shushed him. God, Julian. He was trying to tell me.

JULIAN: (sitting beside him, finally, no seat between them) He knew the whole time and wanted you to know. That's the kind of man he was. Terrible at the saying. Never failed at the meaning.

(Long silence. Frank exhales, a sound close to weeping but not.)

FRANK: There's a plot next to his. He bought three. Cheap in 1998.

JULIAN: Three.

FRANK: One for you. He never took your name off anything. Not the plot, not the shop's incorporation papers. You're still on the payroll accounts, Julian. Frozen, but there.

JULIAN: (a broken laugh) Ten years and I'm an heir.

FRANK: (standing, straightening his tie) The reception's at Doreen's. Chips and ham sandwiches, you know the spread. (beat) You should come. There's paperwork. But mostly... ham sandwiches.

(He starts to exit, stops.)

FRANK: You still doing the crossword every morning?

JULIAN: Every morning.

FRANK: He did it in pen. Right to the end.

(Frank exits. Julian looks at the casket a long moment, then at the folded coat in his hands. He reaches into the pocket, takes out an old envelope — doesn't open it — and lays it gently on the casket.)

JULIAN: Okay, Dad. Okay.

(He puts on his coat, follows his brother out. Lights fade on the casket, the lilies, the empty chair in the back row.)

END OF SCENE
