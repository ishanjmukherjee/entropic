Title: The Weight of Pine

Characters:
- LEO (early 40s) - Sharp, contained, successful.
- MARTIN (late 30s) - Weathered, quiet, carrying a slight limp.

Setting:
A small, sterile room just off the main chapel of a funeral home. Upstage, a large, frosted window lets in flat, grey light. The distant, muffled murmur of organ music and shuffling feet bleeds through the walls. Two identical, uncomfortable-looking armchairs are positioned facing away from each other. A small table holds a pitcher of water and two untouched glasses.

(At rise, LEO stands perfectly still, staring at the frosted window as if he can see through it. He wears an impeccably tailored black suit. A single duffel bag sits at his feet. The main chapel's murmur swells, then recedes. The door opens with a soft click. MARTIN enters. His dark jacket is too big, hanging off his frame. He freezes when he sees LEO. A long beat.)

MARTIN
(Slowly closing the door)
Didn’t know you’d be here.

LEO
(Not turning)
No one told you I was coming?

MARTIN
No one tells me much of anything. Haven’t for a while.

LEO
(Turns his head, a slight, cold smile)
And yet, here you are.

MARTIN
It’s a funeral, Leo. It’s not an exclusive club.
(He moves to the pitcher, his limp more pronounced. He struggles with the lid for a moment.)
You look... expensive.

LEO
You look exactly the same.

MARTIN
(Pours water, hand slightly shaky)
That’s a lie and you know it. I look like I’ve been left out in the weather. You look shrink-wrapped.

(Martin drinks the water in one long gulp. Leo watches him, a flicker of something unreadable crossing his face.)

LEO
I flew into O’Hare this morning. Rented a car. Drove straight here. The service hasn’t even started and I already want a shower. The air in this town… it sticks.

MARTIN
That’s the ethanol plant. Smells like money to some. And progress.

LEO
It smells like damp bread and regret. How can you stand it?

MARTIN
I don’t notice it anymore. Guess you get used to anything.

LEO
(Points to Martin’s leg)
What happened?

MARTIN
Nothing.

LEO
You’re limping.

MARTIN
I said it’s nothing. Old break. Healed wrong. You’d be the last person I’d complain to about it.

(A tense silence. The muffled organ starts playing a slow, mournful hymn. Leo flinches at the first chord.)

LEO
“Abide with Me.” He hated this hymn. Said it was for people who’d given up. He specifically told me once, “If they play ‘Abide with Me’ at my funeral, I’m getting up and walking out.” Where’s the pastor who actually knew him?

MARTIN
(Quietly, to the floor)
He died. Two years ago. This is the new guy. I gave him a list of Dad’s favorite things. The fishing spots, the baseball team, the chair he always sat in. He’s doing his best with what he’s got.

LEO
A list. You gave him a list. That’s… that’s a museum plaque. A little card next to an empty exhibit. A list.

MARTIN
Then you should have been here to write the eulogy. You’re the one with all the words.

(Leo’s composure finally cracks. He spins around, his voice a low, sharp blade.)

LEO
Don’t. Don’t you dare. You stayed. You got the chair, the fishing spots, the slow decay. You got the man who could barely speak at the end, whose hands shook so badly he couldn’t button his own shirt. I did not get that. I got a phone call at three in the morning from a neighbor I’ve never met, telling me it was over. Your list… your carefully curated list for a stranger… it’s a lie. It’s airbrushing the last decade.

MARTIN
(His face twists, voice finally rising)
What did you want me to put on the list, Leo? The DUI that cost him his license? The week the power got shut off because he forgot who to pay? The name he called me the night I had to wrestle the car keys from his hand? The one that broke two bones in my foot? You want that in a program, printed on nice cardstock?

(The air leaves the room. Leo stares at Martin’s foot. The water glass trembles in Martin’s hand. He sets it down, hard.)

LEO
(His voice is stripped bare, quiet.)
He… he broke your foot.

MARTIN
He didn’t know it was me. He thought I was trying to steal the truck. He was fighting a thief. It was the last time he ever tried to drive. I called an ambulance, and I told them he fell. That’s a thing on a list. “He fell.”

(Martin limps to one of the chairs and sits down heavily, staring at the back wall. Leo remains standing, unmoored. He looks from Martin’s duffel bag to his brother’s slumped shoulders. The organ music swells, then fades into a soft, final chord. A low murmur of the crowd standing on the other side of the wall.)

MARTIN
(Without looking up)
They’re standing. It’s starting.

(Leo walks over to the chair and, for the first time, sits next to his brother. They both face the same direction, towards the wall that separates them from the service. Their postures are identical. Leo reaches for the second glass and pours himself some water. He doesn’t drink it. He just holds it.)

LEO
(Staring straight ahead)
I didn’t know you had to wrestle him.

MARTIN
No. You didn’t.

(Blackout.)
