THE GOOD SUIT

A side room in a funeral home. Folding chairs stacked against one wall. A table with a coffee urn, styrofoam cups, a plate of untouched cookies. Muffled organ music from beyond a closed door.

DANIEL, late forties, stands at the table in a suit that fits him well. He is trying to work the urn's spigot. Nothing comes out.

The door opens. The music swells, then dulls as it closes. MARCUS, early forties, enters. His suit is too big in the shoulders. He stops when he sees Daniel.

MARCUS. You have to tilt it.

DANIEL. (not turning) I know how an urn works.

MARCUS. Okay.

Daniel tilts it. Coffee comes. He fills a cup, then, after a moment, a second. He leaves the second on the table, between them.

DANIEL. You missed the eulogy.

MARCUS. I heard it. I was standing in the back.

DANIEL. Standing.

MARCUS. There weren't seats.

DANIEL. There was a seat. Front row. Aunt Carol kept her purse on it for an hour.

Marcus takes the coffee. Drinks. Winces.

MARCUS. That's terrible.

DANIEL. It's been sitting since nine.

Silence. The organ stops. A murmur of voices takes its place.

MARCUS. It was good. What you said. The part about the truck.

DANIEL. I made that up.

MARCUS. What?

DANIEL. He never taught me to drive stick in the church lot. Mr. Okafor from next door taught me. Dad was at work. Dad was always at work. But you can't stand up in front of ninety people and say that, so.

MARCUS. (a short laugh) He taught me.

DANIEL. I know he did.

A beat. Marcus sets his cup down.

MARCUS. Dan, I didn't come to fight.

DANIEL. Then why did you come? Honest question. Six years. Mom's hip, you sent a card. His first stroke, you sent a text. To me. "Keep me posted." I kept you posted, Marcus. I posted you for six years.

MARCUS. He told me not to come back.

DANIEL. He told everybody everything. He told me I'd never make partner. He told Mom the casserole was dry, every Sunday, for forty years. You were the only one who believed him.

Marcus pulls at his jacket sleeve, which hangs past his wrist.

DANIEL. That's his.

MARCUS. What?

DANIEL. The suit. That's his gray one.

MARCUS. Mom gave it to me this morning. I came with a sport coat and she looked at me like I'd shown up in a bathing suit. She had it out on the bed already. (beat) She'd had it pressed.

DANIEL. She's been keeping it for you.

MARCUS. It doesn't fit.

DANIEL. No. He was bigger than both of us.

Marcus sits on one of the folding chairs. After a moment, Daniel takes another from the stack, unfolds it, sits. Not close.

MARCUS. Did he ask about me? At the end?

Daniel studies his coffee.

DANIEL. You want the truck version or the real one?

MARCUS. The real one.

DANIEL. No. He didn't. (beat) But the last month he kept calling me by your name. I'd come in to shave him and he'd say, "Marcus, you're holding it wrong." And I'd say, "It's Daniel, Dad." And he'd say, "I know who you are." And then he'd do it again.

MARCUS. Did you correct him every time?

DANIEL. At first.

MARCUS. And then?

DANIEL. And then I held it wrong. And he told me how to fix it. And I said, "Thanks, Dad." (beat) He seemed happier with you than he ever was with me.

Marcus puts his hand over his mouth. His shoulders move once inside the big jacket. Daniel lets him be.

The door opens a crack. A voice, offstage: "Boys? They're bringing the cars around."

DANIEL. (calling) One minute, Carol.

The door closes. Daniel stands, folds his chair, returns it to the stack. He looks at Marcus, then reaches down and turns back the cuff of his brother's left sleeve, once, neatly. Then the right.

DANIEL. There.

MARCUS. It still doesn't fit.

DANIEL. No. (He picks up both cups, drops them in the trash.) You're riding with Mom and me.

MARCUS. Is there room?

DANIEL. There's a seat.

He opens the door. Light and voices. Marcus stands, and goes through first.

Blackout.

END OF SCENE
