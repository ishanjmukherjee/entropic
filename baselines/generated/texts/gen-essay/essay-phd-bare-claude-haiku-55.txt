Choosing a research problem is the first major decision of a PhD, and it is easy to get wrong in both directions. Too narrow, and you exhaust your topic before you have learned how to think like a researcher. Too broad, and you spend years circling questions you cannot answer. Most students do not get to choose freely at the start, and that is fine. What matters is that you learn to make the choice deliberately rather than drifting into it.

Begin by understanding what a research problem is. It is not a topic. "Machine learning for healthcare" is a field you might work in. "Can calibrated uncertainty estimates change how clinicians act on a model's predictions?" is a problem. A good problem names a gap in what is known, states why filling that gap matters, and suggests a way of making progress. Before you commit to anything, practice writing one sentence in this form. If you cannot, you do not yet understand the area well enough to choose within it.

Read widely, but read with a purpose. In your first year, you will be tempted to read every paper in your area, and you will drown. Instead, identify ten to twenty papers that define the central conversation, and trace their citations forward and backward. Notice which questions keep coming up, which claims are contested, and where authors write sentences like "this remains an open problem" or "we leave this to future work." Those sentences are where problems live. Keep a running document of questions that occur to you while reading. Many of them will be naive, but a few will be worth pursuing, and you will recognize them later.

Pay attention to your own curiosity, but test it. Curiosity is necessary, since a PhD is too long and too lonely to sustain on obligation alone. But curiosity alone is not a plan. Ask yourself what you would still find interesting if the work were slow, the results were ambiguous, and nobody seemed to care for two years. If the answer is "a lot," you have found something real. If the answer is "not much," the topic may be attractive for the wrong reasons, such as prestige or fashion.

Consider tractability honestly. A good PhD problem is one you can make meaningful progress on within the time you have, with the data, tools, and collaborators you can realistically access. Ambitious problems are fine, but break them into pieces, and make sure at least the first piece can be finished in under a year. Ask yourself what the smallest publishable result would look like. If you cannot identify one, the problem may be too large to start with. Check whether the data you need exists, whether you can get access to it, and whether you have the skills to analyze it or can learn them quickly.

Talk to people, and choose your advisor as carefully as your problem. Your advisor will shape your problem more than any paper will. Find out how they supervise: how often they meet, whether they give detailed feedback, how they handle disagreement, and what happened to their previous students. Talk to current and former students, and ask them directly what they wish they had known. Your advisor's research interests should overlap with yours, but the stronger signal is whether their working style fits how you learn. A brilliant advisor who never answers email can be a worse match than a good one who engages closely.

Expect your problem to change, and treat early choices as provisional. Most students end up working on something different from what they proposed in their first year, and this is normal. Your first project is partly an education in how to do research, and its results may matter less than the skills you acquire. Set a checkpoint, perhaps at the end of your first or second year, to reassess whether the problem still excites you and whether it is still going somewhere. Changing direction is not failure, but drifting without deciding is.

Finally, remember that the problem you choose is also a choice about the kind of researcher you will become. Some problems reward deep specialization, while others reward connecting ideas across fields. Some favor theory, others favor building systems or collecting data. None of these is better in the abstract. Choose the one that matches the person you want to be in five years, and be honest with yourself about whether that person is the one who would enjoy the work.
