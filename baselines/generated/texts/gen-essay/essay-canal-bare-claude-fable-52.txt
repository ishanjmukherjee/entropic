When the Erie Canal opened in October 1825, Governor DeWitt Clinton sailed from Buffalo to New York City and poured a keg of Lake Erie water into the Atlantic. The ceremony, called the Wedding of the Waters, marked the completion of a 363-mile ditch that skeptics had mocked for eight years as "Clinton's Folly." Thomas Jefferson had called the idea "little short of madness" when it was proposed to him. Yet within a decade the canal had reshaped the economy of a continent, and its effects on American commerce, settlement, and politics lasted long after railroads made the waterway itself obsolete.

The problem the canal solved was geographic. The Appalachian Mountains walled off the eastern seaboard from the interior, and in the early nineteenth century moving goods overland was ruinously expensive. Hauling a ton of freight from Buffalo to New York City by wagon cost about a hundred dollars and took weeks. Farmers in the Ohio Valley found it cheaper to float their crops down the Mississippi to New Orleans than to send them east, and many Americans worried that the West would drift, economically and perhaps politically, away from the Atlantic states. New York had one great advantage: the Mohawk Valley, the only significant gap in the Appalachian chain between Georgia and Canada. A canal through that gap could link the Hudson River to the Great Lakes.

Building it was another matter. The United States had almost no trained civil engineers, and the federal government declined to fund the project. New York State paid for it alone, at a cost of roughly seven million dollars, and the men who built it learned as they went. Surveyors with no formal engineering education designed eighty-three locks to lift boats nearly six hundred feet between the Hudson and Lake Erie, carried the canal over rivers on stone aqueducts, and cut through the Niagara Escarpment at Lockport with a staircase of five double locks. Workers, many of them Irish immigrants, dug the channel by hand, forty feet wide and four feet deep, through forest and swamp. Along the way they invented stump pullers, developed a hydraulic cement that hardened underwater, and effectively created the American engineering profession. Veterans of the Erie went on to build canals and railroads across the country.

The economic results were immediate. Freight rates between Buffalo and New York fell by about ninety percent, to under ten dollars a ton, and travel time dropped from weeks to days. Tolls repaid the construction debt within about a decade. Wheat, flour, lumber, and salt moved east; manufactured goods, furniture, and settlers moved west. A barrel of flour from Rochester could now undersell flour milled much closer to the coast, and a farm family in Ohio could buy cloth and tools at prices their parents would not have believed.

No place benefited more than New York City. In 1820 it was already the nation's largest port, but Philadelphia, Boston, and Baltimore were serious rivals. The canal ended that contest. By funneling the trade of the Great Lakes basin down the Hudson, it made New York the gateway to the American interior, and the city's commerce, banking, and population grew accordingly. Its rivals scrambled to respond. Pennsylvania built an expensive and awkward system of canals and inclined planes over the mountains, and Baltimore chartered the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, but neither overcame New York's head start.

Along the canal itself, villages became cities. Rochester, powered by the falls of the Genesee and connected to markets by the canal, became the nation's leading flour-milling center. Syracuse grew on salt, Buffalo on the transfer of cargo between lake ships and canal boats. Farther west, the canal redirected the settlement of the continent. Migrants from New England and New York poured through it into northern Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, Illinois, and Wisconsin, giving the upper Midwest a Yankee character and binding its trade to the Northeast rather than to the South. That commercial alignment mattered a generation later, when the Civil War came and the states of the Old Northwest stood with the Union.

The canal carried ideas as well as cargo. The corridor it created across upstate New York became known as the "burned-over district" for the religious revivals that swept it repeatedly. Charles Finney preached in Rochester; Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon in Palmyra, a canal town; abolitionism and temperance flourished along the route; and the first women's rights convention met in 1848 at Seneca Falls, on a connecting waterway. Rapid growth, new wealth, and constant movement of people made the region unusually receptive to reform.

By the 1850s railroads were overtaking the canal, though it was enlarged several times and carried freight well into the twentieth century. Its deeper legacy was the proof it offered: that public investment in infrastructure could pay for itself many times over, and that a single well-placed line of transportation could decide which cities would rise and how a nation would hold together.
