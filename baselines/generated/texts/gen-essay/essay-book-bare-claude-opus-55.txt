Somewhere in the middle of G.B. Edwards's The Book of Ebenezer Le Page, the narrator, an old Guernseyman who has rarely left his island and is proud of it, remarks that he has never understood why people go abroad to find out who they are. He knows who he is. He is Ebenezer Le Page, of Les Moulins, in the parish of the Vale, and that is a sufficient answer to any question worth asking. It is a cantankerous, small-minded sentiment, and by the end of the novel's four hundred-odd pages it has come to seem like one of the wisest things anyone has ever said to me.

The book's history explains some of its obscurity. Gerald Basil Edwards was born on Guernsey in 1899, left for England as a young man, and spent decades in literary near-failure, drafting ambitious projects he never finished. Ebenezer was meant to be the first volume of a trilogy. He finished it in old age, could not find a publisher, and died in 1976 in a rented room in Weymouth. The novel appeared posthumously in 1981, championed by John Fowles, and was praised, reissued, praised again, and then largely forgotten. It has never quite found the readership it deserves, perhaps because it resists being summarized into anything that sounds appealing. An old man on a small island remembers his life. That is the whole pitch.

But what a voice. Ebenezer writes as he talks, in a plain, digressive, faintly ungrammatical English that carries the rhythms of the Guernsey French he grew up speaking. He is funny without trying to be, and occasionally without knowing he is. He holds grudges for sixty years. He disapproves of nearly everyone, especially his relatives, Methodists, Jerseymen, and the people who began building bungalows on good farmland. Yet the disapproval is so candid, and so often turned on himself, that it becomes a form of intimacy. Within a few pages you feel you have been sitting in his kitchen for an afternoon, and that he has decided, grudgingly, to trust you.

The life he recounts spans roughly the first three quarters of the twentieth century, and the great events arrive the way they actually arrive for most people: sideways, through the lives of friends. The First World War is the death of Jim Mahy, Ebenezer's closest companion, a loss he never names as the wound it plainly is. The German occupation of the Channel Islands during the Second World War is rendered not as heroism or villainy but as a long, hungry, morally muddled endurance, in which neighbors collaborate, resist, profiteer, and starve in roughly equal measure, and in which Ebenezer is honest enough to admit that he did not always behave well. History, in this book, is weather. It happens to the island, and the island goes on.

At the center of the novel is Liza Quéripel, the woman Ebenezer loves for most of his life and never marries. Their relationship is one of the most unsentimental love stories I know. They are both proud, both stubborn, both capable of cruelty, and the reasons they fail to come together are never quite clear even to Ebenezer, which is exactly the point. He tells it as an old man still trying to work out what happened, and the reader, watching from outside, can see what he cannot quite admit: that his independence, the thing he is proudest of, has cost him almost everything.

This is where the book's quiet ambition becomes visible. It looks like a regional novel, a piece of local color, the kind of thing a tourist board might stock. It is actually a profound meditation on what it means to belong to a place so completely that the place becomes a kind of self, and on the price of that belonging. Ebenezer's Guernsey is vanishing as he writes. The fishing and the greenhouses are giving way to tax exiles and holiday traffic, the old patois is dying, the families he knew are scattering or dying out. His book is an act of preservation, but he knows it is also an elegy, and he is too stubborn to say so directly. The tension between his gruff refusal of self-pity and the obvious grief underneath it gives the prose an extraordinary charge.

The ending, which concerns Ebenezer's search for someone to inherit what he has, both his property and, more importantly, his sense of the island, is among the most moving final chapters in twentieth-century fiction. I will not describe it except to say that it earns its tenderness by having withheld tenderness for so long.

Why is this book not better known? Partly because its author was dead before it was published and could not promote it. Partly because its virtues are slow ones: accumulation, voice, the gradual revelation of a character who does not understand himself. And partly, I suspect, because we tend to assume that great novels of the century must be about the centers of things, the cities and the wars and the intellectual movements. Edwards wrote about the margin, and in doing so wrote one of the truest accounts I know of how an ordinary life is actually lived: locally, stubbornly, imperfectly, and for keeps.
