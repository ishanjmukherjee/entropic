I have spent the better part of a decade reading transit agency budgets the way other people read mystery novels, and I can tell you where the body is buried. It's buried in the consultant contracts, the station caverns, and—above all—a set of assumptions everyone treats as natural law that turn out to be national habits.

The received wisdom is that tunneling is expensive everywhere and rising fast. This is false in an interesting way. New York's Second Avenue Subway cost roughly $2.6 billion per mile of route. The sheikdom-scale projects of the Arabian Peninsula come in well under a billion. Paris's Line 14 extension, which I've written about before, came in around $450 million per kilometer—call it $700 million a mile—including stations that would make a Dallas tollway authority weep. The deepest point of that line runs beneath the Seine through waterlogged alluvium, which is not easy ground. And yet Paris delivered it on schedule, within a few percent of budget, while Melbourne spends a decade agonizing over whether a tunnel might disturb the sleep of the bats.

So the question is not "why is subway construction expensive?" The question is "why does it cost eight times more per mile in New York than in Paris, when the Parisian soil is worse and the Parisian stations are better?" The answer is not geology, and it is not corruption, whoever the current batch of tabloids blame this week. It is the procurement system, the labor arrangements, and a client that has forgotten how to be a client.

Start with design. When RATP or the Barcelona metro authority builds a line, they do something that now sounds almost avant-garde: they design the station first, as a thing that will be built, and then let the contract follow. In New York, the MTA writes contracts before the design is complete—their famous "design-build" hybrids that sometimes end up neither designed nor built—and then pays contractors to fill the blanks with change orders. A change order on a bored tunnel is not like a change order on a kitchen renovation. It is assessed against whatever the contract said, and the contractor has every incentive to underbid the base and recoup on the changes. The diligence study we did for the Transit Costs Project at NYU found this structurally, not occasionally. Alon Levy, who ran that database, once estimated that something like a quarter of the cost difference between New York and comparable European projects could be traced to change orders on incompletely designed scopes before a single tunnel-boring machine touched clay.

Then there is the station cavern problem. A bored tunnel is not the expensive part; the expensive part is where you put the human beings. A mined station cavern in soft ground can cost more per meter than a kilometer of running tunnel, yet agencies keep specifying them because their reference designs, inherited from contractors who profit from big excavations, say so. Cut-and-cover, despised since the 1970s for tearing up streets, is cheaper by multiples—and can be done in two-year street disruption windows if you actually sequence it properly, as Seoul and Taipei did. Seoul built Line 9's stations as long-span boxes with side platforms, simple and repetitious, at a small fraction of New York per-kilometer costs. When I visited the Seoul metro authority in 2019, the planner I met kept repeating one phrase through the translator: "the same, the same, the same." Standardization is not glamorous. It is why their projects finish.

The third factor is labor, and here I will say something that annoys my own side. It is not public-sector unions per se. Transit unions in Copenhagen or Turin are not weak. What those systems have is work rule flexibility negotiated into project agreements—shift patterns that match the construction sequence, small crews on the surface, "only one burning" rules about how many torches may strike at once per contractor shop. What New York and Boston have instead is featherbedding written into law. On the Green Line Extension, the MBTA discovered it was paying flaggers whom nobody could identify a function for, and "consultants to oversee consultants" in an arrangement one auditor described—a friend of mine, actually, who asked not to be named in the report—as "a hall of mirrors with invoices." The project originally came in at nearly $3 billion for 4.3 miles of surface light rail. Not even a tunnel. A surface line. That is a record that should have triggered firings; it triggered a rebrand.

But the deepest cause is what I call client decay. An agency that builds a subway every three years has civil engineers on staff who know what things ought to cost, because they bought one last year. An agency that builds a subway every thirty years has no institutional memory and must buy everything—including the knowledge of what is a fair price—from the seller. The MTA's capital program staff is a fraction of the size it was in the era when the subway last expanded fast, and their supervision is outsourced. Paris builds so comparatively cheaply in part because it has never stopped building. That is the whole trick. The French and Korean and Spanish cost advantages are not cultural in some mystical way; they are the compound interest of practice. And you can acquire them, if you start now and don't stop, because Los Angeles—which began as a cost disaster, the Red Line at mid-1990s prices that embarrassed the federal government—built institutional skill and now bores its Regional Connector tunnels for a fraction of East Coast rates per mile.

The lesson I want everyone to draw is that cost is a choice. Geology does not choose. Unions and lawyers do not inevitably choose it either, and I say that as someone who has defended both in print. Agencies choose it through scope documents, station typologies, contract structures, and the decision to hire out their own judgment. Anyone who tells you it's simply too expensive to build subways is telling you about the invoice, not about physics. But we can keep being astonished by the invoice until somebody decides to read it.
