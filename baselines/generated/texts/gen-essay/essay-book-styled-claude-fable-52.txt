The best book I know about how to think carefully is shelved under popular engineering, has a cover that looks like a 1970s polytechnic prospectus, and is called Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down. J.E. Gordon published it in 1978, when he was a professor of materials at Reading, after a career that ran from Glasgow shipyards to wartime Farnborough, where he worried about the glue in wooden aircraft. People who know it tend to recommend it as a painless introduction to stress and strain. That is true in the way that Moby-Dick is a painless introduction to whaling. I think it is one of the great English essay collections of the last century, and that its central argument, which almost nobody outside fracture mechanics has absorbed, is more useful than anything on the decision-making shelf.

The argument is this: strength is the wrong thing to care about. For two hundred years engineers calculated the stress in a part, compared it to the strength of the material, added a safety factor, and went home. Then things broke anyway, at stresses a third or a fifth of what the arithmetic allowed. Gordon's fifth chapter explains why, using A.A. Griffith's 1920 work on cracks, and he does it almost entirely in words. A crack spreads when the strain energy released by its growth exceeds the energy needed to make new surface. Below a critical length it sits there harmlessly; above it, it runs at something like the speed of sound in the material. What protects you is the work of fracture, which for glass is a few joules per square metre and for mild steel is somewhere around a hundred thousand times that. This is why a steel ship can have hatches cut in its deck and a crack as long as your arm without anyone dying. It is also why "stronger" steels, which buy strength at the cost of toughness, have killed so many people.

Read that paragraph again with an organisation in mind instead of a hull. Gordon never draws the analogy, which is one reason I trust him, but the analogy is right there: systems fail by the propagation of flaws they already contain, and the property that matters is how much energy a flaw has to pay to grow. I have sat through a great deal of talk about "robustness" that would have been improved by one diagram from page 100.

He has the story to go with it, too. A ship's cook noticed a crack in his galley floor. The officers told him it was nothing. Being a methodical man, he painted a mark at the tip of the crack and wrote the date beside it, and kept doing so as it crept along. When the ship eventually broke in two, the half that was towed into port happened to contain the galley, and the cook's dated paint marks turned out to be the best record anyone had of a large crack growing slowly toward its critical length. Gordon tells this in about a hundred and fifty words and moves on. A lesser writer would have built a chapter, and a TED talk, on that cook.

What I love most is the range, which is never decorative. He needs to explain shear, so he explains why Madeleine Vionnet's bias-cut dresses clung the way they did in the 1920s, and he is entirely serious about it: cloth cut at forty-five degrees has a low shear stiffness and a large effective Poisson's ratio, so it contracts sideways under its own weight. He needs to explain why soft tissue has a J-shaped stress-strain curve, so he explains why arteries don't balloon into aneurysms the way a party balloon bulges at one spot. Medieval masons come in for the thrust line, and you learn that the pinnacles and statues on top of buttresses are working parts, dead weight placed to steer the thrust down inside the masonry. There is a long passage on Greek catapults and tendon. He quotes the Admiralty's reply to Thomas Young's paper on elasticity, which says in effect that their Lordships respect science greatly and have not understood a word.

The comparison usually made is to Henry Petroski, whose To Engineer Is Human is far better known in America. I think Petroski is the weaker writer by a wide margin. He circles his one idea about failure. Gordon has forty ideas and a temper. The last chapter, on efficiency and aesthetics, is where reviewers get embarrassed for him, because he says plainly that most modern manufactured objects are ugly and that this is a moral failing of engineers. I used to skip it. Now I think it is the key to the book. A man who has spent three hundred pages showing you that a Gothic buttress, a bat's wing and a yew bow are each exactly as heavy as they need to be has earned the right to be rude about a motorway bridge.

The mathematics amounts to a few appendices you can ignore. My Penguin copy cost four pounds and has fallen apart at the spine, along a crack that, I note, started at a staple hole.
