The best code review I ever received came from a man in Tbilisi I have never met. It was February 2021, I'd submitted a 340-line change to our billing reconciliation job, and he left eleven comments over the course of an afternoon I spent mostly asleep. One of them, the seventh, pointed out that my retry loop would double-charge any customer whose card processor timed out between the authorization and the capture. He was right. He'd written it out in four sentences with a link to the processor's documentation, page 31, and a test case I could paste in. If he had been sitting next to me in an office, he would have leaned over, said "hey, the retry thing is sketchy," and I would have nodded, fixed the obvious half of it, and shipped the other half to production.

I've now managed software teams in both arrangements, two in an office in Somerville, three fully distributed, and I've come to believe something that irritates most of the engineering managers I say it to. Remote work is better for software teams, and the usual objection against it, that it starves junior engineers of mentorship, is an admission that the team was never doing its job properly in the first place.

Here is what the office actually gave junior engineers at my old company. A new hire named Priya (she's now a staff engineer somewhere far more prestigious than we were) spent her first three weeks learning how our deploy pipeline worked by asking Dave. Dave knew everything. Dave also had a calendar so fragmented that by 2019 he was writing his own code between 7 and 9 p.m., after everyone left. When Dave quit, he took with him the only working understanding of why our staging environment needed to be restarted every Tuesday. We found out in his third week gone, when staging fell over and nobody could say why. It took two of us four days to rediscover that a cron job he'd set up in 2016 rotated a certificate that nothing else knew about.

That is what "osmotic learning" looks like up close. It's knowledge stored in the bodies of whoever happens to be loudest and most patient, retrievable only by interrupting them. People who miss the office talk about the hallway conversation as if it were a gift. Often it was a tax, paid by your most senior people, collected at random, with no receipt.

When we went remote, we couldn't do that anymore, and the inability was the point. Priya's equivalent on my next team got an onboarding document that ran to forty-one pages, maintained by whoever had most recently suffered through it. The rule was that every question a new hire asked in chat had to be answered in the doc, then linked. By the end of the first year, a new engineer could ship a change to production on day three without speaking to anyone synchronously. Not because we were heroic writers. We were mediocre writers who had been forced, by geography, to write.

I want to be fair to the other side, because their best argument is real. Remote work makes it easier to be invisible. I had an engineer in 2022 who was quietly drowning for about six weeks. In an office I'd have seen it on his face by day four. Remotely, I saw it in his commit history, which went from steady to sporadic to a single enormous commit with the message "wip." By the time I noticed the pattern he'd half-decided to quit. That was my failure, and it was a failure the office would have partly covered for me.

But notice what the office would have been covering for. A manager who can only detect distress by looking at faces is a manager with a narrow instrument. After that incident I started doing something embarrassingly simple: a fifteen-minute call every week with each direct report, camera optional, with one standing question, "what's the thing you're avoiding?" It worked better than any open-plan floor I've managed, because it was deliberate. The office lets you skip the deliberate version and feel fine about it.

There is also the matter of the work itself. Software is one of the few jobs where the product is mostly made of written language, and where the most expensive mistakes come from things that were said aloud and never written down. The decision to denormalize the orders table, the agreement that the mobile team would own push notifications, the verbal "yeah, we'll handle the timezone thing later." In a distributed team those either get written or they don't happen. In an office they happen and evaporate, and eighteen months later someone runs `git blame` and finds a commit with no ticket, no doc, and an author who left in 2020.

I don't think remote work is easier. It is clearly harder in its first year, and companies that went remote in March 2020 without changing anything else mostly got the worst of both worlds: office habits, conducted over Zoom, with worse audio. The teams that did well were the ones that treated distance as a forcing function and rebuilt around writing. The ones demanding a return now are, in my experience, mostly the ones that never did, and who've confused the comfort of being able to interrupt someone with the substance of working well together.

The man in Tbilisi, incidentally, left the company in 2023. His seven comments are still there in the pull request, still correct, still teaching whoever touches that retry loop next.
