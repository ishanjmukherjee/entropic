For most of history, secret communication depended on a shared secret. If two people wanted to exchange coded messages, they first had to agree on a key, and that agreement had to happen somewhere an eavesdropper could not listen. This worked for generals and diplomats who could send couriers, but it scales terribly. Every pair of correspondents needs its own key, and each key must somehow travel safely before any secure channel exists. Public-key cryptography, invented in the 1970s, solved this problem with an idea that sounds almost paradoxical: a system in which the key used to lock a message can be published to the whole world without revealing the key needed to unlock it.

The core concept is a pair of mathematically linked keys. One is public and can be handed to anyone; the other is private and never leaves its owner. Anything encrypted with the public key can be decrypted only with the corresponding private key. A common analogy is a padlock. Imagine you distribute thousands of open padlocks, all of which snap shut without a key but open only with the single key you keep at home. Anyone can put a message in a box, snap your padlock on it, and send it to you. Not even the sender can reopen the box. The padlock is your public key; the key at home is your private key.

What makes this possible is a class of mathematical operations called one-way functions: computations that are easy to perform but extraordinarily hard to reverse. Multiplying two large prime numbers is a classic example. A computer can multiply two primes of several hundred digits each in a fraction of a second, but given only the product, no known method can recover the original primes in any reasonable time. The best classical algorithms would take longer than the age of the universe for numbers of the size used in practice. Public-key systems rely on a special variety called a trapdoor function, which is hard to reverse unless you possess a particular piece of secret information, the trapdoor, that makes reversal easy.

The RSA algorithm, published in 1977 by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, shows how this works. To generate keys, you choose two large primes and multiply them to get a number called the modulus. The modulus, together with a chosen exponent, forms your public key. Using the two original primes, you compute a second exponent that serves as your private key. To encrypt, a sender converts the message into a number, raises it to the public exponent, and takes the remainder after dividing by the modulus. To decrypt, you raise the result to your private exponent and again take the remainder, which, thanks to a theorem of number theory dating back to Euler, yields the original message. Deriving the private exponent requires knowing the two primes, and finding those from the modulus means factoring it, which is the hard problem. The primes are the trapdoor.

Other systems rest on different hard problems. Diffie-Hellman key exchange, which actually predates RSA, lets two parties who have never met derive a shared secret over a completely public channel. It depends on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem: computing a number raised to a power in modular arithmetic is easy, but working backward to find the exponent is not. Elliptic-curve cryptography applies the same idea to points on an algebraic curve, achieving equivalent security with much smaller keys, which is why it dominates on phones and other modern devices.

The key pair can also be used in reverse to create digital signatures. If you transform a message with your private key, anyone can check the result using your public key. Since only the holder of the private key could have produced a signature that verifies, this proves both who sent the message and that it has not been altered. Signatures underpin software updates, electronic contracts, and cryptocurrency transactions.

In practice, public-key operations are slow compared with conventional symmetric ciphers, so real systems combine the two. When your browser connects to a website over HTTPS, public-key techniques are used briefly to authenticate the server and agree on a temporary shared key, and then a fast symmetric cipher encrypts the actual data. One remaining problem is knowing that a public key truly belongs to whoever claims it. This is handled by certificates: trusted organizations called certificate authorities digitally sign statements binding a public key to an identity, and your browser ships with a list of authorities it trusts.

The security of all this rests on the assumption that certain mathematical problems remain hard. No one has proven they are, and a sufficiently large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could break both factoring and discrete logarithms. For that reason, cryptographers have developed post-quantum algorithms based on different problems, such as those involving lattices, and these are now being deployed. The specific mathematics may change, but the central insight endures: with the right kind of asymmetry, two strangers can establish secrecy and trust in full public view.
