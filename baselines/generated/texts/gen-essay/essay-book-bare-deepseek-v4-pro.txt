It is a curious and quietly devastating experience to read a novel that seems to have been unjustly abandoned by the tides of literary fashion. R. C. Sherriff’s The Hopkins Manuscript is precisely such a book. First published in 1939, on the trembling lip of the Second World War, it was quickly buried by the very cataclysm it had prophesied. While contemporaries like H. G. Wells and Olaf Stapledon explored cosmic calamity with a scientist’s detachment, Sherriff chose a radically different, and profoundly more human, lens. He handed his apocalypse not to a hero, but to a retired schoolmaster with a passion for poultry and an unshakeable faith in the suburban pieties of Edwardian England. The result is a masterpiece of quiet horror, a social comedy that slowly freezes into tragedy, and a work that deserves to be rescued from the footnotes of speculative fiction and placed at its heart.

The novel is presented as a manuscript found centuries after the fact by a future society, a frame that immediately establishes its central irony. The narrator, Edgar Hopkins, is a man so utterly undistinguished that his very ordinariness becomes the point. A bachelor living in the insignificant village of Beadle, Hampshire, his life revolves around winning prizes for his chickens and maintaining his status as a member of the British Lunar Society. It is to this society that astronomers first reveal the staggering truth: the Moon is spiralling inexorably towards the Earth, and in a matter of months, a catastrophic collision will render humanity extinct. The news is delivered to the public not with panic, but with a calm, British sense of procedure. Hopkins’s first, overwhelming reaction is not terror, but a peevish indignation that the impending global annihilation will likely disrupt the upcoming Lunar Society dinner, where he was hoping to show off his latest prize-winning Buff Orpington.

This is the dark, satirical genius of Sherriff’s project. The first half of the book is a comedy of triviality that Evelyn Waugh might have admired. The British government, in an effort to maintain order, disseminates a "Dig for Victory"-style program called the "National Emergency Bulletins," which advise citizens to drain their swimming pools to avoid stagnant water and to organise local community singing to keep spirits up. Hopkins, elevated by the secret knowledge entrusted to him, becomes a monstrously self-important figure, strutting around his village with the condescending pity of a man who knows the date of the execution. He busies himself with preparing a "cataclysm cupboard," stocking it with tinned salmon and a single bottle of port, his preparations as pathetically inadequate as his emotional range. The scale of the impending horror is constantly measured against the yardstick of his own massive vanity; he frets that when the catastrophe comes, looters might target his poultry, which he has painstakingly bred to be "the finest in Hampshire."

Yet the novel’s true power lies in the fact that it does not maintain this satirical sneer. Gradually, almost imperceptibly, the tone shifts. The comedy curdles. The Moon, which at first was a distant, abstract threat discussed over sherry, begins to grow in the night sky until its pockmarked face looms with a terrifying, silent intimacy. The tides go mad, swallowing coastal towns in a single, gluttonous gulp. The veneer of civilisation that Hopkins so cherishes—the dinner parties, the village cricket matches, the rigid social hierarchies—is not shattered in one explosive moment, but slowly eroded, like a chalk cliff under an unrelenting sea. Sherriff’s prose, which began as a pitch-perfect parody of a pompous diary, becomes increasingly stark and stripped of comfort.

The most devastating sequence involves not a comet’s blast, but a simple human tragedy. As the panic-stricken population of London empties into the supposedly safer countryside, a tidal wave of refugees passes through Beadle. Hopkins, who has a room prepared and a store of food, stands at his gate as a silent, shuffling procession of the dispossessed files past. He waits, expecting a friend from the city to whom he once, in a moment of uncharacteristic generosity, offered shelter. But when the friend does not appear, Hopkins, paralysed by his own meanness and a profound failure of imagination, looks upon the desperate faces and closes his gate, bolting it against them. He retreats to his garden, alone with his chickens. It is a moment of profound moral cowardice, depicted without authorial judgment but with a pitiless clarity that makes the reader’s blood run cold. In this single action, Sherriff shows us that Hopkins is not an anomaly; he is a mirror reflecting a civilisation so anaesthetised by its own codes of propriety that it has lost the capacity for spontaneous human compassion. The apocalypse is not just in the sky; it is a spiritual decay that has already occurred.

Sherriff, best known for his play Journey’s End, understood that the most profound drama comes not from grand events but from the human response to them. The Hopkins Manuscript is a book that understands the Apocalypse is not simply a full stop at the end of history, but a slow, grinding process of diminishment, where the world ends first in the failure to imagine another’s suffering. Edgar Hopkins witnesses the complete destruction of his world, yet his greatest sorrow is reserved not for the billions of dead, but for the unread pages of his diary that will sink beneath the new, primordial sea. He remains, to the crushing end, an Englishman more devoted to the form of civilisation than to its substance.

The book’s ultimate achievement is a profound and heart-breaking ambivalence. We despise Hopkins for his pettiness, his pomposity, and his catastrophic moral failure, yet as his lonely, absurdly dignified end approaches, we feel an unexpected stab of pity. He is not a monster, but something far more common and terrifying: a limited man whose emotional myopia prevents him from reaching out to his fellow human beings until it is too late. The Hopkins Manuscript is thus far more than a curio of pre-war anxiety. It is a timeless and urgently relevant fable about the dangers of insularity, the seductive comfort of routine, and the terrifying ease with which a civilisation can bolt its gates against the unknown. It is, quite simply, one of the most moving and intelligent British novels of the twentieth century, and it is a quiet tragedy of our literary culture that it isn’t read by everyone.
