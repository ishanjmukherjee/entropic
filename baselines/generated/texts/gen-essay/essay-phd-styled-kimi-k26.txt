Don't pick a problem because it's important. Pick a problem because you can stand being wrong about it for six years.

I learned this the hard way in my third year, when I abandoned a project on algorithmic fairness that every funding agency wanted to fund and every bilbio.comb threatened to publish in Science if I could only get the causal identification right. I couldn't. The problem was genuinely important—decisions about who gets a loan, who gets parole—but importance is a trap. It disguises impossibility as nobility. I spent eighteen months debugging instrumental variables that kept failing Wu-Hausman tests, waking at 3 AM to rerun regressions with different fixed effects, telling myself the suffering was proportionate to the stakes. It wasn't. The suffering was just suffering, and when I finally quit, I had nothing: no paper, no peace of mind, no affection for the work I'd done.

The project I finished—the one that became my dissertation—was about something absurdly narrow: how 19th-century Danish parish priests recorded illegitimate births in their registers, and what their inconsistent practices reveal about the emergence of statistical thinking as a moral technology. I could have described it at a dinner party only by watching eyes glaze over. But I found something in those archives, in the varnished-script handwriting of a priest named Rasmus Hansen who served Tønder parish from 1827 to 1861, that made me feel complicit in a conspiracy across time. Hansen used a special symbol, a sort of bracketed cross, for births he suspected were adulterous but lacked proof to exclude from the legitimate column. No one had noticed this symbol before; no one had cared to look. When I found it, in a register wrapped in linen the color of old nicotine, I actually laughed out loud in the reading room. The archivist shushed me. I didn't care.

That's the feeling you're looking for. Not the laughing necessarily, though I'll take it. The not-caring-who-shushes-you. The sense that you've found a secret that was waiting specifically for you, that your particular defects as a human being have somehow become qualifications. Hansen's ambiguous cross spoke to me because I am myself ambiguous, evasive, prone to qualifies and bracketed conditions. An important problem would never have offered me that intimacy.

Let me be concrete about what I mean, since you'll hear plenty of vague encouragement to "follow your passion." In my fourth year, I considered three problems seriously. The first: extending a machine-learning framework for predicting protein folding, which my advisor wanted me to work on and which had, that very spring, produced a splashy Nature paper from DeepMind. The second: a historical study of how colonial botanists in the Dutch East Indies standardized plant classification, which I'd come to through an undergraduate seminar I'd never forgotten. The third: a linguistic analysis of how Supreme Court justices modulated their use of Latinate vocabulary when addressing female versus male attorneys, suggested by a friend who'd noticed something anomalous in oral argument transcripts.

I estimated each project's time-to-completion using the embarrassingly naive method of asking recent graduates. The protein-folding project: five to seven years, with high variance; a postdoc probably required; the problem was moving fast, and my contribution risked being scooped or made irrelevant by better-resourced competition. The colonial botany project: four to five years, but archives in Leiden and Jakarta, meaning I'd spend perhaps eighteen months just replicating materials with uncertain leads. The Latinate vocabulary project: existing digital corpus, no travel, probably two to three years—but also, my friend warned, likely unpublishable in top law reviews because it mixed methods they disdained, and unpublishable in linguistics journals because the legal context was too specialized.

I chose botany. It took four and a half years, and I spent eleven months in Jakarta living in a boarding house where the bathroom had no door and rats nested in the thatched roof. I got dengue fever, twice. The archives held treasures I hadn't anticipated—personal letters between botanists competing for the same Javanese orchid specimens, one of whom died in 1903 of dysentery after drinking unboiled water rather than leave a rare Coelogyne unobserved—and I fell in love with the specificity of their quarrels, their petty professionalism, their physical courage in pursuit of what they knew to be minor knowledge. I wrote about sneezes and herbarium mold and the way dried specimens crackle when you unbind them. I finished. The dissertation won a prize that paid my rent for a year. I've never regretted a single rat.

What I want to tell you, new PhD student, is that you will regret the alternative. The project you abandon because it seems insufficiently grand, insufficiently consequential—you will think about it at thirty-five, at forty, at the moments when your actual work feels mechanical and your colleagues' attention wanders at conference presentations. The project you choose because it hurts in the right way, because it embarrasses you slightly to explain, because it requires you to become competent at something absurd like 19th-century Danish parish administration or Javanese rat avoidance—these investments accumulate into a self you recognize. The other kind of problem, the important kind, tends to accumulate into a self you perform, and performance exhausts differently than work. It exhausts without satiation.

I am not saying ignore feasibility. I'm saying invert the usual calculation. Most advice treats intellectual interest and practical completion as competing goods to balance. I think they're more nearly aligned than we admit, but only if you define interest peculiarly. Not: do I find this subject fascinating? But: do I find myself, my specific limitations, necessary to this subject? Does it create occasions for my particular intelligence, which is to say my particular stupidity too?

Rasmus Hansen's bracketed crosses would never have been visible to someone with cleaner research ethics, someone less personally tempted by equivocation. I saw them because I was looking for myself. That's not noble. It's barely even methodological. But it's what I have, and four years after the PhD, it's still what I have—that laugh in the archive, the archivists's shush, my own complete indifference to being shushed.
