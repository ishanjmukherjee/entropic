I've spent fifteen years in security engineering, and I still watch people's eyes glaze over when I try to explain what I actually do. The usual culprits—Diffie-Hellman this, elliptic curve that—land like sedatives. So here's my heretical pitch: public-key cryptography is fundamentally a hack against the problem of meeting in person, and we've elevated it to theological status without admitting how rickety the whole scaffolding remains.

Let me start with Whitfield Diffie trying to pick locks in his Boston apartment in 1974, because that's where this story actually begins, not with the mathematics but with the loneliness. Diffie wanted to talk securely to someone he'd never met. The classical solution—a shared secret, a single key both parties possessed—required a clandestine meeting, a trusted courier, some physical exchange that assumed geography and presence. This was fine for bankers in 1920, useless for dissidents in 1975 or, later, for me trying to buy a coffee with my phone without the barista learning my checking account number.

The trick, which Diffie and Martin Hellman published in 1976, exploits a mathematical oddity that's almost too cute to be useful. You pick a prime number—call it p = 23, though in practice you'd want something with 600+ digits. Then you pick a base, g = 5. I generate a private random number, say a = 6, and compute g^a mod p, which gives me 8. I shout "8" across the room, or across the internet, who cares, it's public. You pick your own secret, b = 15, compute g^b mod p = 19, shout that back. Here's the witchcraft: I take your 19, raise it to my secret 6, get 19^6 mod 23 = 2. You take my 8, raise it to your 15, get 8^15 mod 23 = 2. We share a secret—2—without ever exchanging that number directly, and an eavesdropper who heard both 8 and 19 cannot derive 2 without solving the discrete logarithm problem, which for large primes would require more computation than exists in the known universe.

But notice what we haven't solved. Mallory, the active attacker, could have intercepted both shouts and substituted her own numbers. I think I'm talking to you; I'm actually sharing a secret with Mallory, who shares a different secret with you. She reads everything, modifies anything, and neither of us knows. This isn't a cryptographic problem; it's a trust problem, and cryptography has nothing to say about trust except "handle it elsewhere." The casual explainer usually omits this, presenting public-key exchange as magic. I find this omission intellectually dishonest and practically dangerous.

The RSA variant, developed by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman in 1977, uses different mathematics—factorization of large semiprimes—but arrives at a similar destination with a crucial structural difference. You create two keys: one to lock, one to unlock, and the math makes it infeasible to derive one from the other. I can publish my locking key widely. Anyone can encrypt to me. Only my unlocking key, which I guard, decrypts. This seems to elegantly solve Diffie's problem, and in limited contexts—HTTPS, SSH key pairs—it mostly does.

Where it breaks down is in the 'mostly.' In 2011, DigiNotar, a Dutch certificate authority whose root keys were trusted by every major browser, was compromised. Attackers issued fraudulent certificates for Google.com, *.google.com, CIA.gov, Mossad. For months, Iranian users trying to check Gmail were instead directed to attacker-controlled servers that presented perfect, cryptographically valid certificates. The locks worked perfectly; the keys had been stolen at the source. The project of the Public Key Infrastructure, of chaining trust through certificate authorities, requires us to believe that hundreds of corporations across dozens of jurisdictions will never be compromised, never be compelled, never simply screw up. I don't believe this. I've reviewed the audit logs of CAs. You shouldn't believe it either.

The mathematics—properly implemented, with large enough keys, on hardware without side channels—remains sound. But the implementation is where humans intervene, and humans are the ongoing disaster. I spent three months in 2016 tracking a bug in a major TLS library where random number generation during key creation was, on certain virtualized systems, slightly predictable. 'Slightly' sounds harmless until you realize that 0.001% predictability across billions of key exchanges becomes an exploitable statistical attack given enough traffic. The library had passed formal verification. The reviewers were competent. The bug sat there for four years.

This is why I get impatient with explanations that present public-key cryptography as solved or elegant. It's neither. It's an expensive, fragile workaround for the fundamental problem that we want to communicate securely without ever meeting, and the mathematical structures we've found are baroque, computationally costly, and perpetually surrounded by implementation failures. Elliptic curve methods—Curve25519, the edwards curves—improve performance and key sizes, but introduce new failure modes in twist security and invalid curve attacks that RSA never worried about. The abstraction stack grows taller, not cleaner.

I still use it daily. I maintain SSH keys, I check certificate pins, I've personally verified the PGP fingerprints of colleagues at conferences because I refuse to rely entirely on the web of trust. The technology is necessary; it is also, in its essentials, unsatisfying. What I wish more people understood is that every time you see the lock icon in your browser, what you're really seeing is a claim by a chain of strangers that they validated the identity of another stranger, secured by mathematics that would take too long to break, implemented by programmers who were probably tired, running on hardware designed in a dozen countries. The math is the least worrisome part of this chain. The math is the part that actually works, when it works, which it usually does, unless it doesn't, and you won't know when it doesn't, because that's rather the point of a successful break.

This is the honest explanation. It's messier than the textbooks prefer. I find it more impressive, not less, for that messiness—human ingenuity holding together a questionable structure through accumulated patches and vigilance, building something functionally useful atop genuinely deep mathematics and genuinely shaky social infrastructure. The miracle isn't that public-key cryptography works. The miracle is that it works as often as it does.
