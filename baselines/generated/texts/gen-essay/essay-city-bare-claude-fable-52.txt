The first thing I learned about my new city was that the grocery store closed at nine. I learned this at 9:04, standing in a dark parking lot with an empty refrigerator waiting for me three blocks away. In the place I had left, there was always somewhere open, and I knew where it was without thinking. Here, I knew nothing without thinking. That was the real cost of moving, I would come to understand: not the boxes or the deposit or the long drive, but the sudden loss of everything I used to know automatically.

I moved for a job, which is the least romantic reason to move anywhere. Nobody writes songs about relocating for a slightly better title. But I had also been restless for a while, in a way I couldn't explain to friends who asked why I'd leave a city where I had a favorite bar, a reliable dentist, and a barber who remembered how I liked my hair cut. I told them it was a good opportunity. What I meant was that I had started to feel like a piece of furniture in my own life, placed somewhere years ago and never moved.

So I packed. Packing forces a kind of archaeology on you. I found ticket stubs from concerts I barely remembered, a birthday card from someone I no longer spoke to, three separate can openers. I had to decide, object by object, what belonged to the person I was going to be. I was not as ruthless as I'd hoped. The can openers all came with me.

The first weeks were lonely in a way that surprised me. I had expected to miss people, and I did, but what I missed more were the small, nameless interactions: the nod from the man at the corner store, the neighbor whose dog I knew by name even though I never learned hers. In the new city, no one nodded. I was invisible, which I had imagined would feel like freedom and instead felt like static. I'd walk home from work past windows lit yellow, people cooking dinner inside, and feel like I was watching a play performed in a language I'd studied but didn't speak.

I made mistakes constantly. I took the wrong bus and ended up in an industrial district at dusk. I confidently recommended a restaurant to a coworker and then discovered it had been closed for a year; I'd been reading an old review. I mispronounced the name of a major street for a full month before someone kindly corrected me. Each error was tiny, but they accumulated into a low hum of incompetence. At thirty-one, I had forgotten what it felt like to be bad at ordinary life.

But somewhere in the second month, something shifted. I think it started with the bakery. There was one on my walk to work, a narrow place with a fogged window, and I began stopping there on Fridays. The third or fourth time, the woman behind the counter said, "The usual?" I hadn't known I had a usual. I nearly cried into my almond croissant. It was the first evidence that I existed here, that I had worn the faintest groove into this place and that the place had noticed.

After that, I started collecting grooves on purpose. I joined a weeknight running group, though I am a mediocre runner, because it guaranteed I would say words out loud to other humans every Wednesday. I walked different routes home until I had a map in my head instead of on my phone. I found the park where old men played chess, the bookstore with the cat, the hill where you could see the whole skyline go pink at sunset. I learned which grocery store stayed open until eleven.

What nobody tells you about moving is that the loneliness and the freedom are the same thing, seen from different angles. No one here knew me, which meant no one expected me to be who I had been. I started cooking, something I'd never done in my old life, where takeout menus lived in a drawer by habit. I said yes to invitations I would once have declined. I wasn't reinventing myself, exactly. It was more like finding rooms in a house I'd lived in for years and never fully explored.

I've been here almost a year now. I still miss the old city sometimes, sharply, usually when something small reminds me of it: a certain slant of light, a song from a passing car. I've stopped thinking of that ache as a sign I made the wrong choice. It's just the price of having loved a place, and I'd rather pay it than have nothing to miss.

Last week, a stranger stopped me on the street and asked for directions to the train station. I gave them without hesitating, pointing, naming the streets correctly. She thanked me and walked on. I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, a local, at home.
