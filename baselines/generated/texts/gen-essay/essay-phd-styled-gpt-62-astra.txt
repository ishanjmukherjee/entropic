If you showed me three possible PhD problems, I would ask you a question that barely figures in most discussions of intellectual merit: which one lets you be wrong by Christmas? I would probably pick that one. A doctorate is short, in spite of considerable evidence to the contrary on any given Tuesday. The great luxury it affords is the chance to change your mind several times about one thing. Choose a problem that withholds its first piece of bad news for three years and you have bought yourself remarkably little of that luxury.

Take a problem I would happily give a historian: did the arrival of the railway make bread cheaper in English market towns? There is a respectable argument that it did. Flour could come in from farther away; a local miller could no longer charge quite what he pleased. There is also a respectable argument that a railway gave the miller somewhere more lucrative to send his flour. In a town that had enjoyed cheap bread because it was inconveniently situated, the arrival of the first train might be an excellent day not to be poor.

Now suppose your prospective supervisor gets rather pink with pleasure at the mention of this project. She can already see the map, with little black lines inching across the Midlands. Before you learn to animate the lines, go and find a loaf. By this I mean one actual quotation for the retail price of bread, in one identifiable town, on either side of the opening of its station. Set aside a week. Keep an exact account of what you find, including all the things that look very much like the price of bread until you try to use them.

The London Gazette, for instance, printed grain prices from named markets. These are wonderfully seductive columns. The type is regular, the dates are there, and before lunch you can have a spreadsheet. What you have, however, is wheat sold by the quarter, not bread sold by the loaf. Between the two stand a miller, a baker, their fuel bills, the quality of the flour and the possibility that the baker extended credit until payday. That gap is where most of the interest of your original question resided. It would be a pity to spend four years quietly assuming it away because the Gazette has nice columns.

You may come back on Friday with six advertisements for bakers, a dispute over the weight of a quartern loaf and no price series at all. I would call that a good week. I would call it an exceptionally good week if the dispute interested you more than the railway. Who weighed the loaf? Who was entitled to complain? Was a customer who bought on tick in any position to march a light loaf down to the inspector? You now have some evidence about a fairly important variable in the selection of a thesis: what you actually want to know after the person who assigned the reading has left the room.

My rule is often mistaken for an injunction to be modest. I have almost the opposite objection to the grand project. Too often it offers the student one distinctly modest job: assembling, at considerable personal inconvenience, the illustration to a sentence the supervisor wrote in a grant application two years ago. Ask her what finding would make her take that sentence out. Listen for a concrete answer. “If prices rose in the towns that had previously been cheapest, I’d have to rethink it” is a promising one. “Even a null result would enrich our understanding” would send me back to the other two projects. I want a supervisor capable of disappointment. It means there is something she doesn’t know.

There are fields in which Christmas is an absurd deadline. Nobody excavates half a Roman port to see whether excavation suits them. But you can usually get close to the act on which the larger undertaking depends. Take a tray of pottery that two specialists have already dated independently. Cover the labels. Try sorting it yourself, then look particularly hard at the pieces on which all three of you disagree. Perhaps the dates you need to distinguish two accounts of the port’s decline are thirty years apart and the pottery is good for a century. Better to discover that with a tray on the table than with a trench filling up with rain.

I would save one sheet from that first week, preferably the most annoying one, for the meeting where you settle on your topic. Put it between you and the supervisor. A proposal encourages two people to agree pleasantly about the work while imagining quite different Tuesdays. The sheet makes that harder. Here is the inspector’s report; here is the missing price; here is the smudged word that might be “household” and might be the name of a baker. What would she do with it? And when she reaches across the desk to turn it the right way up, do you find yourself wanting the sheet back?
