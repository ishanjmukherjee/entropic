I have killed three pigs in my life, all by hand, all on a farm outside Athens, Georgia, in the fall of 2016. The first one I botched. The pig screamed—pigs scream like nothing else on earth, a sound that gets into your sternum—and I hesitated, and the knife went in at the wrong angle, and what should have taken eight seconds took closer to a minute. I went home and didn't eat meat for three weeks. Then I ate it again. That sequence—flinching, abstaining, returning—is more honest, I think, than either the vegan absolutism I hear from my students or the bacchanalian nose-to-tail cheerleading I get from chef friends. But it's also the ground on which I've built an actual position, which is this: eating animals is not wrong, but the way almost everyone reading this eats animals almost certainly is.

Let me sharpen that. The claim "it is wrong to eat animals" treats a fork as the moral instrument, and a fork is never the moral instrument. The moral work happens earlier—whether the animal's death bore some proportion to what came of it. That's a criterion, vague as it is, that the pig I botched can meet. Two hundred pounds of pork fed a dozen families on that farm for most of a winter. The pig had spent its life in woods, eating acorns, doing pig things. If you grant, as I think you must, that a pig rolling in leaf litter is living a decent pig life, then the question becomes whether taking that life for food wrongs the pig—and here's where the philosophically fashionable answer (Singer's, roughly: animal suffering counts equally with ours, so killing for palatable food is murder by another name) starts to wobble. Killing and harming are not the same. A quick death after a good life is not obviously a harm to a creature whose alternatives all end in the same place—pigs do not get to die of old age at a hospice, and wild pigs die far worse deaths than any slaughterhouse, botched or otherwise.

I want to be careful here, because this line of reasoning gets abused by people looking for permission slips, and I refuse to write permission slips. So let me be specific about what I'm not excusing. A gestation crate is two feet by seven feet. I've stood next to rows of them in a commercial facility in North Carolina, and the sows inside them—animals built to root, dig, and walk ten miles a day—could do nothing but stand up and lie down, chewing the bars until their mouths bled. Roughly 60 to 70 percent of breeding sows in this country spent their recent lives in crates like those. That is torture, in the plain sense of the word, and I don't care that the animal doesn't narrate the torture to itself. You do not need a concept of freedom to be destroyed by its total absence. Any pork chop in my freezer that traces back to that system is something I've decided I can't eat, not because eating animals is wrong but because paying for that system makes me a customer of torture, and I'm not comfortable being that.

The gap between those two cases—the acorn-fed pig in the woods, the crate-sow in Sampson County—is the actual moral landscape, and most of the public argument happens by pretending the gap doesn't exist. Vegans point at the crate, rightly, and then conclude that the fork itself is damned, which is like witnessing a factory fire and concluding that combustion is evil. Meanwhile the conscientious-carnivore crowd points at the pig in the woods and behaves as if the whole meat economy could be made of such pigs, which is arithmetically delusional. Pastured animal agriculture takes more land, more time, more money. If all 130-odd million pigs slaughtered annually in the US lived like my botched pig, you'd need to convert most of the Southeast back to forest-pasture and charge four times what pork costs now. I've done the farm-budget math; industrial pork is cheap because cruelty is a production input. The cheapness and the cruelty are the same fact seen from two sides.

So the position that survives contact with reality is something like this: I eat meat, roughly once or twice a week, from animals whose lives I have either witnessed or paid a serious premium to sources I've actually visited. The premium isn't a luxury gesture; it's the honest price of the thing. A chicken that lived a chicken's life costs four times a Perdue rotisserie bird, which means I eat chicken a quarter as often as I otherwise would, which—which is the point. Volume is where the ethics live. The average American eats about 220 pounds of meat a year, and there is no sourcing decision, no label, no farmers-market ritual that makes that number defensible. It's defensible only through the industrial cruelty that makes it affordable, which is why I've slowly come to think the most ethical eaters I know aren't the vegans or the nose-to-tail romantics but the people who quietly treat meat as a Sunday thing, the way most of humanity did for most of history. My grandmother, born in 1928 in eastern Poland, butchered maybe six chickens a year and thought people who ate meat daily were decadent. She was right.

You can disagree with me at either end, and thoughtful people do. The vegan will say the crate proves something about the category—that our appetite, once fed, inevitably builds the crate, because demand doesn't distinguish. That's a serious argument and there are days, usually after rereading Singer, when it nearly persuades me. The unapologetic carnivore will say I've drawn an arbitrary line and am paying indulgences to feel clean. Also serious, also occasionally persuasive. But the line isn't arbitrary; it's load-bearing. What I won't grant is that the question reduces to a slogan, either "meat is murder" or "it's just food." The pig I killed badly in 2016 was not a symbol. She was a specific animal, now long eaten, whose life and death can still be judged—better than a crate, worse than never having been needed at all—and the fact that the judgment is hard is precisely why it's worth doing. Most people never do it. That, more than the eating itself, is the wrong.
