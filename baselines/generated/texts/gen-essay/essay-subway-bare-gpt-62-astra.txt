A kilometer of subway can cost several times as much in one country as in another, even when both lines run beneath prosperous, densely built cities. It is tempting to explain the gap by pointing to wages, geology, or corruption. Any of these can matter, but none explains why some high-wage cities repeatedly deliver complex railways at moderate cost while others struggle. The more useful question is what a city buys when it buys a subway. Along with tunnels and tracks, it pays for stations, the relocation of utilities, and the disruption of streets. What each of those costs depends partly on how well the institutions involved make decisions together.

Before drawing conclusions from a cost ranking, it is worth asking whether the figures describe the same thing. One budget may include trains, a maintenance yard, land purchases, and financing charges; another may cover construction alone. A line with widely spaced stops will usually look cheaper per kilometer than one serving a succession of busy neighborhoods. Yet the latter may put far more passengers within walking distance of a station. Comparisons are most revealing when they account for these differences and then ask why comparable pieces of infrastructure—a station of a given depth and capacity, for instance—still have very different price tags.

Stations are a good place to start because their cost is easy to underestimate. On a map, a station is a dot; underground, it is a building that must accommodate platforms, stairs, elevators, ventilation, and emergency exits. Making it longer can mean excavating another stretch of an intersection crowded with pipes and cables. Building it deeper means longer trips for excavated material and more machinery to get passengers to the street. A deep station may be the right choice beneath an existing railway. But where each station is designed afresh, an agency can end up paying for unusual dimensions without gaining much that passengers value. A standard design gives engineers a starting point and makes departures from it decisions they have to justify.

Wages matter, but the wage per hour is only half the calculation. The other half is the number of hours the job takes. Consider a crew ready to excavate a station entrance that discovers an unmapped water main. If nobody on site has authority to approve a diversion, equipment may sit idle while the contractor, utility, and transit agency exchange drawings. The workers are no less skilled for having spent a shift waiting. An agency that investigated the site early and agreed with the utility on who could authorize changes might pay equally generous wages and still spend considerably less.

Contracting determines who pays for such surprises, but it cannot make them disappear. A city may invite firms to bid a fixed price while leaving important questions about ground conditions unresolved. An experienced bidder will charge a premium for risks it cannot measure; a more optimistic bidder may win and later seek compensation when the work proves harder than expected. Where disputes become routine, both parties have reason to document every instruction with a possible lawsuit in mind. A better contract assigns each risk to the party best equipped to manage it. That requires a public agency with enough engineering knowledge to judge a bid rather than simply pick the lowest number.

Political arrangements also shape the bill. Suppose a city could build a shallow station by opening a street from above, but doing so would interrupt deliveries to local shops. Mining a station farther underground might keep the street open at much greater expense. Neither choice is inherently wrong: disruption is a real cost even if it never appears in the transit budget. The trouble comes when the transit agency plans for the shallow option, the street authority rules it out late, and the city pays first to design one station and then to design another. Early consultation is valuable precisely because it gives those affected a say while alternatives are still affordable.

Many of these advantages accumulate through repetition. A region that builds steadily can retain engineers who remember which station layouts worked, and suppliers can invest in equipment knowing there will be another project. A region that builds once a generation must assemble a team, recover knowledge scattered across old reports, and persuade firms to enter a market that may soon vanish. High costs can then reinforce themselves: each expensive project makes the next one harder to fund, and the long pause makes the next one harder to build.

Geology and urban density set genuine constraints, but they do not dictate how often a drawing must be revised or how long a crew must wait for a decision. Those are costs that cities can change without cutting wages, weakening safety rules, or asking neighborhoods to accept unlimited disruption. The best reason to study a less expensive subway abroad is not to import its price per kilometer as a promise. It is to learn which problems its builders solved before the clock on construction started running.
