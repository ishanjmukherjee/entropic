I ran an engineering team of fourteen out of a fourth-floor office in Oakland for six years, and then ran roughly the same team from a spare bedroom for four more. I liked the office. I miss the burrito place downstairs. I also think the office made us worse at building software, and I didn't see how until it was gone.

The standard defense of the office is osmosis. You overhear things. A junior engineer hears two seniors arguing about whether the retry logic belongs in the client or the queue consumer, and absorbs a lesson no one had to schedule. I believed this for years, and it's true as far as it goes. What I missed is that osmosis is a subsidy. It pays for a team's failure to write things down, and like most subsidies it hides the real cost of the thing it supports.

In March 2020 we went home, and within three weeks we discovered that nobody could explain why our billing service wrote to two databases. The person who knew had left in 2018. In the office this had never been a problem, because Dmitri sort of knew and sat near the people who needed to sort of know. The knowledge lived in a twelve-foot radius around his desk. When the radius disappeared, we had to actually figure it out, and the answer was that the second write had been dead code for two years, costing us about $1,100 a month in a replica nobody read from.

That was the pattern, over and over. Remote work didn't create our communication problems. It sent us the invoice.

So we started writing. We had always claimed to write design docs. Now the doc was the only place a decision could happen, because there was no hallway to settle it in. We adopted a rule I stole from somewhere: if a Slack thread passes fifteen messages, someone has to write up the conclusion in the repo, in a `decisions/` folder, with a date. Eighteen months later that folder had 212 files in it. New hires read it like a novel. One of them told me it was the first time she'd joined a company and understood why the codebase was shaped the way it was, and not merely how.

People object that writing is slow. It is. A conversation that takes four minutes at a whiteboard takes forty to write up properly. But the whiteboard conversation serves the two people standing there, and the writeup serves everyone who touches that system for the next five years, including the two people, who will forget. The whiteboard also flatters you. Half-formed ideas sound fine out loud, where tone and hand gestures cover the gaps. On the page, "we'll just cache it" has to survive the question of what invalidates the cache, and it frequently doesn't. I've watched more bad designs die in paragraph three of their own proposals than in any review meeting.

The oldest evidence here predates the pandemic by decades. The Linux kernel and Postgres, two of the most reliable pieces of software humans have produced, were built by people who mostly never shared a room, arguing on mailing lists. Nobody calls pgsql-hackers a compromise forced on its members by geography. The archive is the project's memory, and you can go read a 2009 argument about the visibility map today.

Then there's interruption, where the numbers were starker than I expected. I tracked my own calendar and commit history for a quarter in 2019 and again in 2021. In the office I averaged 1.3 uninterrupted blocks of two hours or longer per week. At home, seven. My meetings hadn't decreased much. What vanished was the tap on the shoulder, which the tapper experiences as thirty seconds and the tapped experiences as losing the entire structure of the problem they were holding in their head.

The honest cost is juniors, and I won't pretend otherwise. The first two engineers we hired remotely with under a year of experience struggled badly. One sat stuck on an environment problem for a day and a half because asking felt like an imposition when it meant typing a message to a stranger. We fixed it clumsily: a standing ninety-minute pairing block every day for the first three months, with a rotating senior, camera optional, no agenda. It worked, but it took deliberate effort that the office used to provide for free. Anyone who says remote mentorship happens on its own is selling something.

Still, I'd rather pay that cost on purpose than keep collecting the office's hidden ones. Our median time from pull request to first review dropped from nineteen hours to six, largely because review became the main form of conversation instead of a chore squeezed between conversations. We hired people in Lisbon, Denver, and Guadalajara whom we could never have convinced to move to Oakland.

Last spring the company's new COO floated a return to three days a week. I sent him the decisions folder and asked which of the 212 files he thought would have been written.
