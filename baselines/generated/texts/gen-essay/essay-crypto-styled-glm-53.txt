People keep telling me that quantum computing is about to destroy encryption, and I've stopped correcting them because the correction takes longer than most conversations. But the misunderstanding underneath that claim is worth correcting, because almost nobody—including most programmers—knows how public-key cryptography actually works, and the real mechanism is stranger and more beautiful than the magic the word "encryption" suggests.

Here is what most people imagine: two keys, one locks, one unlocks, like a padlock and its key. That picture isn't wrong exactly, but it's useless, because it doesn't explain why the padlock can't be reverse-engineered. If I hand you a physical padlock you can take it apart. If I hand you my public key, a string of a few thousand bits, you can stare at it forever and still not recover my private key, and the reason is that the connection between the two keys runs through a mathematical one-way street. The whole edifice—HTTPS, your bank login, Signal, the software updates on your laptop—rests on the fact that some things are easy to do and computationally brutal to undo.

Take RSA, still the workhorse of certificate authorities. Alice picks two enormous prime numbers, p and q. When I say enormous I mean it: a 2048-bit key means p and q are each around 1024 bits, so each is a number with roughly 300 decimal digits. She multiplies them together to get n, and publishes n along with an encryption exponent e. Anyone can encrypt a message to her by raising the message to the power e, modulo n. That "modulo n" is the crucial move: you do arithmetic that wraps around like a clock, and after wrapping a few thousand times, the original structure of your calculation is shredded beyond recognition.

Now the one-way street. Multiplying two 300-digit primes is something your phone does in microseconds. Factoring a 600-digit number into those primes is something the entire world's computing capacity cannot do in the lifetime of the universe. This isn't a proven impossibility—no one has proved that factoring is fundamentally hard—but after forty-plus years of the smartest number theorists alive taking swings at it, the problem has held. The fastest known general factoring algorithm, the Number Field Sieve, would still need longer than civilizations last to crack a properly generated 2048-bit RSA modulus. Your private key is just the knowledge of p and q, and every interesting operation you can do with it flows from that knowledge.

I want to dwell on how weird this is, because I've taught this material and watched the moment of disbelief arrive. RSA was invented in 1977 by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman at MIT, and the story worth remembering is the one about Martin Gardner's Scientific American column that August. Gardner printed a challenge message encrypted with a 129-digit modulus—a number later nicknamed RSA-129—and offered $100 to whoever broke it. It stood unbroken for seventeen years. It finally fell in 1994 to an eight-month effort coordinating about six hundred volunteers across twenty-plus countries, and only because factoring algorithms and机床 hardware had improved beyond what 1977 imagined. The lesson isn't that RSA is weak. The lesson is that "hard" in cryptography means hard relative to the resources a real adversary can assemble, and that the margin is enormous when the keys are chosen correctly.

Diffie-Hellman, the other pillar, works differently but exploits the same asymmetry. Diffie and Hellman published in 1976, and their key exchange is the most elegant thing in the field. Both parties agree on a large prime p and a number g. Alice picks a secret a and sends g^a mod p to Bob. Bob picks a secret b and sends g^b mod p to Alice. Alice raises what she received to her own secret exponent, getting (g^b)^a = g^(ab). Bob computes (g^a)^b = the same thing. They now share a secret that never traveled across the wire, and an eavesdropper who saw both transmissions faces the discrete logarithm problem: given g^a mod p, find a. Same story as factoring—computing the exponentiation is trivial, recovering the exponent is currently infeasible. When Whitfield Diffie explained this to me at a conference years ago, he described it as "two people mixing paint," and the analogy holds: mixing paint is easy, unmixing it is not.

Elliptic-curve cryptography does the same discrete-logarithm trick in a more exotic algebraic setting, and the practical consequence is that keys get smaller. A 256-bit elliptic curve key gives security roughly comparable to a 3072-bit RSA key. This is why your phone's key exchanges are fast and why Bitcoin—whatever you think of it—runs on curves rather than factoring.

Now the honest caveats, because this is where I have opinions. Public-key cryptography is not the thing that encrypts your traffic. RSA and Diffie-Hellman are slow, a thousand-odd times slower than a good symmetric cipher like AES. In real protocols, public-key crypto is used briefly, at the beginning, to agree on a fresh symmetric key, and everything after that uses AES. The public-key part is the elaborate handshake that lets two strangers who never met agree on a secret in the open, with adversaries listening to every byte. That's the actual miracle. Symmetric encryption is old; Caesar did a crude version. What was new in 1976 and 1977 was establishing a shared secret through a public channel at all. Before that, every serious cryptographic system required physically distributing keys in advance—couriers, diplomatic pouches, the whole apparatus of pre-shared trust. The 2016 documentary-style histories make a fuss about GCHQ's Ellis, Cocks, and Williamson having invented the same ideas years earlier in secret; it's true, but classified inventions that nobody can use are a philosophical curiosity at best. What matters is that Diffie, Hellman, Merkle, and the RSA trio published, and publishing is what created the modern Internet.

And the quantum thing, since I opened with it? Shor's algorithm does factor integers and solve discrete logarithms efficiently on a sufficiently large quantum computer, so yes, RSA and Diffie-Hellman die on that day. But a quantum computer able to run Shor against real keys needs thousands of well-behaved logical qubits, and the largest honest demonstrations today involve machines that can't come close. Meanwhile the field has spent a decade standardizing replacements—lattice-based schemes like Kyber and Dilithium, now deployed in browsers by Cloudflare and Google—that rest on different one-way streets, problems involving high-dimensional lattices that even quantum computers have no known shortcut through. The panic is misplaced; the migration is underway and mostly boring, which is how good cryptographic engineering should look.

The deep point survives all the implementation churn. Civilization now relies on specific mathematical facts—that factoring and discrete logarithms are hard, and that "hard" has survived decades of adversarial scrutiny. Every time you see the little padlock in your browser you're looking at a bet on the enduring difficulty of certain arithmetic. It's the best bet in technological history, and I'd take it again.
