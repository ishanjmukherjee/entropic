The cost of building a kilometer of subway varies so dramatically across the world that the figures can seem almost implausible. New York City's Second Avenue Subway, completed in 2017, cost roughly $2.5 billion per kilometer. Istanbul, by contrast, has built metro lines for as little as $100 million per kilometer. Seoul, Madrid, and Paris all manage costs that are a fraction of what American or British cities pay. This enormous gap cannot be explained by any single factor; it is the product of interacting institutional, political, regulatory, and design choices that compound over the life of a project.

Labor costs are an obvious starting point, but they explain far less than people assume. Yes, wages for construction workers and engineers are higher in New York or London than in Istanbul or Delhi. However, labor typically accounts for only a portion of total project costs, and the difference in wages between, say, the United States and Germany is not large enough to explain why German subway projects cost a quarter as much per kilometer. Moreover, some high-cost cities achieve relatively low construction costs, suggesting that labor expense alone is not determinative.

Station design and scope are among the most significant drivers of cost variation. American and British subway stations tend to be enormous, with deep mezzanines, multiple entrances, extensive ventilation systems, and elaborate finishes. A single station on the Second Avenue Subway cost more than entire metro lines in some countries. In contrast, cities like Madrid and Seoul have standardized their station designs, using repeatable templates that reduce engineering time, simplify construction, and allow contractors to build efficiently through repetition. When every station is a bespoke architectural project, costs escalate rapidly.

Regulatory environments also play a crucial role. In many countries, environmental reviews, community consultations, and permitting processes can add years to a project's timeline, and time is money. The United States requires extensive environmental impact statements under the National Environmental Policy Act, a process that can take half a decade or more before construction even begins. Each additional year of planning, legal review, and public comment adds carrying costs, requires updated studies, and creates opportunities for litigation that further delay work. In countries with more streamlined approval processes, projects move from conception to construction much faster, reducing both soft costs and the risk of political interference.

Land acquisition is another major factor. In cities with strong property rights and high real estate values, purchasing or condemning the land needed for stations, ventilation shafts, and construction staging areas can be extraordinarily expensive and time-consuming. South Korea and China, by contrast, have legal frameworks that make it easier for the government to acquire land for public infrastructure. This does not mean the process is painless or always fair, but it does reduce one of the most unpredictable and costly phases of a project.

Perhaps the most underappreciated factor is institutional capacity and project management. Cities that build subways regularly develop deep expertise. They maintain skilled teams of engineers, project managers, and procurement specialists who learn from each project and carry that knowledge forward. Madrid, for example, built an extraordinary expansion of its metro network in the 1990s and 2000s, and the institutional knowledge gained during that period kept costs low even as the network grew. Cities that build rarely, like New York, lose that expertise between projects. They must rely on outside consultants, face a steeper learning curve, and often lack the internal capacity to hold contractors accountable.

Corruption and political dysfunction also inflate costs, though their effects are sometimes overstated. In some countries, kickbacks, patronage hiring, and fraudulent contracting directly siphon money from projects. In others, the problem is more subtle: political leaders may demand changes mid-construction to satisfy constituents, or they may split contracts among multiple firms for political rather than efficiency reasons. Both forms of interference disrupt the construction process and drive up costs.

Geology matters, but less than one might expect. Hard rock can make tunneling expensive, but modern tunnel boring machines handle a wide range of conditions. Cities built on soft clay or below the water table face real engineering challenges, yet many such cities still build subways at reasonable costs. Geology is a constraint, but it is rarely the decisive one.

The cumulative effect of these factors is what produces the staggering differences in final costs. No single reform—cheaper labor, faster permits, smaller stations—would close the gap on its own. The cities that build subways affordably have typically addressed most of these issues simultaneously: they standardize design, streamline approvals, maintain institutional expertise, and manage contracts effectively. The cities that struggle tend to do the opposite, layering complexity upon complexity until a project that should cost hundreds of millions ends up costing billions. Understanding subway costs, then, is ultimately an exercise in understanding how institutions function—or fail—under pressure.
