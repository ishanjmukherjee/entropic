The Erie Canal is the most misleading success in American history. Everything said in its praise is true. It ran 363 miles from Albany to Buffalo, climbed 568 feet through 83 locks, cost a little over $7 million, and earned that back in tolls within about a decade of opening in 1825. It cut the cost of moving a ton of freight from Buffalo to New York City from roughly $100 to under $10. Rochester had about 1,500 people in 1820 and more than 9,000 ten years later, most of them connected in some way to flour. I don't dispute any of it. My quarrel is with what the country decided it meant.

What it meant, to nearly everyone watching, was that a state government could borrow heavily, dig a ditch, and get rich. Madison had vetoed federal money for internal improvements on his last full day in office in March 1817, so New York went ahead alone, and the triumph looked like a triumph of method. It was a triumph of location. The Appalachians run from Alabama to Maine, and in that whole wall there is one place where you can get from tidewater to the Great Lakes basin without going over a mountain: the Mohawk Valley, a trough the glaciers left behind. Rome, where they broke ground on July 4, 1817, sits only about 430 feet above the sea. The commissioners started there, in the middle, because the digging was easy and they needed good news quickly. One level west of Utica ran nearly seventy miles without a single lock.

This is also why the canal's famous amateurism worked. Benjamin Wright and James Geddes, the principal engineers, were country judges who had learned surveying to settle land disputes. Canvass White paid his own way to England, walked some two thousand miles of towpath taking notes, and came home to find a limestone near Chittenango that made cement which hardened underwater. I love these men. But the story we tell about them, that pluck and a spirit level were enough, leaves out that they were putting a four-foot-deep channel through terrain that had been graded for them ten thousand years earlier. Where the land did resist, the job turned ugly fast. In the Montezuma marshes in the summer of 1819, something like a thousand laborers went down with fever. At Lockport, Nathan Roberts had to blast a double flight of five locks up the face of the Niagara Escarpment, and it took years. Those were the exceptions on the Erie. Everywhere else in America they were the rule.

Pennsylvania learned this at ruinous expense. Terrified of losing the western trade, Philadelphia pushed through the Main Line of Public Works, which had to cross the Allegheny Front at around 2,300 feet. The solution was the Allegheny Portage Railroad: ten inclined planes, stationary steam engines, and canal boats built in sections so they could be hauled over the summit on railcars. It was ingenious and it was absurd. In 1857 the state sold the whole works to the Pennsylvania Railroad for $7.5 million, far less than it had sunk into them. Maryland's Chesapeake and Ohio Canal broke ground on July 4, 1828, the same day as the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, and reached Cumberland in 1850, eight years after the trains did. It never got anywhere near the Ohio. Indiana, with a population still clearing stumps, passed its Mammoth Internal Improvement Act in 1836 and authorized $10 million in borrowing for canals and turnpikes in every county whose legislator wanted one.

The bill arrived after the Panic of 1837. By 1842 eight states and the Florida Territory had defaulted on their debts. English investors had bought the bonds because New York's had been so good, and the Reverend Sydney Smith, who held Pennsylvania paper, wrote that he could not sit at dinner beside a Pennsylvanian without wanting to seize his coat and boots and divide them among the creditors.

Then came the second misreading, an overcorrection that I think did more lasting harm than the first. Through the 1840s and 1850s, state after state rewrote its constitution to forbid or hobble public borrowing. New York's 1846 constitution required a statewide referendum for long-term debt, and that rule is still in force. The states did not stop borrowing. They invented the public authority, a body that issues bonds the state pretends not to owe, run by people nobody elected, with accounts few citizens could read. Robert Moses built an empire in that loophole. Every opaque bridge-and-tunnel agency in the country descends from a debt panic that descends from a canal mania that descends from one valley being mistaken for a principle.

Go to Little Falls, where the Mohawk gorge narrows to a few hundred yards. The barge canal runs through it. So does the old New York Central main line, now CSX, and so do state routes 5 and 5S, with the Thruway close by on the southern slope. Two centuries of builders, with different money and different machines, all came through the same gap.
