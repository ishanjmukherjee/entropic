Subway construction in the United States costs far more per kilometer than in many other countries. New York's Second Avenue Subway, whose first phase opened in 2017, cost billions of dollars per kilometer. Comparable projects in Madrid, Paris, and Seoul have often come in at a fraction of that price, and Chinese cities such as Shanghai and Chengdu have built lines for a small share of the American figure. The gap is real, and it is not explained by any single factor. It reflects differences in labor costs, regulation, political incentives, construction know-how, and the everyday habits of the agencies that build and govern transit.

Labor is the first explanation people reach for, and it matters, but it is not the whole story. Tunneling is labor-intensive, and wages in New York are high. Yet labor accounts for a smaller share of subway budgets than many assume. Much of the money goes to materials, equipment, engineering, design, and administration. A project can be expensive even when workers are paid moderately, if it requires a long chain of consultants, lawyers, and contractors, each taking a cut of the budget. Labor costs explain part of the difference between American and European prices, but they cannot account for the gap between American and Asian ones alone.

Regulation and process explain much more. In the United States, a major transit project often requires environmental impact reviews, historic preservation assessments, community consultations, and public hearings, each of which can add years and millions of dollars. Litigation is common, and every lawsuit creates delay, and delay raises costs through inflation and redesign. Projects also face overlapping jurisdictions, with city, state, and federal agencies each imposing their own requirements. Each rule may be defensible on its own terms, but together they make planning slow and expensive. Other countries have their own reviews, but many run them more quickly and with fewer opportunities to stall a project indefinitely.

Design choices also drive costs upward. American stations are often generous in size, with mezzanines, wide platforms, and extensive accessibility features such as elevators, which are required under the Americans with Disabilities Act. These are worthwhile goals, but they add expense. Many European and Asian systems use simpler, more standardized station designs, repeated across the network. Standardization lowers engineering costs and speeds construction, because crews and contractors learn a single template rather than reinventing each station. When every station is a bespoke project, every station carries bespoke costs.

Construction methods matter too. Some lines are built by cut-and-cover, digging a trench from the surface and then covering it, which disrupts streets and businesses. Others use tunnel boring machines that dig deep underground with less surface disruption. The choice depends on geology, city density, and political tolerance for disruption. Countries that have built many tunnels develop specialized expertise, equipment, and supplier networks. Experience compounds: the tenth line built by a crew is usually cheaper than the first. The United States built few subways for decades, so its engineering capacity atrophied, and each new project effectively restarts the learning curve.

Political incentives shape all of this. Transit projects are large, visible, and often spread across many districts to win political support, which can produce fragmented routes and stations placed for political rather than engineering reasons. Authorities that are shielded from short electoral cycles, or that have stable funding, can plan more coherently. Where a single agency or state-owned company controls planning and construction, it can negotiate prices, bundle contracts, and resist scope creep more effectively than a fragmented system of overlapping authorities.

Finally, there is the question of accountability and cost transparency. Some low-cost countries have been criticized for hidden expenses, weak safety oversight, or inflated prices in other forms. Chinese cost figures in particular are difficult to verify, and comparisons are complicated by currency conversions, differing project scopes, and what is included in the reported total. Still, even after adjusting for these caveats, the gap remains large enough to suggest that the problem lies less in what subways fundamentally require and more in how societies choose to build them.

The lesson is not that America must copy any particular country. It is that subway costs are shaped by choices about rules, designs, institutions, and priorities. A city that wants cheaper transit must be willing to streamline reviews, standardize its designs, build consistently enough to develop expertise, and give its agencies clear authority and stable funding. Until those choices change, the subway will remain expensive wherever those conditions are missing.
