When Google announced that certified Android devices would stop installing apps from developers who hadn't registered their identity with Google, the reaction split along familiar lines. Google framed it as a security measure. It cited its finding that apps sideloaded from the internet carry far more malware than those from the Play Store, and it pointed to scam campaigns in Southeast Asia where victims are talked into installing fake banking apps. Critics framed it as a power grab: the end of Android's claim to openness, a threat to F-Droid and independent developers, and a convenient way to entrench Google's control just as courts and regulators were pushing it to loosen that control.

Both sides have a point. Neither offers much of a theory. The debate mostly trades anecdotes about scammed grandparents for slogans about owning your device. Each side treats its framing as self-evidently decisive. What's missing is an account of what exactly is being regulated, who should be doing the regulating, and what counts as an acceptable cost.

Start with what is being gated. The policy does not review app content. It verifies developer identity. That distinction matters, and both sides tend to blur it. Identity verification is closer to a business license than to censorship. It makes bad actors traceable without deciding in advance what software is allowed. Apple's notarization on macOS is the nearest precedent, and few people regard the Mac as a locked-down appliance. That is partly because the Mac lets a determined user override the check. So the real question isn't whether Android should verify developers. It's how costly the exit is for someone who wants to run unverified code. Google's later concession of an "advanced flow" for experienced users suggests it understands this. But the whole argument turns on details that are rarely discussed explicitly. How many steps does the override take? Does it reset after updates? Can it be done without a computer? Will it survive the next policy revision?

Second, there is the threat model. The scam scenario Google describes is unusual. The victim is not tricked by a malicious app hiding in a store. She is coached in real time, often by phone, by someone impersonating a bank or the police, into deliberately bypassing warnings. This is the hard case for the "let users decide" position. The user is deciding, just under manipulation. But it is also an awkward case for Google's remedy. A scammer with a stolen or purchased identity can register as a developer. Verification raises the cost of fraud and improves attribution after the fact. It does not stop a coerced installation in the moment. If coercion is the problem, the natural tools are friction designed for coercion: cooling-off delays, or blocking installs during active phone calls. Developer registration addresses a somewhat different problem, and the debate rarely asks whether the means fit the stated end.

Third, there is the question of who holds the registry. Even granting that some identity layer is useful, it does not follow that one company should run it, least of all a company that also runs the dominant app store and competes with many of the developers it will be verifying. The web solved an analogous problem imperfectly but instructively. Certificate authorities are plural, browsers can distrust them, and no single firm's commercial interests determine who may run a website. A theory of Android verification would ask whether the registrar should be neutral, contestable, or multiple. It would ask what due process a developer gets when their registration is refused or revoked. Today these look like product decisions. They are better understood as governance decisions.

Fourth, there is anonymity. Some valuable software is written by people who cannot safely attach their legal names to it, such as censorship-circumvention tools, security research, and apps for dissidents. A registry held by a company subject to many governments' legal demands is a list someone will eventually subpoena. This concern is not hypothetical, and it is entirely separate from the malware question. It needs its own answer rather than being folded into a general complaint about openness.

Put these together and a more useful frame emerges. A device policy like this one should be judged on a few axes. How expensive and how durable is the user's override? Does the remedy match the actual threat? Is the gatekeeper neutral and accountable? What happens to people who need to publish without being identified? A policy could do well on some of these and badly on others. Verification with a cheap, durable override, a neutral registrar, and a pseudonymous tier would be very different from verification with a buried, fragile override controlled entirely by a store operator. Yet the current discourse treats "developer verification" as a single thing to be for or against.

The stakes are large. Android is the main computing device for billions of people, many of whom will never own anything else. Whether that device behaves like a general-purpose computer or like an appliance licensed by its manufacturer is one of the more consequential questions in technology policy. It deserves better than a contest between the scammed grandmother and the hobbyist with a custom ROM. Both are real. What we lack is a principled way of weighing them, and until we have one, the outcome will be set by whoever controls the defaults.
