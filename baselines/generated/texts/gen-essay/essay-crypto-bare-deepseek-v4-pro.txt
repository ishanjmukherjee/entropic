The fundamental problem of secure communication lies in the exchange of a secret key. For centuries, two parties wishing to communicate privately needed to first meet in person or use a trusted courier to share a common key, which they would then use to encrypt and decrypt their messages. This system, known as symmetric-key cryptography, has a critical flaw: the secure channel is required before any secure communication can occur over an insecure channel. The invention of public-key cryptography in the 1970s solved this seemingly intractable key-distribution problem in a way that is both mathematically profound and practically revolutionary. It does not rely on a single shared secret, but on a pair of mathematically linked keys that are asymmetric: a public key, which can be broadcast to the world, and a private key, which remains a closely guarded secret.

The core analogy for understanding this system is that of a mailbox. The public key is like an open mailing address to which anyone can send a letter. The act of sending a letter is equivalent to encrypting a message with that public key. Once the letter is dropped into the mailbox, it is physically impossible for the sender, or anyone else without the proper tool, to retrieve it through the narrow slot. However, the mailbox’s owner possesses the private key—the physical key that unlocks the back door. This private key, and only this key, can open the box and retrieve the accumulated messages. In this elegant model, the public key encrypts, and the private key decrypts. The security relies not on the secrecy of the encryption process but on the practical impossibility of deriving the private key from its public counterpart.

This asymmetry is made possible by a class of mathematical functions known as one-way functions. A one-way function is easy to compute in one direction but computationally infeasible to reverse. A classic analogy is mixing two cans of paint: combining a can of yellow paint and a can of blue paint to make green is trivially easy, but trying to separate the green paint back into its original yellow and blue components is practically impossible. Public-key cryptography is built on mathematical one-way functions that possess a hidden shortcut—a trapdoor. This trapdoor is the private key. Without it, an adversary is left with an impossible unmixing problem, even though they possess all the public information.

The most famous and widely implemented algorithm that embodies this principle is the RSA algorithm, named after its inventors, Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman. Its security is based on the one-way nature of multiplying two large prime numbers. The process begins with the generation of a key pair. First, two very large prime numbers, conventionally called p and q, are chosen at random. These are the secret ingredients. Multiplying them together is the easy, forward-direction step, yielding their product, N = p × q. The number N is the modulus and forms a part of both the public and private keys. The crucial point is that while a computer can multiply p and q in a fraction of a second, given only the product N, the process of discovering the original factors p and q—known as prime factorization—is a staggeringly difficult computational problem for which no efficient method exists for numbers that are hundreds of digits long.

The next step involves creating the actual keys using N and another value derived from p and q. The public key consists of N and an encryption exponent, e, which is usually a small, fixed prime number like 65,537. The private key is a corresponding decryption exponent, d, which satisfies a specific mathematical relationship with e and the original primes p and q. The derivation of d from e, p, and q is straightforward for the key’s creator. A user who wants to receive a secret message makes their public key (N, e) widely available, while their private key d is stored securely and never shared. The beauty of the system is that the mathematical relationship allows a message encrypted with the public key (N, e) to be decrypted only by the corresponding private key d. An eavesdropper who intercepts the public key knows N and e, but to calculate the private key d, they must first factor N to recover p and q. The entire security of the system rests on the computational difficulty of this factorization task.

To encrypt a plaintext message, the sender first converts it into a number, m, that is smaller than N. They then compute the ciphertext, c, by taking m to the power of e and finding the remainder when divided by N—a modular exponentiation operation expressed as c = m^e mod N. This is the mathematical equivalent of dropping the message into the mailbox. The ciphertext c can now be sent over any insecure channel. To decrypt it, the recipient takes c, raises it to the power of their private key d, and finds the remainder when divided by N: m = c^d mod N. The underlying number theory, rooted in Euler’s theorem, guarantees that this modular exponentiation perfectly reverses the encryption, recovering the original message m. Anyone without d is faced with a discrete logarithm-like problem within the modular arithmetic that is computationally intractable.

This core functionality of encryption is only one half of what public-key cryptography enables. By reversing the operation, it creates a powerful digital signature. If a user encrypts a message—or more commonly, a cryptographic hash of a message—with their private key, anyone in possession of the corresponding public key can decrypt it and verify its source. This process, expressed as s = m^d mod N and verified by checking if m = s^e mod N, provides non-repudiation, proving that the message could only have been sent by the holder of the private key. Just as the mailbox system allows anyone to send a private message that only the owner can read, the signature system allows the owner to create a verifiable mark that anyone can authenticate but no one can forge.

Public-key cryptography solves the ancient problem of key distribution by replacing shared secrecy with mathematical asymmetry. Its security is not absolute but computational; it relies on the current limits of mathematics and processing power to make reversing the one-way function impractical. This breakthrough transformed the digital world, providing the secure foundation for everything from e-commerce and encrypted web traffic to software updates and blockchain technology, all resting on the simple yet profound ability to share a lock while keeping the key.
