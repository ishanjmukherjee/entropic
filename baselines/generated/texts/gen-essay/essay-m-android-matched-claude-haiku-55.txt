Google's plan to require developers of Android apps to register with the company, even for software installed outside the Play Store, has drawn fierce criticism. Critics have called it a threat to open computing, a gift to surveillance, and a corporate power grab. Google has answered that the requirement will reduce malware and scams, that power users keep a path to install unverified software, and that most people will never notice. Both sides argue hard, but the argument rarely gets to the question underneath: what is a phone, and who gets to decide what runs on it? Without a clear answer to that, the debate is mostly a clash of intuitions.

The defenders' case is easy to state. Sideloading is the main channel through which mobile malware reaches ordinary people. Scammers impersonate banks and government agencies, and they persuade victims to install fake apps that read text messages and capture one-time passcodes. A verification gate, the argument goes, raises the cost of distributing such software at scale, much as code signing did for desktop operating systems. Who could object to that?

The trouble is that this argument proves too much and too little. It proves too much because nearly any restriction on general-purpose computing can be justified by the harm it prevents. It proves too little because it says nothing about the structure of the authority being exercised. A security benefit tells us that a rule is useful. It does not tell us whether the party writing the rule should be the one who writes it, or whether the rule should be enforced in a way that cannot be overridden by the person who owns the device. Security is a property of a system. Legitimacy is a property of the relationship among the parties in it. The debate keeps answering the first question while the second goes unexamined.

Critics do better when they invoke ownership and autonomy, but they often treat these as obvious goods rather than as concepts needing defense. "It's my phone" is a powerful intuition, but it leaves open what ownership of a computing device actually includes. Does owning a device mean controlling its software, or only its hardware? Does a manufacturer's continuing role in updating, signing, and distributing software create standing that survives the sale? If a company can revoke the ability to install software on a device it sold, is that still a sale in any meaningful sense? These are old questions in property and contract law, and the answers have never been settled. Android's situation makes them sharper, because the phone is both a personal object and a node in a platform controlled by a single company.

A useful theory would also explain why the same policy looks different depending on where you stand. For a developer in a country with weak legal institutions, a registration requirement is not an abstract risk; it is a condition of reaching users at all, and Google becomes a gatekeeper with no meaningful appeal. For a user, the policy is an invisible default that becomes visible only at the edges, in warnings, friction, and the advanced settings most people will never find. For regulators, the question is whether a dominant platform's rule-making is a form of private governance that deserves public scrutiny. A serious account would say how these perspectives relate. It would tell us which harms count, whose consent matters, and what an acceptable compromise would look like, rather than just listing grievances.

Such a theory would probably have to distinguish between control that protects a user from third parties and control that protects a platform from competition or accountability. A verification scheme that stops a fraudster from impersonating a bank is one thing. A verification scheme that also lets the platform decide which developers are commercially tolerable is another, even if the same technical mechanism serves both. The debate tends to blur these, because the same mechanism can be described in either vocabulary, and each side picks the one that suits it.

None of this means the policy is obviously wrong. Perhaps the security gains justify the loss of autonomy, and perhaps a well-designed advanced path preserves enough freedom. But that conclusion needs to be argued from premises about ownership, authority, and the proper role of dominant platforms, not assumed from the fact that malware is bad or that freedom is good. Until those premises are stated and defended, the debate will keep producing the same exchange: a warning about security, a complaint about control, and no shared way to decide which one should win. The Android lockdown deserves better than that, and the first step is to ask what kind of thing a phone is supposed to be.
