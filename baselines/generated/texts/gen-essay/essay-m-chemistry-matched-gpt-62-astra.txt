When coal burns, carbon atoms join with oxygen atoms to make carbon dioxide. The carbon is still carbon, and the oxygen is still oxygen. A chemist could, with enough energy, separate them again. Yet when a particular kind of uranium atom undergoes radioactive decay, what remains is an atom of thorium. No chemical analysis will turn up the missing uranium, because it is not missing. It has changed its identity. Learning how that could happen meant revising an idea on which chemistry had been built: that, whatever else might change in nature, the elements themselves endured.

The distinction lies in which part of the atom takes part in a reaction. An atom has a tiny nucleus, containing protons and usually neutrons, surrounded by electrons. In ordinary chemistry, electrons may be shared, transferred, or rearranged as bonds form and break. The nuclei remain intact. In a nuclear reaction, the nucleus itself changes. Since the number of protons defines the element, a change in that number produces a different element. A change in the number of neutrons alone produces a different isotope of the same element. Nuclear changes can release far more energy per atom than chemical ones because the energies that bind nuclei are so much greater than those involved in chemical bonds.

One of the first clues came in 1896, when the French physicist Henri Becquerel found that uranium salts could darken a photographic plate through opaque wrapping. He had expected sunlight to be needed to excite the salts, but plates kept with them in a dark drawer showed a strong image. Marie Curie pursued the puzzle by measuring the radiation from different substances. Its intensity depended on how much uranium was present, not on the chemical compound in which it was bound. The source seemed to lie within the atom. Working with Pierre Curie, she identified two other radioactive elements, polonium and radium.

In the early twentieth century, Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy explained radioactivity as the spontaneous transformation of one kind of atom into another. No one could predict when a particular unstable atom would decay, but the time required for half of a large sample to decay was remarkably dependable. This “half-life” turned an apparent irregularity into a clock. Geologists eventually learned to read it in minerals, using the relative amounts of radioactive atoms and their decay products to date rocks far older than any surviving fossil.

People also learned to bring nuclear changes about deliberately. In 1919 Rutherford reported that bombarding nitrogen with alpha particles produced hydrogen nuclei; the nitrogen had been transformed into oxygen. In the 1930s, experiments with neutrons led to a more startling result. Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium among the products of neutron bombardment of uranium. For chemists, the result was hard to dismiss: the material behaved like barium in test after test. For physicists, it was hard to explain. Barium was much lighter than uranium. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch recognized what the chemical evidence implied: the uranium nucleus had split. They called the process fission.

Understanding fission brought a question of control. Fission could release neutrons that caused further fissions, sustaining a chain reaction. In Chicago in 1942, a team led by Enrico Fermi demonstrated that such a reaction could be maintained at a controlled rate. The wartime effort also produced the atomic bombs used against Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, with catastrophic human consequences. In the following decade, reactors began supplying electricity. Despite the novelty of their fuel, most nuclear power stations would have looked familiar to an engineer from the age of coal: heat makes steam, and steam drives a turbine. What differs is the source of the heat, and with it the particular demands of radiation protection and long-term waste management.

Some of the most useful applications depend not on a large release of energy but on a signal faint enough to come from a minute amount of material. A physician can give a patient a compound containing a radioactive isotope and follow its passage through the body with a detector. Ordinary chemistry helps determine where the compound goes; nuclear physics makes its journey visible. In a thyroid scan, for example, the gland collects radioactive iodine just as it collects other iodine, revealing which regions are unusually active. The technique works precisely because a nuclear difference need not make much chemical difference at all.

Nuclear science did not overturn the chemist’s account of burning coal. It explained why that account works and where it stops. For the purposes of a flame, an iron nail rusting in the rain, or a meal being digested, the elements are stable participants, exchanged among compounds but not themselves remade. Inside a star, by contrast, fusion builds helium from hydrogen and releases the energy that ultimately reaches Earth as sunlight. Even the coal fire owes its light to both kinds of change: ancient plants stored sunlight in chemical bonds, but the sunlight began in the changing nuclei of the Sun.
