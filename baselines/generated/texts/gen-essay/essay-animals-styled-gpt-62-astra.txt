If I run my thumbnail along the middle of a mussel shell, I can feel the winters. Growth slows in cold water and leaves a ridge. It isn’t a reliable way to tell the animal’s age, since a nasty spell in June can leave a mark too, but it is a good cure for the impression, encouraged by a bowl of moules marinières, that a mussel is a little packet of food which grew its own crockery. This one had a winter. I am going to eat it anyway. I think that is defensible. I don’t think eating a chicken is.

My rule is that I won’t have an animal killed for supper if there is good reason to think it can mind what happens to it. By “mind” I mean something considerably more modest than having plans for Thursday. A chicken with a damaged leg will choose feed containing carprofen, an anti-inflammatory painkiller, over ordinary feed; sound birds show no comparable preference. The lame birds eat more of the medicated stuff as their lameness gets worse. This experiment, published by T. C. Danbury and colleagues in 2000, did more to settle the chicken question for me than any amount of footage from a processing plant. Here was a bird going about the small, intelligent business of getting some relief. I find I can’t take its interest in that enterprise seriously and its interest in staying alive lightly.

That last step is where several of my friends get off. Granted the leg, they say; fix the leg. Give the bird a yard, a dust bath, some shade under a currant bush. Then take it off its perch one night and kill it before it knows anything about it. Where exactly is the injury? My answer is: in the morning. There would have been scratching under that currant bush in the morning. The bird needn’t have formed an intention about the bush for the scratching to have been worth something to it. Otherwise sleep would perform a remarkable piece of moral laundry on us all every night.

I have more patience with the farmer’s objection that without the prospect of a sale there would have been no bird at all. This is true, and peculiarly uncomfortable for those of us who like the look of a field with hens in it. We would like the farmer to go on providing our view while we purchase lentils. Still, I can’t see how bringing a creature into existence gives me credit to draw on against its subsequent interests. If a farmer keeps one hen past laying age because his daughter is fond of her, nobody thinks he has cheated the hypothetical pullet that could have occupied her square of floor. I’m for the hen on the floor.

Fish make this position socially inconvenient. At a wedding there is invariably somebody prepared to convey to the kitchen, in the tone of a diplomat who has secured a difficult concession, that I will eat the salmon. I won’t. Watch a grouper hunting with a moray eel: the grouper shimmies its head to recruit the eel, which can get into reef crevices the grouper can’t. I don’t require a salmon to pass a grouper’s exam. I mention it because our ordinary estimate of what goes on inside a fish seems to derive chiefly from the fact that a dead one has much the same facial expression as a live one.

Why give the mussel the benefit of so little doubt? It has ganglia. It closes up when disturbed. Neither fact establishes that there is anything it feels like to be a mussel; neither establishes the contrary. What tips me is the poverty of the evidence for experience, taken together with what actually replaces a meal of mussels. A rope of farmed blue mussels needs no feed crop. The animals filter their dinner out of the passing water. A field of peas, by contrast, is a place from which a farmer must contrive to exclude a great many other pea eaters. I’m not going to produce a spurious balance sheet of dead weevils per helping. I do think the confident claim that the pea is the merciful choice owes something to the convenient invisibility of everything that happened before it reached the freezer.

There’s a dirty little aesthetic cost to this rule. I miss roast chicken, particularly the rag of salty skin between the thigh and the body, which comes away on the carving knife and never makes it to anybody’s plate. I don’t miss bacon nearly as much, whatever the evangelists for bacon might like to hear. When I scrub mussels at the sink I sometimes find a tiny crab wedged among the byssal threads, all elbows, trying to make itself smaller. Crabs learn to avoid places where they have received shocks, even when those places offer the dark shelter they ordinarily seek. Out it comes. It is a ridiculous business, standing over two pounds of animals destined for the pot, fussing over the fate of something the size of a shirt button. I would find it more ridiculous to drop it in for the sake of consistency.
