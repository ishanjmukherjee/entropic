For the first seven weeks I lived in Philadelphia, I kept a tape measure in the pocket of my winter coat. I was looking for a bookcase thirty-one inches wide, the distance between the radiator and the doorframe after allowing for the knob. This gave me something to do on Saturdays besides call my sister in Minneapolis and listen to her unload the dishwasher. I went to every secondhand shop within reach of the 47 bus. I learned where to get off for a good hoagie. I found a man on South Street who would resole a pair of boots for less than the boots had cost. I could conduct an entire Saturday without consulting my phone. At the time I mistook this for progress.

What I should have done was agree, much earlier, to take out somebody else’s garbage. My admittedly narrow theory of moving is that you don’t live in a city until somebody there has a practical reason to be annoyed with you. Affection is lovely, but you can manufacture quite a bit of it in a weekend. It’s harder to counterfeit being the person who was supposed to put the cans out.

I had been good at this in Minneapolis without knowing it was a skill. I had a key to my sister’s bungalow. Every November I went over with a little brass fitting to blow out the line to her backyard tap, a job I resented because she invariably asked me to do it on a Sunday when the Vikings had the early game. In Philadelphia my Sundays were mine in a way they hadn’t been since I was nineteen. By the fourth one I would have paid cash for a woman to stand at a screen door saying, “Are you sure you got it all?”

Downstairs from me lived a retired court stenographer named Adele, who kept her blue recycling bin in the vestibule because, as she explained, she had already bought that bin twice. One Thursday she knocked while I was frying an egg. She’d had an injection in her right shoulder. Could I carry the bin to the curb? I put down the spatula and did it with the conspicuous good cheer of a man who hopes to be invited to dinner. She said, “The other one too,” and went inside.

Her shoulder took six weeks to improve. By then Thursday night was my night. This was a rowhouse cut into three apartments, and the boy on the third floor generated an astonishing volume of rigid plastic from a diet consisting largely, as far as I could tell, of supermarket sushi. I learned to nest the trays. I learned that Adele drank a bottle of inexpensive Spanish red most weeks and an expensive Oregon pinot the week her daughter visited. This struck me as intimate information to possess about a woman whose first name I’d learned off a misdelivered gas bill.

In April I spent a Thursday night in Baltimore for work. When I got home Friday the vestibule smelled of shrimp tails. “You could’ve said,” Adele told me. I started to explain that I’d gone straight to the train from the office, as if the relevant question were whether I’d physically passed the garbage on my way out of town. Halfway through I heard what she was saying. I could have told her on Wednesday. It hadn’t occurred to me that my movements were of consequence to anyone within a hundred miles.

There are more flattering methods of introducing yourself to a place. You can join a choir and let people discover you have a decent tenor. You can give a dinner party where the chicken comes out at precisely the moment you choose. What I recommend about garbage, particularly to people like me who move in their thirties and arrive with a pretty well-rehearsed account of themselves, is the absence of any provision for showing off. Nobody on our block came to the window to see how beautifully I had flattened a carton. When I screwed up, on the other hand, there were shrimp tails.

The bookcase turned up in June, on the sidewalk two doors down. Twenty-nine inches, pine, with a crescent of purple candle wax on the middle shelf. I had one end up the stoop when Adele came out and took the other, using the shoulder I’d understood to be permanently out of commission. We got it upstairs by removing the bottom drawer of my stove, which projected just far enough into the passage to catch a corner. She knew to do this because she had helped the previous tenant move a desk out. When we set it down she took one look at the radiator and said I’d cook the glue out of the binding of every book on the bottom shelf. I said I’d allowed two inches. She gave me the look my sister gives me when I tell her I’ve got all the water out of the line. I keep the tape measure on that shelf now, and a pair of pliers Adele sometimes sends the sushi boy up to borrow.
