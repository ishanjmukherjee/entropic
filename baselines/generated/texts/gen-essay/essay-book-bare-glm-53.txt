There are novels that arrive fully canonized, their reputations secured by prizes and syllabi and the quiet consensus of the culture, and then there are novels that seem to have slipped through history's fingers—not because they failed, but because they fell into the gap between categories, too strange for one shelf and too modest for another. Jean Stafford's "The Mountain Lion" (1947) is one of these, and it deserves better than the near-oblivion it currently enjoys.

Stafford is remembered today, when she is remembered at all, for her short stories, which won the Pulitzer Prize in 1970, and perhaps for the biographical fact of her first marriage to the poet Robert Lowell, which cast her permanently as a footnote in someone else's legend. This is a distortion of her talent. "The Mountain Lion," her second novel, is a ferocious, funny, and finally devastating book about childhood, and it stands comparison with the acknowledged masterpieces of that terrain: "Catcher in the Rye," published four years later, looks almost sentimental next to it.

The novel follows ten-year-old Ralph Fawcett and his older sister Molly through a childhood that is ostensibly privileged—wealthy California relatives, prep schools, summers at a ranch in the Colorado Rockies—but emotionally arid and grotesque. Their mother has died; their father is remote; the children navigate a world of adults who are variously vain, oblivious, or predatory. Ralph, in particular, is one of the great comic inventions of American fiction: a boy in CONSTANT, delirious inner revolt against the phoniness of grown-ups, given to grandiose schemes, paranoid rituals, and a running private commentary of astonishing venom. He hates his fellow students, hates his relations, hates the summer guests who litter the ranch with their affectations, and Stafford renders his hatred with such panache that the reader laughs on nearly every page.

But comedy in this novel is a load-bearing structure, and the laughter it produces is the kind that leaves bruises. Stafford understood something that few writers about children have articulated so precisely: that childhood is not a pastoral but a battlefield, that its central drama is not innocence threatened by the world but ignorance colliding with the world, repeatedly and painfully, until knowledge arrives too fast and too disproportionately. Ralph and Molly experience almost nothing gradually. Their losses—of their mother, of their dog, of each other—arrive as catastrophes without context, and Stafford refuses to prettify the resulting psychic damage. Molly grows inward and hypochondriacal; Ralph grows outward, in explosions of fantasy and rage. Both children are, in the book's exact and terrible phrase, unmothered, and the novel charts what that absence does with a clinical clarity that never becomes cold.

The prose is the great pleasure and, probably, the source of the novel's neglect. Stafford was a stylist of the ornate, witty, Latinate school—her sentences have the composure and casualty-count of a Waugh or a Powell—but she aimed that mandarin style at a child's consciousness and at American landscapes, a collision nobody quite expects. Her descriptive passages about the Colorado mountains are among the finest nature writing in mid-century fiction, pitch-perfect and unsentimental, and herdialogue (the dinner-table conversations at the ranch are a master class) captures the flat, self-serving idioms of the American well-to-do with lethal accuracy. The book asks a real commitment from its reader: you must be willing to find a novel simultaneously hilarious and unbearable, and to accept that its tone will never stabilize into comfort.

And then there is the ending, which I will not spoil except to say that it is one of the most shocking in American literature—shocking not as a stunt but as a culmination, the final demonstration of everything the book has argued about how carelessness and vanity, compounded over years, can end in the destruction of a child. It is the rare ending that changes the meaning of everything preceding it without contravening anything preceding it.

Why did "The Mountain Lion" fade? Partly timing: 1947 was crowded with war novels and social realism, and a comic-gothic study of wealthy children had no obvious constituency. Partly gender: Stafford's gifts were assimilated to the "woman writer of delicate stories" category whenever her reputation was being managed by others. And partly the book itself, which resists the sympathetic softness that makes novels about children easily adoptable. It has never been out of print, exactly, but it circulates now mostly as a writer's writer's book, passed hand to hand like contraband.

That should change. "The Mountain Lion" is a novel of first-rank intelligence about grief, vanity, and the brutal comedy of growing up, written by a stylist at the height of her powers. It is funnier than most comic novels, darker than most dark ones, and truer than most truthful ones. Sixty-some years on, it still has the capacity to ambush a reader completely. Underrated doesn't begin to cover it—unknown, largely, is closer to the truth, and that is our loss, not the book's.
