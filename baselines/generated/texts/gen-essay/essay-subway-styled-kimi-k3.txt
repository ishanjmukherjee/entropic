The Second Avenue Subway's first phase — three stations, 1.8 miles of tunnel, on a line that had been planned since before my grandparents were born — came in at roughly $2.5 billion per mile. Milan built its M4 line through the center of one of Europe's oldest, densest cities for about $250 million per mile. Rome, a city where digging a trench means standing over a meeting with archaeologists before lunch, builds subway for a fifth of New York's cost. These aren't cherry-picked numbers. Alon Levy and the team at the NYU Transit Costs Project have spent years collecting construction cost data from over a hundred projects across five countries, and the pattern holds: the Anglosphere — the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia — builds rail at two to ten times the cost of Spain, Italy, France, or the Nordic countries. New York is the worst offender in the dataset.

I want to be precise about what this essay argues, because the two explanations you usually hear are both wrong. It isn't unions, and it isn't geography. Madrid's subway workers are unionized; so are Milan's. Manhattan schist is famously tunnel-friendly — the engineers who dug the 7 line extension had an easier time than the teams boring under Madrid's waterlogged Mio-Pliocene sediments, and Madrid's metro expansion in the mid-2000s still came in at under $60 million a kilometer. When the best construction jobs in America produce this kind of variance, you can't blame dirt. The difference, as I'll try to show, is institutional: American transit agencies forgot how to build things, and they pay other people to do it badly, at prices that no one in the room has the ability or incentive to question.

Start with how the contract gets written. In Spain, the Consorcio de Transporte Metropolitano de Madrid could hand contractors a design that was close to final because the state employed the engineers who made it. The contractor's job was to build what was drawn. In the United States, by contrast, agencies gutted their in-house engineering staff over the last four decades — a choice that looked like fiscal discipline on a spreadsheet but functioned as a lobotomy. New York's MTA and Boston's MBTA now routinely outsource preliminary design to sprawling consultancies, whose business model rewards billable hours and scope, not speed. The contractors bid on incomplete designs, win with lowball estimates, and then extract the real price through change orders — each one negotiated against an agency that no longer has engineers senior enough to say "no, that's not what the contract means." I've spoken with estimators who describe change order negotiations in which the public side of the table was outnumbered four to one. That asymmetry is the cost.

The Procurement Act of 2012 in New York, and the "lowest responsible bidder" statutes across the country, compound the problem. The law assumes the public is buying a commodity — that a tunnel is a tunnel the way a ream of paper is a ream of paper — so it awards the job to whoever's price is lowest, regardless of that firm's history of burning agencies with change orders. Madrid's construction authority could prequalify contractors on performance and exclude the ones who slow-walk disputes. American agencies are forbidden from doing the same, and the result is an adversarial dance in which both parties understand the bid is a fiction and the real negotiation begins after the ribbon is cut on a project that hasn't been designed yet.

Then there's what I think of as the ambulance-chaser premium, though the term isn't quite fair: the legal and political environment that surrounds construction in the Anglosphere does genuinely make everything slower. In Boston, the GLX extension was delayed, in part, by the requirement to accommodate a single property owner whose easement claims required redesigns of a maintenance facility. In New York, the utility relocation process for Second Avenue involved negotiating with Consolidated Edison and Verizon as though each manhole cover were a treaty. Compare that to Istanbul, where the municipality expropriates what it needs and argues about it afterward, or to Madrid, where the regional government simply had the legal standing to say: this line is being built, your street will reopen in eighteen months, file your claim. Reasonable people can debate which model better respects individual rights; the full-speed-ahead presumption of a unit value is." But these are treaty problems, not engineering problems. A French agency facing a property dispute doesn't redesign the tunnel; it compensates the owner and proceeds. The Anglosphere's combination of fragmented authority, litigiousness, and deference to process — much of it enacted for good reasons, in response to real mid-century abuses — has created a vetocracy in which every stakeholder holds a veto and the meter runs while they deliberate.

There are deeper cultural factors too, and here I want to push back on my own tribe. The transit-critic community — people like Levy, Yonah Freemark, the writers at Streetsblog — tends to frame this as purely a managerial failure: hire better engineers, reform procurement, prequalify contractors. All true. But I think there's something harder to name underneath it, which is that Anglosphere countries stopped treating infrastructure as a normal thing governments do. In Spain, building a metro line is like repaving a road — it's what the state exists to do, and doing it well and cheaply is a source of professional pride, not a political event. In New York, every project is a moonshot, a legacy item, a once-in-a-generation undertaking that must satisfy every constituency and carry every political freight. The Second Avenue Subway's stations have $200-million mezzanines and bespoke public art not because riders need them but because the project's ambition is proportionate to its cost, and its cost is proportionate to its ambition. It's a feedback loop. Once a project is expensive, it must be monumental; once it's monumental, it cannot be cheap.

The fix is not mysterious. Hire back the engineers. Design to 100 percent before you bid. Prequalify contractors and ban the bad ones. Use unit-price contracts so change orders don't become extortion sessions. Tell the lawyers to settle faster and the politicians to cut fewer ribbons over bigger holes. New York could build subway at Milan's prices within a decade if it chose to. The evidence is not speculative: Los Angeles built its first subway segments at roughly Parisian costs in the 1990s, before the institutions decayed; it can decay back into competence. But the choice has to be made by people who understand that the current price is a policy, not a law of nature — and who find that intolerable.
