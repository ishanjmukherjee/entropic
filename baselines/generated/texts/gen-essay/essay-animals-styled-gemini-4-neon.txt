Three years ago, I stood in a muddy paddock in eastern Pennsylvania watching a man named Gary try to coax a 260-pound Berkshire hog onto a trailer. Gary was the kind of farmer food writers weep over. His pigs rooted in oak woods, ate spent brewers' grain from a local IPA outfit, and had names until they were about six months old. This one had been called Barnaby. Barnaby did not want to get on the trailer. He knew—maybe from the smell of the rusted floorboards, maybe from the way Gary’s voice tightened—that the oak woods were over. It took forty minutes, a plastic sorting board, and a lot of screaming. Not a squeal. A high, ragged, unmistakably panicked scream that made my teeth ache.

I bring up Barnaby because whenever I say I think it is wrong to eat animals, someone immediately invokes a farmer like Gary. They don't defend the Tyson broiler sheds in Arkansas where forty thousand birds sit in their own ammonia for six weeks until their legs buckle. Nobody defends that anymore, except the lobbyists. Instead, thoughtful omnivores retreat to the pastoral fantasy. They tell me about rotational grazing, about Joel Salatin, about the "one bad day" philosophy. It is a lovely story. It is also a mathematical hallucination.

Americans eat roughly twenty-seven billion pounds of beef a year, plus another twenty-seven billion pounds of pork and fifty billion pounds of chicken. In 2018, a pair of researchers—Matthew Hayek and Rachael Garrett—calculated what would happen if the United States switched entirely to grass-fed beef. Because pasture-raised cattle grow slower, you would need about 30 percent more of them on the land at any given time just to maintain our current supply, and even then, the country’s existing pastureland could only support about 27 percent of the beef we currently eat. The "ethical meat" counter at your co-op isn't a blueprint for the future; it is a boutique indulgence for people who can afford fourteen-dollar pork chops and want to feel like nineteenth-century yeomanry while they eat them.

Even if the math worked, though, Barnaby is still screaming on that ramp. The real dodge isn't just logistical; it's moral. We have decided that a creature's life is ours to terminate the moment we fancy a BLT, provided we were nice to it first. Try applying that logic to a golden retriever and see how fast the police show up. In the late 1990s, an animal scientist at Penn State named Stanley Curtis taught a pair of pigs—named, with grim humor, Hamlet and Omelette—to manipulate a joystick with their snouts to move a cursor on a screen. They learned almost as fast as chimpanzees. They were better at it than dogs. We are not talking about animated vegetables. We are talking about mammals with rich, anxious, curious interior lives, and we kill about 125 million of them a year in this country alone.

I know the rebuttals. Lions eat gazelles. Humans evolved on marrow and roasted mammoth. Yes, and humans also evolved to die of abscessed teeth at thirty-five, yet I still visit a dentist. Nature is a slaughterhouse, not a moral compass. A lion has no choice and no grocery store; I have a Trader Joe's six blocks away and a functioning prefrontal cortex. That said, I’m not a purist, and I think the vegan movement hurts itself by pretending all animal life sits on the same moral plane. I eat oysters. I eat mussels. A Wellfleet oyster has no brain, no central nervous system, just a couple of rudimentary nerve ganglia. It cannot want to stay in the oak woods. It cannot feel dread. Eating a bivalve is, as far as any neurobiologist can tell, ethically identical to eating a turnip, except the oyster filtered fifty gallons of seawater a day before it ended up on my half-shell.

What bothers me most isn't even the cruelty—cruelty is an old, familiar human vice—it's the sheer, smug dishonesty of how we talk about it. I have more respect for the guy at the steakhouse who shrugs and says, "I know it's messed up, I just like ribeye," than for the foodie who buys a heritage-breed chop and tells himself he is participating in a sacred ecological cycle. The cycle doesn't need you to eat the pig. You just like the taste of the pig. Once you strip away the pastoral marketing and the evolutionary excuses, that is all that is left on the plate: a fleeting sensory pleasure purchased at the cost of someone else's entire existence. I spent thirty years eating meat before I finally admitted to myself that the trade wasn't worth it. I still miss bacon. I just don't miss pretending it was fine.
