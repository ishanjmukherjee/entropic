Remote work is better for software teams than office work, and the strongest evidence for this comes from what engineers do all day. Writing software is a cognitive task. It requires long stretches of uninterrupted concentration, the ability to hold a complicated system in one's head, and a steady rhythm of reading, reasoning, and testing. The open-plan office, whatever its architectural merits, was built for a different kind of work. Its constant low-level noise, its impromptu conversations, and its unpredictable interruptions fragment the attention that programming demands. Remote work, for all its distractions at home, gives engineers control over their own environment, and that control is worth a great deal.

Consider the research on focus. Studies of knowledge workers consistently find that it takes an average of around twenty-three minutes to return to a task after an interruption, though the exact figure is disputed. Whatever the number, the principle holds: a developer deep in a problem loses something real when someone taps them on the shoulder or when a colleague's conversation drifts across the room. Remote engineers can batch their communication, silence their notifications, and choose when to be reachable. They can protect a four-hour block for debugging without performing a public display of busyness. In an office, that same block is often mistaken for unavailability, and the engineer is pulled into meetings that could have been messages.

Remote work also widens the pool of talent. A team confined to one metropolitan area draws from a narrow labor market, and that market is often expensive. Hiring engineers who can work from anywhere lets companies find people with the right skills rather than the right postcode. It lets a parent of young children, a person with a chronic illness, or someone caring for an aging relative remain in a demanding career. It also lets talented engineers in smaller cities or other countries participate in work that would otherwise be closed to them. Software is unusually suited to this, because its primary output is text, code, and artifacts that move easily across networks. The tools for asynchronous collaboration, including version control, issue trackers, written design documents, and recorded demos, were largely built by software engineers for software engineers, and they work well at a distance.

Critics argue that remote work weakens collaboration, mentorship, and culture. These concerns deserve a serious answer, not dismissal. Junior engineers do learn a great deal by overhearing senior colleagues debug, and spontaneous whiteboard sessions can solve problems quickly. Serendipitous encounters matter, and a team that never meets may drift apart. But the office does not guarantee any of this. Many engineers in offices spend their days in video calls with colleagues in other buildings or time zones, and much of the so-called hallway collaboration is simply the same people talking on a different screen. Where remote teams have succeeded, they have done so by making deliberate choices: writing decisions down, documenting architecture, pairing intentionally, and bringing people together in person for planning retreats or periodic gatherings. These practices are good engineering regardless of location. Indeed, the discipline remote work imposes often improves a team's communication, because it forces knowledge out of people's heads and into shared, searchable form.

The mentorship objection is the most serious, and it is also the most solvable. Mentorship at a distance requires intention. Scheduled one-on-ones, code review as a teaching medium, and structured pairing sessions can transmit skill as well as proximity does. Some companies have found that remote onboarding works better when it is explicit and documented, because new hires cannot rely on absorbing unwritten norms by osmosis. An office makes mentorship easier to neglect, not easier to practice.

Finally, consider the question of who bears the costs. Office work imposes commutes, which consume time and money and contribute to fatigue and pollution. It imposes fixed schedules that suit some people and exclude others. It concentrates the expense of real estate, furniture, and utilities on employers, and the benefits of a flexible life are largely invisible to managers who measure productivity by who is present. Remote work redistributes these costs and makes the working day more humane for many people.

No arrangement is perfect, and the best answer for many teams will be hybrid or deliberately mixed. But the default assumption that software teams belong in offices is weakly supported. The work itself favors focus, the talent market favors reach, and the tools favor asynchronous collaboration. When a team builds its practices around those facts, remote work is not a compromise. It is the better arrangement.
