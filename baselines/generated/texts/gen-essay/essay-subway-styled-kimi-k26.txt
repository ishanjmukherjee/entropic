The last time I rode the Seoul Metro, I counted six separate accessibility features in a single station that would have required three environmental impact studies and a ribbon-cutting by a city council member in San Francisco. Platform screen doors, level boarding, tactile paving that actually made sense—none of it felt ornamental, none of it screamed "statement architecture." The entire Line 9 extension, 25 kilometers of fully automated driverless trains through some of the world's most challenging geology, cost roughly $160 million per kilometer. Meanwhile, the Second Avenue Subway's first phase in Manhattan—less than two miles, three new stations—clocked in at $2.5 billion per mile when final invoices came due in 2017. That's not a cost overrun. That's a different category of infrastructure entirely, the way a Fabergé egg is a different category of breakfast.

I've been crawling through this particular rabbit hole for eight years now, starting from a pretty conventional assumption: surely American construction costs reflect something legitimate. Higher wages, tougher regulation, older utilities, the sheer archaeological density of building beneath Manhattan. Each of these explanations has some weight, until you stack them against comparable cases abroad and watch them collapse like wet cardboard.

Take station depth. Transit twitter loves to blame New York's stratospheric costs on the deep caverns required by the city's impossible geology. But the Barcelona Metro Line 9, bored through equally nightmarish conditions beneath a dense medieval city, managed $80 million per kilometer for its fully automated, earthquake-resistant tunnels. Paris's Line 14 extension, running through the same karstic limestone that makes New York builders weep, came in around $280 million per kilometer—expensive, yes, but roughly one-fifth the per-kilometer cost of the Second Avenue job. The geology argument falls apart the moment you stop comparing New York to Phoenix and start comparing it to other old, wet, complicated cities that nevertheless build things.

What I've come to believe—and this is where the hate mail starts—is that American transit costs represent a deliberate institutional choice masquerading as a technical problem. We have built a system designed to make infrastructure expensive, because expensive infrastructure serves the people who plan, contract, regulate, and litigate it. Not riders. Not taxpayers in any meaningful collective sense. The professional class that manages scarcity.

Consider the staffing ratios. The Second Avenue Subway employed roughly 700 sandhogs, actual tunnel workers, at peak construction. It also employed, by MTA documents I've pored over, over 200 "resident engineers" and "construction managers" from various consulting firms, at billing rates that would fund a small nation's cabinet. The Barcelona method—design-build with clear risk assignment, where the contractor actually answers for outcomes rather than generating paperwork—requires a tiny fraction of this oversight phalanx. Alon Levy at the Marron Institute has documented this obsessively: American transit projects carry overhead ratios three to five times those of comparable European or East Asian projects. We're not paying more for better outcomes. We're paying more for the process itself, which has become self-sustaining and self-justifying.

Then there's the grift that presents itself as democratic process. The environmental review for New York's recent Interborough Express—a repurposing of existing freight tracks, essentially a project with no new tunneling—stretched past four years before a single shovel touched dirt. Not because anyone seriously believed the environmental impact was unstudiable, but because each review cycle generates contracts for consultants, opportunities for neighborhood groups to extract concessions, and employment for the professional enablers who translate between community boards and bureaucracies. Paris built an entire circumferential metro line, the M15, through comparable political complexity in less total time than New York spent studying whether to run trains on tracks that already exist. The French process is not less democratic. It's differently democratic— technical decisions made by technical people, with political oversight at the strategic level rather than the rivet level.

I want to be careful here, because the cheap "corruption" narrative doesn't quite capture what's happening. Yes, the Second Avenue Subway's contractor list reads like a reunion of MTA officials' former colleagues. Yes, the "open shop" requirements that certain New York politicians extract function as pure transfers from riders to specific union locals. But the deeper pathology is legal and procedural. American infrastructure law has evolved to make every participant maximally unaccountable for outcomes while remaining maximally billable for process. The SEQR review in New York, NEPA federally, California's CEQA—they do not function as environmental protection in any meaningful sense. They function as employment programs for lawyers and delay generators that allow incumbent interests to extract rents.

The stations themselves tell the story. Notice how American transit stations keep getting bigger as they get more expensive? The Second Avenue Subway's 72nd Street station could host a modest concert. Its cavernous mezzanine, unnecessary for crowd management, was designed to the MTA's "program requirements" that specify minimum ceiling heights and corridor widths with the specificity of a neurotic's bedtime routine. Compare this to the Tokyo approach: standardized station boxes, repeated identically, designed by the system engineers who will operate them rather than the starchitects who will photograph them. The Toei Oedo Line's stations feel almost aggressively functional because they are—expensive enough to last fifty years, cheap enough to build many of.

I found myself unexpectedly furious about this during a recent trip to Copenhagen, where the City Circle Line opened in 2019 at roughly $250 million per kilometer—not Seoul-cheap, but functional, attractive, and built in eight years from announcement to operation. The stations were modest, repetitive, clearly designed by someone who understood what they were actually for. The Danes have strong unions, extensive environmental review, democratic participation. They simply do not permit these legitimate social values to metastasize into veto points for every actor with contingent professional interest in delay.

What would it take to build cheaply in America? The technical answers are well-rehearsed by now: standardization, design-build procurement, limiting change orders, staffing reform. But I increasingly think the political answer matters more. We would need to decide, collectively, that infrastructure is for riders rather than for the elaborate procession of professionals who currently extract value from its slow creation. That decision would be ugly. It would involve confrontations with unions, with community boards, with environmental consultants, with the entire graduate-educated stratum that has built careers in the interstices of American incapacity. No politician has incentive to initiate this confrontation, because the beneficiaries of cheap infrastructure—future riders, diffuse and unorganized—cannot match the concentrated power of process incumbents.

So we will continue to study, review, consult, litigate, and occasionally build—expensively, slowly, badly. The Second Avenue Subway's second phase, the three additional stations that remain unfunded, carries a current price tag exceeding $6 billion. For perhaps a billion dollars, a Korean or Turkish contractor could have the entire thing operating in five years. The gap between these figures is not a technical mystery. It is the measure of what we have decided to value instead.
