A kilometer of subway track in New York City can easily cost more than $2 billion to build. In Madrid, Paris, or Seoul, a comparable stretch of underground rail often costs between $100 million and $300 million. This staggering disparity—frequently a tenfold difference—is one of the most pressing puzzles in modern urban planning. As cities worldwide race to expand public transit to combat climate change and ease congestion, the question of why some wealthy democracies can build subways cheaply while others face cripplingly high costs has taken on urgent importance.

When confronted with these numbers, officials in high-cost countries—most notably the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia—often blame local conditions. They point to difficult geology, dense urban cores, high construction wages, or strict safety regulations. Yet international comparisons quickly dismantle these excuses. Paris and Rome are dense, ancient cities riddled with archaeological hurdles, yet they build transit for a fraction of American or British costs. Sweden and France boast high wages, strong labor unions, and rigorous safety standards, yet their construction budgets remain manageable. The true drivers of transit cost disparities are not physical or economic, but institutional.

One of the most significant cost drivers is how stations are designed and excavated. Stations, rather than the tunnels connecting them, account for the lion’s share of subway budgets. In low-cost countries like Spain and South Korea, stations are typically standardized, compact, and built as close to the surface as possible using "cut-and-cover" methods, where streets are temporarily dug up and then roofed over. In contrast, high-cost countries often build cavernous, bespoke stations deep underground to avoid disrupting street traffic and local businesses. These deep stations require massive mined caverns, oversized mezzanines, and complex ventilation systems. Furthermore, agencies in the Anglosphere frequently treat stations as architectural monuments rather than utilitarian transit hubs, adding hundreds of millions of dollars in unnecessary concrete, steel, and custom engineering.

Equally important is state capacity—the technical expertise held within public transit agencies. Countries that build subways cheaply tend to employ large teams of in-house engineers, planners, and project managers who design projects and oversee contractors directly. Crucially, they also build continuously, maintaining a steady pipeline of projects that preserves institutional memory and keeps a skilled workforce employed. Conversely, agencies in the United States and the United Kingdom have hollowed out their internal bureaucracies over the past several decades, relying heavily on private consulting firms to design, manage, and oversee construction. Because these consultants have little financial incentive to control costs—and because projects are built in sporadic, once-in-a-generation bursts—public agencies lose the ability to spot padded bids, standardize designs, or effectively manage complex engineering challenges.

Fragmented governance further inflates budgets. Building a subway requires coordinating across municipal, regional, and national authorities, as well as private utility companies. In countries like Italy or France, national laws empower transit agencies to swiftly relocate underground water pipes, electrical conduits, and gas lines, often requiring utility companies to move them at their own expense. In the United States, transit agencies rarely possess this authority, forcing them into lengthy, expensive negotiations with private utilities and overlapping local jurisdictions. Compounding this is a political culture of "vetocracy," where local interest groups and property owners can use environmental reviews and litigation to delay projects for years or extract costly concessions, such as extra entrances, soundproofing, or deeper tunnels. Every year of delay adds millions in inflation, financing, and administrative overhead.

Finally, procurement practices and labor rules play a subtle but vital role. High-cost countries often bundle massive, complex projects into single contracts that only a handful of giant construction conglomerates can bid on, stifling competition. Contractors, wary of unpredictable delays and shifting political requirements, bake massive risk premiums into their bids. While high hourly wages alone do not explain the cost gap, inefficient labor practices—such as overstaffing tunnel-boring machines or rigid work rules negotiated in the absence of strong public oversight—can significantly drive up expenses.

Ultimately, the vast disparity in subway construction costs is not a law of nature, nor is it an unavoidable consequence of democracy or wealth. It is the result of specific political and institutional choices. When governments prioritize minimizing temporary surface disruption over fiscal discipline, outsource their core technical expertise, and allow fragmented jurisdictions to hold infrastructure hostage, costs inevitably spiral. Reversing this trend requires rebuilding public sector capacity, embracing standardized designs, and streamlining governance. Until high-cost nations learn these lessons from their peers abroad, their ambitions for greener, more connected cities will remain buried under mountains of unnecessary debt.
