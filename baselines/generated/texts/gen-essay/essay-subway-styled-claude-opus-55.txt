The Second Avenue Subway cost about $2.5 billion per kilometer. Madrid, across the 1990s and early 2000s, built more than a hundred kilometers of metro for something like a tenth of that per kilometer. When I tell people this, the first response is almost always geology, and the second is unions. Both are wrong, or at least wrong in the way that matters. The real difference is that Madrid's transit agency knew how to build a subway, and New York's had forgotten.

I mean that literally. Under Manuel Melis Maynar, who ran the Madrid expansions, the agency kept its engineers in-house and made them decide things. It picked a standard station design and repeated it. It used cut-and-cover where it could, deep boring where it had to, and it ran several tunnel boring machines at once rather than treating each one as a heroic expedition. Melis had a famous rule: no project would take longer than a single four-year political term, because a mayor who might not see the ribbon-cutting has every reason to let a project rot. He also refused to let consultants write the specifications for the work they would then supervise. None of this is exotic. It is simply what a competent client does, and Madrid was a competent client.

The Second Avenue Subway was a client in name only. The MTA hired consultants to design it, consultants to manage the consultants, and contractors who priced in the expectation that the design would change halfway through, because designs at the MTA always change halfway through. The stations are the giveaway. The 96th Street station is roughly three times the length its platforms require, because the back-of-house rooms, the ventilation plant, the crew facilities, and the signal equipment were each specified by a different stakeholder and none of them was told no. Each room might be defensible on its own. Together they produce a cavern that has to be excavated under one of the densest neighborhoods on earth. Stations, not tunnels, are where the money goes, and stations are where a weak client bleeds.

The labor story is real but secondary. Brian Rosenthal's reporting in the Times found tunnel crews of around twenty-five people doing work that crews of nine or ten do elsewhere. That is genuine waste. But it is a symptom. An agency with its own engineers and the political backing to use them can negotiate staffing. An agency that has outsourced its own judgment cannot, because it does not know what the job actually requires, and the people who do know are paid more when the job requires more.

My evidence for this claim is the countries that have gone the other way. The Transit Costs Project at NYU, which Eric Goldwyn, Alon Levy, and Marco Chitti have run for the past several years, has a case study I keep returning to: Stockholm. Sweden used to build cheaply. Then, in the name of modernization, it shifted toward the British model, with a smaller public agency, more design-and-build contracting, and more risk pushed onto private firms. Costs rose. Not to New York levels, but noticeably, and in a country with no sandhogs, no Tammany Hall, and bedrock that tunnelers dream about. If geology and unions were the story, Stockholm should have stayed cheap. It got more expensive because it hollowed out the client.

Italy is the mirror image, and it is the case that should embarrass Americans most. Italy is not a country anyone praises for administrative efficiency. Yet Italian metros come in well under Anglo-American costs, partly because decades of anti-mafia procurement rules forced the state to keep detailed engineering knowledge in public hands. You cannot police a contractor you do not understand. Corruption control produced competence almost by accident.

The objection I hear from people who have worked on these projects is that the American system is more democratic. It gives every community board, every utility, and every property owner a chance to extract something. I take this seriously, and I think it is backwards. Madrid had neighbors too. What it lacked was a process in which every concession was free to the person asking for it, because the cost was spread across a capital budget no one could see. Real democratic control over infrastructure means someone accountable saying "this station will have two entrances, not four, and here is the money we saved." The American version is closer to a veto auction.

So my prescription is unglamorous. Hire engineers into the public agency, pay them enough that they stay, and let them own designs for a decade or more. Standardize stations and stop treating each one as a monument. Cap the consultants. Publish costs in a form people can compare. None of this requires repealing environmental law or breaking a union, though both debates will continue and both have their place.

The hard part is cultural. American agencies have learned to see in-house expertise as overhead and outsourcing as prudence. They have it exactly reversed. The most expensive thing a government can buy is advice about what it should want.
