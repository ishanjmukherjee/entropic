Every explanation of public-key cryptography I was given as a student began with a padlock. Bob mails Alice an open padlock, she snaps it shut on a box, and only Bob has the key. It is a charming picture, and I have come to think it is the worst way to teach the subject, because it describes an operation that modern systems have almost entirely stopped performing. When your browser loaded this page, nothing was encrypted with anyone's public key. TLS 1.3, finalized as RFC 8446 in August 2018, removed that option outright.

The padlock is a fossil of RSA, and RSA got famous for reasons that had little to do with being the right foundation. Martin Gardner wrote it up in Scientific American in August 1977, complete with a 129-digit challenge number that Rivest estimated would take 40 quadrillion years to factor. It took seventeen years. (The plaintext, recovered in 1994, read "The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage.") RSA happened to have a strange symmetry in which one function could both encrypt and sign, and a generation of textbooks mistook that coincidence for the essence of the field.

The essence is in the paper that came a year earlier, Diffie and Hellman's "New Directions in Cryptography," and it is not about locking anything. It is about two people shouting numbers across a crowded room and ending up with a secret that nobody else in the room knows.

Here is the whole thing with numbers small enough to check by hand. We agree publicly on a prime, 23, and a base, 5. I pick a secret, 6, and compute 5^6 mod 23, which is 8. I shout "8." You pick a secret, 15, compute 5^15 mod 23, and shout "19." Now I take your 19 and raise it to my 6, getting 2. You take my 8 and raise it to your 15: also 2. We both hold 2, and we got there because (5^6)^15 and (5^15)^6 are the same number. The eavesdropper heard 23, 5, 8, and 19. To get 2 she needs one of our exponents, which means answering the question "5 to what power gives 8, mod 23?"

With 23 she tries all the possibilities before her coffee cools. With a 2048-bit prime, she is stuck, and that is the core of the idea. Modular exponentiation is cheap in one direction: by repeated squaring, a laptop does it in well under a millisecond. Running it backward, the discrete logarithm problem, has resisted every algorithm anyone has devised since people started looking hard. Nobody has proven it must be hard. We have fifty years of clever people failing, which is a different kind of assurance than a proof and, honestly, the only kind this field has ever had.

Notice what a "public key" is in this picture. It is my 8: the residue left by running my secret through a function that is easy forward and miserable backward. The private key is the 6. There is no padlock or box, and no message yet. The two of us derive a shared number, feed it to a fast symmetric cipher like AES or ChaCha20, and that cipher does the actual work of encrypting. Public-key math touches the conversation for a few hundred microseconds at the start and then gets out of the way.

Signatures fall out of the same asymmetry, and here the padlock story does real damage. People are told that signing is "encrypting with the private key," which is true only of textbook RSA and is nonsense for everything else. A Schnorr or Ed25519 signature is a proof that I know the exponent behind my public value, bound to a particular message, constructed so that it reveals nothing about the exponent itself. It works by blending my secret with a fresh random number, and it works only if that number really is fresh. Sony signed PlayStation 3 firmware with ECDSA using the same "random" value every time, and in 2010 the fail0verflow group recovered the private key with high-school algebra: two equations, two unknowns.

That incident illustrates the other reason I prefer teaching it this way. The padlock makes the scheme sound like a mechanism, and mechanisms either work or they don't. The real thing is a claim about what an adversary cannot compute, and it comes with conditions. RSA encryption, used the padlock way, turned out to be remarkably easy to get wrong: Bleichenbacher's 1998 attack on its padding was still breaking servers in 2017. That is partly why the protocol designers finally removed it.

Today the prime is usually 2^255 − 19, the group is points on an elliptic curve instead of integers, and "raising to a power" becomes adding a point to itself many times. The skeleton is unchanged from 1976. Two people each run a secret through a one-way function, swap the results, and each runs the secret again on what they received. The order of operations doesn't matter, so they meet at the same number.

The padlock explains none of that. Exponents commuting explains all of it, and takes one line of arithmetic.
