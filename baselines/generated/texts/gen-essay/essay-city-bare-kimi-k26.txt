The boxes sat in the hallway for three weeks before I opened them. Not because I was busy—I'd left my job, my relationship, my entire previous life behind with deceptively little effort—but because I needed those cardboard towers to remain sealed, to prove that some part of my history was still intact, still waiting, still possible to return to. This was the fantasy I maintained during my first month in Portland, that I was merely visiting an extended version of myself, that the real me remained in Chicago, preserved in amber, retrievable whenever homesickness finally outweighed stubbornness.

I moved for the predictable reason: I told everyone I needed a change. What I never admitted was that I had started to feel like a ghost in my own routine, haunting the same coffee shops and L stops without generating any new memories. My friend Sarah had moved to Portland two years earlier and sent me a photo of her backyard, actual grass and an actual cherry tree, and something in me cracked open like a geode, revealing this hidden longing for dirt under my fingernails, for weather that required interpretation, for a life I couldn't autopilot through.

The reality arrived gradually, like a Polaroid developing in reverse. First the shock of familiarity: grocery stores are grocery stores, insomnia is insomnia, the particular heaviness of Sunday evening exists in every time zone. Then the specificity of strangeness: the way people here actually stop for pedestrians, how conversations about hiking routes replace discussions about brunch lines, the performative casualness of professional emails signed with "cheers." I found myself overcompensating in both directions, too eager to declare my love for this new place, too quick to defend Chicago against imaginary criticism, performing authenticity for an audience of myself.

My apartment occupied the second floor of a converted Victorian, all original windows that rattled in the wind and refused to close completely. I chose it specifically for its flaws, somehow believing that discomfort would accelerate my transformation. The first winter, I developed an elaborate relationship with the radiator, waking at 3 AM to adjust its valve, listening to it gurgle and hiss like some mechanical pet. I talked to it sometimes, apologized when I banged my knee against it, these small domestic insanities that accumulate when you live alone in an unfamiliar place. The radiator never answered, but its predictable rhythms became a kind of companionship, evidence that something in this new life responded to my presence.

I joined a pottery class in February, the dead month when the gray skies stopped feeling atmospheric and started feeling punitive. The wheel became my metaphor, because of course it did, because I was desperate for narrative coherence, for this experience to mean something beyond random displacement. I failed spectacularly at pottery. My bowls collapsed, my cylinders wobbled, my hands refused the muscle memory that other students seemed to access intuitively. But I kept going, arriving early to wedge my clay with unnecessary violence, staying late to clean the communal tools with something approaching devotion. The instructor, a woman named Gloria who must have been seventy, watched me destroy my twelfth attempt at a simple mug and said, "You're trying to make it be something. Clay doesn't want to be anything. It wants to be clay." I almost cried in that basement studio, not from frustration but from the unexpected relief of permission, of being told that transformation didn't require force.

The friendship with Gloria surprised me, this slow accretion of recognition, the way she'd save me specific glazes she thought I'd like, how I started bringing her coffee exactly how she took it without her ever having told me. I'd moved here believing I was finished with making friends, too old at thirty-two for the vulgar vulnerability of new connection, the exposure of not knowing someone's history, the careful choreography of self-revelation. But friendship, I learned, doesn't always arrive with cinematic drama. Sometimes it's simply continuing to show up, allowing the repetition of presence to gradually become something neither of you could accurately name.

I started walking differently here. In Chicago, I'd moved with urban efficiency, eyes forward, path optimized, treating public space as obstacle to navigate. Portland demanded something else; the hills required pacing, the lack of grid encouraged wandering, and somehow I stopped caring about efficiency, started noticing the particular moss that grew on north-facing trees, the way certain neighborhoods smelled of cedar after rain. My body changed without my permission, thighs strengthening against inclines, shoulders relaxing from their defensive hunch, this physical education that my mind resisted even as my cells adapted.

The boxes eventually got opened, of course, during a February ice storm that trapped me inside for four days. I found things I'd forgotten owning: a sweatshirt from a concert with my college boyfriend, photographs of a version of me who believed everything was leading somewhere intentional, a ceramic bowl my mother made that I'd always found ugly. I threw away the sweatshirt, kept the photographs without looking at them, and washed the bowl, placing it on my kitchen table where it caught afternoon light. It was still ugly, lopsided and glazed in colors that clashed, but I'd lived long enough to understand that beauty wasn't the only criterion for keeping something, that some objects earn their place through endurance, through having survived the absurd improbability of continuing to exist.

I never decided to stay, exactly. The decision accumulated in small moments: renewing my lease, Gloria's birthday when I finally called her my friend, the morning I caught myself giving directions to a tourist and realized I knew this place, not completely, not with the deep knowledge of native belonging, but enough to be useful, enough to have become temporary home for someone else. I still don't know if Portland is permanent, if anyplace is permanent, if permanence itself is anything more than a comforting fiction we tell against the truth of constant flux.

What I know is that the radiator still hisses, and I still sometimes wake to listen. I know that my pottery has improved incrementally, though I still make lumpy, earnest objects that refuse perfection. I know that Sarah moved to Denver last spring, and I helped her pack her boxes, recognizing something in her determined chaos, that same mixture of fear and performance and genuine, terrifying hope. I didn't warn her about anything. There's nothing to warn someone about, really, when they're undertaking the necessary work of becoming unfamiliar to themselves. I just handed her tape for the boxes and said, "The bad days are also the process," which was the truest thing I knew, which remains the truest thing I know.
