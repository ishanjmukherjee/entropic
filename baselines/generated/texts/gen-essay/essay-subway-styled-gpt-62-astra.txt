If you want to understand why a subway costs what it does, take the Q train to 72nd Street in Manhattan and, for once, pay attention on the way out. Look at how far you can walk upstairs, above the platform, before committing yourself to an exit. There is a second railway station up there, in effect, except that no trains run through it. I like the porcelain portraits by Vik Muniz. I like the fact that a passenger who has come up the wrong stairs needn’t go back down. What I would have liked better is a train to East Harlem.

The first phase of the Second Avenue Subway cost roughly $4.5 billion for 1.8 miles and three new stations. Madrid built the 40.5-kilometre MetroSur, with 28 stations, for about €1.6 billion; it opened in 2003, fourteen years before the Q reached 96th Street. These are not prices you can divide into each other and get a meaningful answer. The years differ, the ground differs, and getting under a block of the Upper East Side presents difficulties not generally encountered in a field outside Fuenlabrada. Still, one ought to be able to explain why the field got a whole railway and Manhattan got three stops. My candidate for the decisive difference is the public agency’s ability to say, with technical authority, “No, we don’t need that.”

Consider the hole at 72nd Street. The passenger platform is about 615 feet long, enough to berth a ten-car train. The station cavern is roughly 1,300 feet long. Before anybody writes in: yes, the electrical equipment has to go somewhere. So do the fans. My objection is to costing the job as though the dimensions of that somewhere had been handed down on stone tablets. A room at the end of a mined cavern in Manhattan schist is a rather expensive place to put a cabinet. An engineer employed by the railway, familiar with the cabinet and empowered to tell the architect to put it somewhere else, can earn her lifetime salary before lunch. Nobody will photograph the room she didn’t build.

People have a favorite simpler explanation, usually one that coincides with an opinion they held before they became interested in trains. On the right it’s the sandhogs’ wages. On the left it’s the consultants’ fees. Both camps can produce a genuinely appalling invoice. Neither explains Stockholm, where construction workers are hardly an oppressed and disorganized proletariat and engineering consultants have not been outlawed. Wages matter, but so does the number of people drawing them per hour, and so, especially, does the number of hours spent excavating a room whose chief virtue is that deleting it would require another round of approvals.

What I admire about Madrid’s great expansion under Manuel Melis is almost embarrassingly prosaic. The region kept ordering stations. An engineer who learned something on one could apply it to the next. Standard station boxes went in from the surface where the streets allowed it; the tunnel-boring machine could pass through and carry on. You didn’t need to convene a symposium on the identity of the neighborhood to decide how passengers would get from the platform to the ticket gates. There are good objections to this method. A shopkeeper looking out at a dug-up street has one. But at least somebody on the buying side knew what it would cost to indulge the objection, in money and months, because he had bought the alternative last Tuesday.

New York, by contrast, held a groundbreaking for the Second Avenue Subway in 1972 and another in 2007. You can keep a ceremonial shovel in working order over an interval like that. It’s a bad interval over which to keep a team of estimators. When a railway rebuilds its organization for each job, the most valuable knowledge migrates to the firms that can move their people from one client to another. The public owner can still insist on things. Indeed, insisting on things, in writing, becomes its principal way of demonstrating that it is in charge. What it loses is the ability to distinguish an expensive precaution from an expensive superstition.

I would trade a surprising amount of federal construction money for a modest appropriation that paid a metropolitan railway’s engineers to design the next line before there was any guarantee of building it. This sounds like the scandalous proposal to pay civil servants to produce drawings that might end up in a drawer. It is precisely that proposal. Draw three stations, price them, argue over where the fan goes, send the drawings back. Keep the people who know why the second version was cheaper. Then, when the governor finally arrives with the money and the shovel, have something better to hand him than a list of consultants who might know where to start.
