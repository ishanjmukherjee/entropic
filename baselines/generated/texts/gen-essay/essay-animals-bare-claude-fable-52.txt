Few moral questions touch daily life as directly as whether it is wrong to eat animals. Most people eat meat, most have done so throughout history, and yet a persistent minority of philosophers, religious traditions, and ordinary people have argued that the practice is a serious moral error. The question deserves careful treatment, because the strongest arguments on each side are better than their caricatures suggest.

The case against eating animals begins with a simple observation: many animals can suffer. Pigs, cows, chickens, and probably fish have nervous systems that produce pain, and behavior that indicates fear, distress, and, in many species, something like pleasure and social attachment. If suffering is bad when it happens to a human, it is hard to explain why it would be morally irrelevant when it happens to a pig. Peter Singer famously argued that disregarding an interest solely because of the species of the being who holds it is a prejudice, "speciesism," analogous to disregarding interests on the basis of race or sex. One need not accept the analogy in full to feel the force of the underlying point: the capacity to suffer, not membership in a biological category, seems to be what makes suffering matter.

This argument gains strength from the facts of modern meat production. The vast majority of meat in industrialized countries comes from factory farms, where animals are confined in crowded conditions, bred for growth so rapid that it causes skeletal and cardiac problems, and often slaughtered after short lives containing little that could be called well-being. Tens of billions of land animals pass through this system each year. Against this suffering stands, for most consumers in wealthy countries, the pleasure of taste and the convenience of habit, since a well-planned plant-based diet can be nutritionally adequate for most people. When a serious harm is weighed against a relatively minor benefit, the critic argues, the conclusion follows without any exotic premises.

A second line of argument, associated with Tom Regan, holds that the problem is not just suffering but killing itself. Animals are "subjects of a life," with experiences and a welfare of their own, and to treat them as mere resources violates their rights however gently it is done. Environmental considerations add a third argument: animal agriculture contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, and water pollution, harms that fall on humans as well as animals.

The defenders of meat eating have serious replies. The first is that humans differ from animals in morally significant ways. Humans make long-term plans, enter into reciprocal moral relationships, and understand their own lives as narratives stretching into the future. On contractualist views, morality is a system of mutual obligations among beings capable of recognizing them, and animals stand outside it, owed kindness perhaps but not the full protections owed to persons. Even if animal suffering counts, death may harm a chicken far less than it harms a human, because the chicken lacks the future-directed desires that death frustrates.

A second reply distinguishes killing from cruelty. Many who reject the abolitionist position accept that factory farming is indefensible while maintaining that eating animals raised with good lives and killed painlessly is permissible. Such animals, the argument goes, would never have existed without the practice of farming, and a good life followed by a quick death may be better than no life at all. This view counts against most meat actually sold, but it challenges the claim that eating animals is wrong as such.

Third, there are arguments from nature, culture, and circumstance. Predation is woven into the natural world, and humans evolved as omnivores. Meat is central to many cultural and culinary traditions. For Indigenous communities, pastoralists on land unsuitable for crops, and people facing poverty or food insecurity, animal foods may be a necessity rather than a luxury. Critics respond that naturalness does not establish rightness and that tradition has defended many practices now considered wrong, but the point about necessity is widely conceded: few argue that a subsistence hunter acts wrongly.

Where does this leave the question? The debate reveals more agreement than it first appears. Nearly every serious position accepts that animal suffering matters to some degree, and it is difficult to find a philosophical defense of industrial farming as currently practiced. The real disagreement concerns the harder cases: whether painlessly killing an animal that has lived well wrongs it, how much weight animal interests carry against human ones, and how to act under uncertainty about animal minds.

Reasonable people continue to answer these questions differently. Some conclude that the uncertainty itself counsels restraint, since the cost of wrongly abstaining is small and the cost of wrongly participating in mass suffering is large. Others conclude that moderate consumption of humanely raised animals respects everything that genuinely matters. What seems hardest to defend is not either of these positions but the unreflective middle: eating whatever is cheapest without asking how it came to be. Whatever the final answer, the question itself is one that anyone who eats has reason to take seriously.
