I own three copies of this book. The first is a battered paperback rescued from a library sale in Olympia, Washington, in 1997, its spine cracked at page 74 from a binding flaw common to the first print run. The second is a hardcover I loaned to a girlfriend in 2003 and never saw again; I still sometimes wonder if she kept it. The third is the one I am holding right now, a pristine replacement I bought out of a sense of archival duty. None of these details are sentimental fluff. They are forensic evidence. They prove that a book can be so singular, so stubbornly itself, that you end up stockpiling it against a world that seems determined to let it vanish.

The book is The Experience of Place by Tony Hiss, published in 1990. It has a forgettable beige cover with a sketch of a park bench on the front. No algorithm would ever recommend it to you, and it has been almost entirely digested by the polite oblivion of the urban planning section, filed between Jane Jacobs monographs and hopeful guides to backyard composting. This is a profound category error. Hiss’s book is not about urban planning. It is a radical monograph on the phenomenology of attention, and I am convinced it is one of the half-dozen most important nonfiction books of the late twentieth century. I am also convinced that stating this opinion makes me sound completely unhinged at a dinner party, which is precisely why I am writing it down.

The thesis is sneaky in its audacity. Hiss argues that we possess a kind of perception he calls “simultaneous perception,” a mode of awareness that is distinct from the focused, task-oriented stare we use to read a spreadsheet or dodge a taxi. Simultaneous perception is a low-frequency hum of awareness that takes in the periphery, the textures, the light, the sound of a distant radiator, the proprioceptive sense of your own body occupying a volume of air. More dangerously, he claims that this mode of perception is not a luxury. It is a biological necessity. Our surroundings, he argues, are not a backdrop for our lives but an active ingredient in our cognitive process. When we are placed in a “thin” environment—a windowless corridor lit by a flickering fluorescent tube with a ballast that whines at precisely 120 Hz—our nervous system does not just feel annoyed. It sickens. We experience something akin to perceptual starvation.

This is not the gauzy mysticism of a lifestyle guru telling you to walk barefoot in the grass. Hiss was a staff writer for The New Yorker, and his method is a cold-eyed journalism of the senses, a genre he had to invent because nobody else was doing it. He goes crawling through the Amtrak tunnels under Penn Station to analyze why they induce a specific, low-grade panic. He rides the Staten Island Ferry and dissects the optical joy of the “slip” of the water, the way the prow carves a fold in the gray surface that creates a visual anchor. He visits the Olympic National Forest and talks to a Forest Service researcher named Tom Brown about the measurable physiological response people have to “prospect and refuge,” the deep genetic craving to see far and feel sheltered simultaneously. Brown’s studies tracked saccadic eye movements, the tiny flickers of the eye that indicate the brain is searching for something to latch onto. In a sterile hallway, the eye flails. At the edge of a meadow, it dances gracefully between the near branches and the far horizon. This is the data of the soul.

I learned about the 120 Hz ballast whine from this book. I have never been able to unlearn it. That is the curse and the gift of reading Hiss. He recalibrates your irritation. You are no longer just inexplicably grumpy in a Home Depot; you are now a connoisseur of your own sensory asphyxiation, able to name the exact frequency that is sanding down the edges of your sanity. He uses the term “vraie vie,” a phrase borrowed from the Situationists, to describe moments when the city arranges itself into a coherent, breathing whole—when the light, the crowd, the architecture, and your path through it fuse into a singular, aesthetic event. Once you have this vocabulary, the moments of clanging sensory dissonance become unbearable. You realize that a huge percentage of our built environment, from the open-plan office to the highway strip mall, is an act of violence against a cognitive system we refuse to acknowledge exists.

The reason the book is underrated, I suspect, is that it falls into a no-man’s land of genre. It was reviewed politely upon release, mostly by architecture critics who treated it as a thoughtful accessory to design theory, a book about why trees are nice. This completely misses the point. His project is far more subversive. He is doing for sensory bandwidth what Rachel Carson did for chemical toxicity. He is demonstrating, with journalistic precision, that the low-level, background quality of our sensory world has a direct causal link to our mental health, and that we are, through sheer neglect, engineering a world of sensory deprivation chambers. He is not saying we should all move to a farmhouse in Vermont. He is saying that the Amtrak redesign of Penn Station in the early 1990s—with its low ceilings and claustrophobic choke-points, a project he excoriates with a specific, devastating close reading—was not just an aesthetic failure but a public health error.

Near the end, he tells a story about a man in a hospital bed. The man is dying, and his window looks out onto a brick wall. Hiss describes the texture of that brick, the monotony of the mortar lines, and what it means to spend your final conscious moments in a visual field devoid of depth, change, or life. There is no melodrama. Just a quiet, furious observation about the cruelty of a society that can place a dying man in front of a brick wall and call it adequate. It is one of the most quietly devastating passages I have ever read, a perfect marriage of an essayist’s eye and a reformer’s heart. I read it maybe once a year, whenever I start to doubt that paying attention to the quality of the light in a room matters. It matters. This book will convince you, with a calmness that turns out to be far more persuasive than rage, that the feel of a place is the most pressing political issue no one is talking about.
