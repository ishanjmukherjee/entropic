For most of history, secret communication rested on a single shared secret. If two people wanted to exchange private messages, they first had to agree on a key, a word, a number, or a codebook, and then use that same key both to scramble and unscramble their messages. This approach, called symmetric cryptography, works well, but it has a stubborn logistical flaw: the key itself must somehow be delivered securely. If you could already send the key safely, you might not need encryption at all. And as networks grew to connect millions of strangers, the idea of pre-arranging a secret with everyone you might ever talk to became absurd. Public-key cryptography, developed publicly in the 1970s, solved this problem with an idea that still seems almost paradoxical: a lock that anyone can close but only one person can open.

The core idea is to split the key in two. Each participant generates a mathematically linked pair: a public key, which can be shared freely with the entire world, and a private key, which is kept secret. Anything encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the corresponding private key. A useful analogy is a padlock. Imagine handing out thousands of open padlocks to anyone who asks. Anyone can snap one shut around a box, but only you hold the key that opens it. A stranger who wants to send you a confidential message simply locks it with your padlock and mails it. Even the sender cannot reopen the box once it is closed.

What makes this possible is a class of mathematical problems known as one-way functions, operations that are easy to perform but extremely difficult to reverse. The best-known example underpins the RSA algorithm, named after its inventors Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman. Multiplying two large prime numbers together is trivial for a computer, even if each prime has hundreds of digits. But taking the resulting product and working backward to find the original two primes is extraordinarily hard. No known method on ordinary computers can factor a well-chosen 2048-bit number in any practical amount of time. In RSA, the public key is built from that large product, while the private key depends on knowing the original primes. The math is arranged so that raising a message to one exponent and reducing it modulo the product scrambles it, and raising the result to a second, secret exponent restores it. Computing that secret exponent requires knowing the factors, which only the key's owner has.

Other public-key systems rely on different hard problems. The Diffie-Hellman key exchange, published in 1976, uses the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem. Two parties each pick a secret number, perform a calculation, and exchange the results publicly. Through a clever property of modular exponentiation, each can then combine the other's public value with their own secret to arrive at the same shared number, while an eavesdropper who saw everything exchanged cannot reconstruct it. Elliptic curve cryptography, now widely used, applies a similar principle to points on specially chosen curves, achieving equivalent security with much smaller keys, which makes it attractive for phones and other constrained devices.

Public-key cryptography does more than conceal messages. It also enables digital signatures, which reverse the roles of the two keys. To sign a document, you process it with your private key, producing a signature that anyone can check using your public key. If the check succeeds, the verifier knows two things: the document was signed by the holder of the private key, and it has not been altered since, because even a one-character change would cause verification to fail. Signatures are what let your computer confirm that a software update genuinely came from its developer and not from an impostor.

In practice, public-key methods are slow compared to symmetric encryption, so real systems combine the two. When you visit a secure website, your browser and the server use public-key techniques to authenticate the server and agree on a fresh symmetric key. Everything after that, the pages, passwords, and payment details, is encrypted with the fast symmetric key. Public-key cryptography handles the difficult problem of establishing trust and sharing secrets; symmetric cryptography handles the heavy lifting.

One problem remains: how do you know a public key really belongs to the person or website it claims to? An attacker could hand you their own public key while pretending to be your bank. The usual answer is certificates, digital documents in which a trusted authority signs a statement binding a public key to an identity. Your browser ships with a list of these authorities and checks the chain of signatures each time you connect.

The future holds a new challenge. Large quantum computers, if built, could efficiently solve the factoring and discrete logarithm problems, breaking RSA and elliptic curve systems. Researchers have responded by developing post-quantum algorithms based on different hard problems, such as those involving mathematical lattices, and standards bodies have begun adopting them. The specific mathematics may change, but the elegant underlying idea of a public lock and a private key will almost certainly endure as the foundation of trust on the internet.
