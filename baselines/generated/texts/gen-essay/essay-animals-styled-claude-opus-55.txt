The summer I was nineteen I worked on a chicken-catching crew on the Delmarva Peninsula, and I can tell you exactly what a broiler house smells like at two in the morning. It smells like ammonia strong enough to make your eyes run before you're through the door, and under that a sweetish rot that clings to your hair for days. We worked at night because the birds are calmer in the dark. The crew leader, a man named Delroy who had done the job for eleven years, could carry fourteen birds at once, seven to a hand, grabbed by the legs. I managed eight on a good night. The birds were forty-seven days old and weighed about six pounds, and a good number of them could not walk the length of a sedan without sitting down. Their legs had not kept pace with their breasts. That is what they have been bred for.

I bring this up because the question people ask me, once they learn I spent a summer doing that, is whether I think eating animals is wrong. They want a yes or a no, and the honest answer is that the question is badly posed. "Animals" is doing too much work. The morally serious question is which animals, raised how, and in what numbers, and once you ask it that way you arrive somewhere that annoys vegans and carnivores about equally. My view is that eating chicken is wrong, clearly and badly wrong, and that eating beef from cattle raised mostly on grass is a much smaller wrong, possibly not one at all. And the great dietary shift of the last sixty years, in which Americans swapped red meat for "lean" poultry on the advice of cardiologists and, later, climate campaigners, has been one of the largest increases in deliberately inflicted suffering in human history.

Start with arithmetic, because almost nobody does. A steer goes to slaughter at around 1,300 pounds and yields something like 500 pounds of retail beef. A broiler yields about four pounds of meat. So the meat of a single steer stands in for somewhere north of a hundred chickens. If you believe, as I do, that a chicken's life matters less than a cow's but not a hundred times less, the math is already ugly for poultry. Then look at what each life actually contains. A beef animal in the American system typically spends most of its life on pasture or range with its mother, and the last four to six months in a feedlot. Feedlots are grim, and I won't pretend otherwise. But a cow on a Montana range is living something recognizable as a cow's life. A broiler lives its six or seven weeks on litter soaked with its own waste, with less than a square foot of floor space, in a body engineered to grow so fast that its heart and legs routinely fail. There is no version of that life that is good for the bird. The industry's own veterinarians know this. They just call it "leg weakness" and "sudden death syndrome," which sound like weather.

In 1960 Americans ate about 28 pounds of chicken per person per year. Now it is roughly a hundred. The United States slaughters around nine billion chickens annually, against something like thirty-three million cattle. When a well-meaning friend tells me she's "cut back on red meat" and switched to chicken breasts, I don't think she understands that she has, in moral terms, traded one animal for a hundred and fifty.

The climate argument is the one that gives me pause, and I don't want to wave it away. Beef carries a far heavier carbon cost per pound than chicken does, mostly from methane and land use. A reasonable person could weigh that and conclude the chicken is the lesser harm. I think they're wrong, because I think suffering endured by a creature who can feel it outweighs a marginal increment of atmospheric carbon spread across eight billion people. But I can't prove that ranking, and I notice that people who reach the opposite conclusion tend to have never stood in a broiler house. I've noticed, too, that the climate argument is rarely offered as a reason to eat less meat overall. It's offered as permission to keep eating plenty of it, provided it has feathers.

So where does that leave the larger question? I eat beef, perhaps twice a month, and I buy it from a rancher in Wyoming whose cattle I have seen. I don't eat chicken or eggs from conventional layers, whose lives are arguably worse than the broilers'. I am aware this makes me a hypocrite in the eyes of people with a cleaner line, and they may be right that the cow I eat would rather have kept living. I take that seriously. But a moral position that treats a grass-fed steer and a hundred and fifty Cornish Cross chickens as equivalent, both simply "meat," has stopped looking at the animals.

Delroy, as far as I know, still catches chickens. On my last night he told me he hadn't eaten one in years. He wasn't sentimental about it. He said he'd just seen too many of them try to stand up.
