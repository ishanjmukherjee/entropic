The movers left at four o'clock on a Tuesday, and by five I had discovered that I had no idea where to buy a lightbulb.

This is not a metaphor. I mean it literally. My new neighborhood—I'd landed in Providence, Rhode Island, after eleven years in Chicago—had, as far as I could tell from three sweaty loops around it, one hardware store, and it closed at four. In Chicago I'd had a hardware store two blocks away that was run by a man named Sal who kept a Galaga machine by the register and charged you whatever he thought the part was worth, which was sometimes nothing and sometimes eleven dollars for a washer. In Providence I had a wiki of nothing. My first evening in my new city was spent reading by the light of a lamp with a dead bulb, and I remember thinking, with the special self-pity available only to someone who has just signed a lease: I have made an enormous mistake.

Everyone tells you the first two months of a move are hard. Nobody tells you the hardness is boring. It's not dramatic loneliness, not the moving-to-Paris loneliness of novels. It's the dull administrative loneliness of not knowing which pharmacy is the good one, of having four grocery stores and no way to distinguish them, of standing in the coffee shop on Wickenden Street wondering if you're allowed to be a regular yet or whether regulars must be grown, like orchids, over years. For about six weeks I lived in a state of low-grade假冒 competence, nodding along when people mentioned streets I couldn't picture, saying "oh yeah" about restaurants I'd never find.

I think now that this boredom is the actual work of moving. And I've come to an unfashionable conclusion about it: the "put yourself out there" gospel—join the kickball league, go to the networking happy hour, download the friend-finding apps, treat loneliness as a logistics problem—is mostly wrong. Not just Providence wrong but wrong everywhere.

Here's what actually made Chicago home. Not the six improv classes I took in my first year there, each one populated by people I exchanged exactly nothing with beyond scene suggestions. What did it was the guinea pig. In the spring of 2015 I adopted a guinea pig from a woman in Rogers Park I found on Craigslist, and her building didn't allow him, so she asked me to meet her at the pet store on Western Avenue to buy supplies, and the pet store guy, Manny, talked to me about hay for twenty minutes because it was a slow Tuesday. I went back the next month for more hay. Then for crickets, for a friend's kid's lizard. Then because Manny's cousin made tamales on weekends and sold them out of the back. By the end of that year I knew the mail carrier's name (Donette), knew that the taqueria on the corner did a secret pozole on Fridays, knew that Sal's Galaga machine took tokens from the laundromat next door for some reason neither Sal nor the laundromat owner could explain. None of that was joined. None of it was an event. It was all just repetition plus mild obligation: an animal needed hay every month, so I had an alibi for showing up.

We've professionalized sociability in a way that has ruined it. Every guide to moving somewhere new treats friendship as acquisition—you need three to five close friends, here's how to convert acquaintances, here's the two-drink follow-up text—and the result is that people arrive in cities armed with strategies and leave them scratched raw. The strategy mind is a lonelier mind. When you're scanning a room for connection potential, you're not in the room.

What I did in Rhode Island, once the lightbulb crisis resolved (a neighbor, Rosa, saw me carrying a ladder back from a failed second-loop hardware store hunt and just gave me two bulbs, explaining that her husband bought them "in bulk, like a lunatic"), was refuse, on principle, to join anything for four months. Instead I did the same walk to the same bakery every Sunday at the same time. That's the whole method, if I have one. Pick a small circuit, walk it until it's boring, keep walking it after it's boring. The boredom is the soil. Somewhere around week nine, the man at the bakery, Tarek, started setting aside a sesame loaf he thought I'd like before I said I wanted it. A month later he asked me what had happened to my hand (a box-cutter incident during the unpacking-hallway-shelf period) and I found myself telling him a story I hadn't told anyone in this city, and there it was: the first thing resembling intimacy, and it arrived through bread and repetition, not through any scheduled social activity. I never once had to say "so what do you do."

Is it slow? Immensely. Eleven months in I still couldn't tell you the name of the person across the street. But a city becomes yours the way scar tissue forms—slowly, without your consent, in the exact shape of the small wounds and small repetitions that caused it. The people I know here: Tarek, Rosa, the woman at the letterpress studio who now nods at me through the window as I pass on Sunday. Total elapsed social events attended: zero. Total friends: maybe not zero. It's hard to count what grows this way.

The movers were right to leave at four. Everything worth finding in a new city keeps lousy business hours at first. You have to keep showing up after closing to catch it.
