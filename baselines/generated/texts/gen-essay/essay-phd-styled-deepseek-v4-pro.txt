Let me tell you the single most useful thing anyone ever told me about choosing a research problem, which I promptly ignored for four years and almost sank my doctorate because of. It came from my advisor’s wife, actually, at a garden party in my first term, and she wasn’t even an academic. She was a ceramicist. We were standing by a table of grocery-store vegetable platters and I was explaining, with the unbearable earnestness of the newly admitted, the vast and important questions I intended to solve. She listened, nodded, and said, “It sounds like you’re trying to bake a soufflé before you know how to separate an egg.”

I smiled the smile you give to people who don’t understand the grandeur of your intellectual project. I was going to solve the hard problem of consciousness, or a piece of it anyway, by building a computational model of analogy-making that would finally explain how fluid concepts crystallize from the noise of sensation. I had Hofstadter and Lakoff on my shelf, I had a fresh copy of MATLAB, I was ready. And for years I produced almost nothing. Spinning. Writing code for models that were too ambitious to ever run, reading paper after paper in adjacent fields until my bibliography was a graveyard of abandoned threads. I was drowning in the sublime.

The advice I should have gotten, which I give to every new PhD student now with the kind of aggressive clarity that comes from having done it wrong, is this: choose a problem that is almost embarrassingly concrete. Choose something where the thing you are trying to explain is so small and so specific that you can hold the entire phenomenon in your mind at once, rotate it, and describe its edges. If your proposed research question contains the word “fundamental” or “framework” or “nature of,” you are already in danger. You need a question that can be stated in a sentence a ten-year-old would understand but that, when you pull on just one thread of it, opens into a five-year project.

I eventually crawled out of the hole by accident. In a fit of frustration, I decided to stop trying to model analogy as a whole and instead model how one person, in one specific laboratory experiment from 1998, did one specific thing: noticed that a heat-flow equation was mathematically identical to a fluid-flow equation and used that to solve a problem. That’s it. Microscopic. Kevin Dunbar’s “In Vivo” study, participant E3, session 4. I had the transcripts, I had the moment, I had the precise analogy: heat is like water. It took me eighteen months to build a model that could replicate that single cognitive event, and in doing so I had to solve real problems about structured representation and retrieval that I never would have encountered if I’d stayed up in the clouds. The model was ugly. It had seven parameters. It worked on exactly one analogy. And defending that specificity, the sheer narrowness of it, made me a real researcher.

The objection you will have, because I had it constantly, is that this feels like triviality. Why spend five years on one tiny thing that six other people care about? The answer is that a diamond anvil cell is tiny too, and it lets you see what happens to crystal structure at pressures that exist only at the center of the earth. A well-chosen small problem is not a small contribution; it’s a controlled detonation. When you really, truly explain one mechanism in one context, you’ve built a tool that someone else can use on their adjacent mystery. The grand theorizing comes later, once you’ve earned the right by actually touching the dirt.

There is a practical corollary here about your advisor, and it’s the other half of the same coin. Many new students select a problem based on their advisor’s stated five-year grant trajectory, that glossy vision in the NSF proposal. Don’t. Look at what your advisor actually measures. Look at the data sitting on the shared drive right now, or the apparatus that already works in the basement lab. I know a student in geophysics whose advisor talked endlessly about the mantle’s phase transitions, but what the advisor could actually provide was twenty years of seismic data from the Tonga trench and a set of pre-processing scripts written in Fortran 77 that ran on a single wheezing Sun workstation. The student who grabbed the Tonga data graduated. The student who waited for the mantle-mapping project’s funding to come through is no longer in the program. Your problem must be solvable with the materials at hand, not the materials that would exist in a world where grants arrive on time and instruments don’t drift out of calibration.

This also means you need to develop a taste for what a solvable problem looks like. It’s a skill, not an innate gift. You acquire it by reverse-engineering papers. When you read a paper that genuinely satisfies you, don’t just admire the finding; flip to the methods section and ask what the authors must have had in their hands on day one of the project. A single immunohistochemistry protocol that worked? A corpus already annotated? A mathematical simplification they noticed would reduce a system from four variables to two? You will start to see that the best work in your field operates not in the grand salons of abstraction but in the machine shop of specific affordances. The great George O. Smith, a forgotten Golden Age sci-fi writer, had a rule about plotting: don’t ask what’s interesting, ask what’s wrong. In research terms, don’t ask what’s profound. Ask which joint in the current explanation is the one that actually creaks when you put weight on it.

One last thing, and this is harder to hear. There will be a moment, about two years in, when you hate your little problem. You will find it cramped, inelegant, insufficiently cosmological. You will read Slavoj Žižek or whoever and feel that you’ve abandoned poetry for plumbing. Resist the urge to bolt. That hatred is a phase change in your understanding. It means you’ve internalized the problem deeply enough to feel its constraints as personal insults. Exactly then, you are close to the good stuff. The platypus doesn’t hate its niche. The researcher who has truly mastered a tiny domain can begin to see, through the specific, the faint outline of the universal. The platypus, by the way, has electroreceptors in its bill. You don’t find that out by studying “animal sensing” in general. You find it by spending ten years up to your elbows in a muddy Australian creek, staring at one very strange creature, and asking it questions it can actually answer.
