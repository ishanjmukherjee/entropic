I remember the exact moment I decided to leave. It was a Tuesday in November, the kind of gray afternoon that makes you feel like the sky is pressing down on your chest, and I was standing in the parking lot of my apartment complex holding a bag of groceries that had started to leak milk. The milk was pooling around my shoes, and I looked down at it and thought: I cannot stay here one more day. Not because the milk mattered. Not because the city was bad. But because I had become someone I didn't recognize, living a life that felt like it belonged to a stranger, and I needed to see if I could find myself somewhere else.

I moved to Portland in February, which means I moved to Portland in rain. Not the dramatic, cinematic kind of rain you see in movies, but the persistent, quiet kind that gets into everything. Your coat never fully dries. Your shoes develop a permanent dampness. The air smells like wet stone and moss. I thought it was fitting. I had always believed that moving should feel like something, that there should be a weather event to match the internal upheaval, and the rain delivered.

The first week was a kind of fever dream. I unpacked boxes in the wrong order—books before plates, plates before sheets—and I ate takeout on the floor because I couldn't figure out where I wanted the table to go. The apartment was small, a one-bedroom on the second floor of a building that leaned slightly to the left, and the previous tenant had left a coffee stain on the kitchen ceiling shaped like a rabbit. I stared at that rabbit every night for the first month. It became my only companion.

Loneliness is the thing nobody warns you about. They tell you it'll be hard, they tell you you'll miss your old life, but they don't tell you that the hardest part isn't missing people—it's the silence of not being known. In my old city, I was a person with a history. The barista knew my order. My neighbor would wave from her porch. I had a favorite bench in a park that felt like it belonged to me. Here, I was nobody. I walked through crowds and felt invisible in a way that was both terrifying and, I had to admit, a little freeing. There is a strange power in being no one, in being a blank page. But you can only feel powerful for so long before you start to want to be read.

I made my first friend at a laundromat. She was folding a fitted sheet with the kind of confidence I'd never possessed, and I asked her how she did it. She laughed and said she'd been practicing for ten years. Her name was Maya, and she lived three blocks away, and she invited me to a dinner party that weekend. I almost didn't go. I stood in front of my closet for twenty minutes, paralyzed by the question of what a person wears to a dinner party in a city where she knows no one. I ended up wearing a black sweater and jeans, which I now realize was the correct answer all along.

That dinner was the first time I laughed in my new apartment's city. A real laugh, not the polite kind you give strangers. Maya's friends were loud and argumentative and warm, and they asked me questions about why I'd moved, and I told them the truth, which is that I didn't fully know, and they accepted that. Nobody demanded a better answer. Nobody said I should have tried harder to make it work where I was. They just passed me another glass of wine and moved on to a debate about whether hot dogs are sandwiches.

It took me about six months to feel like I lived there rather than just existed there. The shift was subtle. One morning I woke up and knew which direction the coffee shop was without thinking about it. I had a route I preferred for running. I recognized the sound of the particular bus that passed my building at 7:15. These small recognitions accumulated like sediment until they became something solid, something I could stand on.

I don't know if moving made me a better person. I think that's a story we tell ourselves to justify the disruption, the cost, the grief of leaving things behind. What I do know is that it made me a more honest person. When you strip away the familiar, you're forced to confront what's actually yours and what was just borrowed from the people and places around you. I learned which parts of myself were real and which were performances I'd been giving for an audience that no longer existed.

The coffee stain rabbit is still on the ceiling. I moved out of that apartment a year ago, but sometimes I think about it. I think about how it watched me eat dinner on the floor, how it watched me cry in the kitchen at 2 a.m., how it watched me slowly, imperfectly, build a life from nothing. I never cleaned it. Some things are better left as they are.
