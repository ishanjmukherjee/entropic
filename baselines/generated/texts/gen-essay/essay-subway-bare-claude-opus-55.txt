In 2017, New York opened three new stations on the Second Avenue Subway at a cost of roughly $4.5 billion, about $1.5 billion per kilometer of track. Around the same time, cities like Madrid, Seoul, and Milan were building subways for a fifth to a tenth of that. The gap cannot be explained by geology, wages, or the price of steel. Researchers at NYU's Transit Costs Project, who compared hundreds of projects worldwide, found that the United States, the United Kingdom, Singapore, and Australia consistently build at the top of the cost range, while Spain, Italy, South Korea, Turkey, and the Nordic countries (at least historically) sit near the bottom. The reasons have less to do with engineering than with institutions: how agencies are staffed, how they design stations, how they write contracts, and how they handle politics.

The most visible driver is station design. Tunneling itself, done with boring machines, is a relatively standardized and globally competitive activity, and its cost varies less than people assume. Stations are where budgets explode. New York's Second Avenue stations were dug as enormous caverns, with mezzanines running the full length of the platform and back-of-house space for equipment and staff that far exceeds what comparable systems use. Each station became a bespoke monument. Madrid, by contrast, built shallow stations using cut-and-cover methods, with standardized layouts repeated across the network, and minimal space beyond what passengers need. A station that is twice as long as necessary and twice as deep does not cost twice as much; it can cost several times as much, because excavation, waterproofing, and ventilation all scale steeply with volume and depth.

The second factor is labor, though not in the way people usually think. Construction wages in New York are high, but so are wages in Stockholm and Copenhagen. The difference lies in staffing. Studies of American tunneling sites found crews several times larger than those doing equivalent work in Europe, a result of union work rules negotiated over decades and an agency culture that rarely pushed back. Labor productivity, not the hourly rate, determines what a tunnel costs.

Third, and perhaps most important, is the hollowing out of in-house expertise. Low-cost builders tend to have public agencies with permanent, experienced engineering staff who design projects, supervise contractors, and remember what went wrong last time. Madrid's metro expansion in the 1990s and 2000s was run by a small, empowered team that built continuously, so knowledge accumulated. Many English-speaking countries took the opposite path, shrinking public engineering departments and outsourcing design and oversight to private consultants. Consultants are not villains, but an agency that cannot evaluate their work becomes a poor client. It pays for redundant studies, accepts conservative designs that minimize the consultant's liability, and loses the ability to negotiate. Sweden offers a cautionary example: as Stockholm shifted toward consultant-heavy procurement and new contracting models, its costs, once among the world's lowest, rose sharply.

Contracting practices compound the problem. When agencies try to transfer every conceivable risk to private builders through large design-build packages, contractors respond by pricing that risk in, often generously. Few firms can bid on such massive contracts, so competition thins, and the remaining bidders know it. Cheaper systems tend to break projects into smaller pieces, keep design in public hands, and share unforeseen risks, such as unexpected ground conditions, with contractors rather than demanding they insure against everything. The result is more bidders and lower prices.

Then there is the surface. Building beneath dense cities means dealing with water mains, gas lines, electrical conduits, and telecom cables, often poorly mapped and owned by different entities. In New York, utilities can demand that the transit agency pay for upgrades rather than mere relocation. Add to this extensive environmental review processes that can be litigated for years, community demands for mitigation, and political pressure to minimize any disruption to street life, and agencies end up choosing deeper, slower, costlier methods to avoid conflict. Each concession seems reasonable in isolation; together they can double a budget.

Finally, culture and accountability matter. In low-cost countries, overspending tends to be politically costly and transit officials are judged on delivering kilometers. In high-cost countries, money for megaprojects often arrives in large, irregular bursts from federal governments, which weakens the incentive to economize and invites politicians to load projects with extras. Because these cities build so rarely, each project is treated as a once-in-a-generation event rather than a routine task to be done efficiently.

None of this is destiny. Italy, despite a reputation for bureaucratic dysfunction, builds subways cheaply partly because it reformed procurement after corruption scandals and developed strong public engineering capacity, and its rules on preserving archaeological finds forced careful, standardized methods. The lesson from the cheapest builders is not that they cut corners or pay workers poorly, but that they treat subway construction as a craft to be mastered: keeping expertise in-house, designing modestly, contracting intelligently, and building continuously. Cities that relearn those habits could build several times as much transit for the same money, which may be the most consequential infrastructure reform available to them.
