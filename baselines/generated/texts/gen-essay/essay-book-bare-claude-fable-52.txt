J.L. Carr's A Month in the Country runs to barely a hundred pages, and I suspect that is part of the reason it remains underread. Published in 1980, shortlisted for the Booker Prize, adapted into a quiet film that almost vanished, it has never gone out of print and never really arrived either. People who love it tend to press it on friends with the slightly anxious air of someone lending out a family photograph. It deserves better than that cultish half-life, because it is one of the most nearly perfect short novels in English, and it accomplishes in an afternoon's reading what much longer books about war, memory, and lost happiness labor at for hundreds of pages.

The premise is modest. In the summer of 1920, Tom Birkin, a veteran of Passchendaele with a facial twitch and a stammer, arrives by train in the Yorkshire village of Oxgodby. His wife has left him. He has been hired, under the terms of a spinster's will, to uncover a medieval wall painting believed to lie under centuries of whitewash in the parish church. He is poor enough to sleep in the belfry. In the meadow outside, another survivor of the war, Charles Moon, lives in a tent and digs for the lost grave of the benefactor's ancestor, who for some forgotten reason was buried outside consecrated ground. Over the weeks that follow, Birkin scrapes patiently at the plaster, a great Judgment emerges in reds and blues, and the village slowly absorbs him: he umpires cricket matches, eats Sunday dinner with the stationmaster's Methodist family, and falls silently in love with the vicar's wife, Alice Keach.

Very little happens, and that is the point. Carr understood that healing does not make a plot. Birkin does not have a breakthrough or deliver a speech about the trenches. He simply notices, one day, that his twitch is less bad than it was. The war is almost never described, and yet it sits under every page the way the painting sits under the whitewash. A single scene, in which Birkin accompanies a young girl to visit a dying child and briefly comes apart, carries more weight than chapters of explicit horror would. Carr trusts the reader to feel what is being held back, and that restraint is the book's great moral and aesthetic achievement.

The painting itself is the novel's central image, and Carr handles it with a craftsman's delight. Birkin comes to know the anonymous medieval painter through his work: his expensive pigments, his impatience with the dull bits, his fierce attention to the damned tumbling into hell. One falling figure, marked with a crescent scar, is painted with such individuality that it must be a portrait. The small mystery of who that man was connects, in the final pages, with Moon's excavation, and the solution is satisfying without being cute. But the deeper pleasure is watching one workman recognize another across five hundred years. The book is, among other things, a love letter to doing a job properly, and to the strange intimacy that restoration creates between the living and the dead.

What lifts it from charming to devastating is the narration. Birkin tells the story as an old man, decades on, and he knows what the young man in the belfry does not: that this summer is the happiest he will ever be, and that it will end. The love affair with Alice Keach never occurs. There is a moment in the bell tower when she leans against him and he could turn and take her in his arms, and he does not, and the moment passes, and that is all. Carr refuses to tell us whether this was cowardice or decency, and the old Birkin does not seem to know either. "We can ask and ask but we can't have again what once seemed ours for ever," he writes, and the sentence has the plainness of something actually learned rather than composed.

It would be easy to mistake all this for nostalgia, a soft-focus hymn to haymaking and chapel teas. But Carr is too sharp for that. Moon's secret, when it comes out, reveals the cruelty that the army and the country were capable of toward their own heroes. The vicar, whom Birkin despises, turns out to be a lonelier and more pitiable figure than he supposed. The village's kindness is real, but so is its narrowness. The golden summer is golden partly because it is surrounded by darkness on both sides, the war behind and a diminished life ahead.

Carr was a retired headmaster who published the novel's later editions himself from his house in Kettering, and something of that independence clings to the book. It belongs to no school, flatters no fashion, and wastes no words. Read it in a single sitting, preferably in late summer. Then do what its admirers have always done, and give it to someone else.
