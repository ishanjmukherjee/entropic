At Lockport the Erie Canal did something I particularly admire: it spent good money to let two boats pass each other. To get over the Niagara Escarpment, the builders put five locks end to end, a stone staircase rising some sixty feet. Then they put another five right alongside them. A single flight would have got a barrel of flour from the bottom to the top. It would also have left the man with a descending boat chewing his lip at the top. That second flight is where I locate the genius of the Erie Canal. Americans had been getting things over formidable obstacles for a long time. What they had trouble doing was telling a customer when to expect delivery.

The usual account puts the emphasis on cheapness, and the figures are hard to argue with. Freight that had cost something on the order of $100 a ton to haul overland between Buffalo and Albany could go by canal for about $10. A farmer who previously found it economical to ship his rye only after distilling it could now contemplate selling the rye. But a cheap ride available now and again is a windfall. A cheap ride available on Tuesday, with another boat coming Wednesday if your millstone cracks on Monday, is grounds for borrowing money. I think the canal’s great achievement was to turn an astonishing journey into an ordinary business assumption. Whether that was an unmixed blessing depends rather heavily on whose note came due.

Consider Rochester. The Genesee River had a splendid set of falls for turning millstones and a perfectly exasperating tendency to flow north into Lake Ontario. The customers with money were east. At Rochester the canal went across the river, in a stone aqueduct more than eight hundred feet long. I like the sheer impertinence of that crossing. One stream could now do the grinding while the other took away the flour. In 1820 Rochester had about 1,500 inhabitants; in 1830 it had more than 9,000. Nobody had improved the falls. What had improved was a miller’s chance of running his stones all afternoon without having to wonder where on earth he would put the flour.

There was a peculiar sort of government behind this triumph of private enterprise. New York State borrowed the money, owned the ditch and collected the tolls. Its canal commissioners had to worry about things a wagoner could leave to Providence: the depth of water over a lock sill, for instance, or what to do when two captains both considered themselves first in line. The original channel was only four feet deep, forty feet wide at the surface and twenty-eight at the bottom. Those last two numbers matter. Water makes a handsome level road, but the edges of this one sloped inward; an ambitious captain couldn’t just pull out and overtake as though he were on the Albany Post Road. Somebody had to make rules. Somebody else, generally in no frame of mind to appreciate the grandeur of the republican experiment, had to wait.

The queerest evidence of the value of dependable freight is what happened when it stopped. Each winter the canal froze or was drained, and everybody knew it would. There was a last-boat season, with shippers anxious to beat the closing and freight rates capable of making a latecomer wince. The calendar of a warehouse in Buffalo thus acquired a division more consequential than Christmas: grain that would get out before the ice, and grain on which somebody would pay storage and interest until spring. The ditch had not emancipated commerce from the weather. It had given the weather a line in the accounts.

And whose country had acquired this useful new calendar? In 1838, thirteen years after the canal opened, the Treaty of Buffalo Creek sought to clear the way for the removal of Haudenosaunee people from much of their remaining land in western New York. Seneca opponents fought it; a revised treaty in 1842 preserved the Allegany and Cattaraugus reservations but not Buffalo Creek. I wouldn’t charge that transaction to a lock at Lockport. I would ask anyone calculating the increase in western New York land values to say for whom an increase was good news. A reliable route to Manhattan made land more valuable to a man who meant to sell it and more dangerous to a family that meant to keep it.

For the opening festivities in 1825, Governor DeWitt Clinton carried Lake Erie water east aboard the Seneca Chief and poured it into New York Harbor. The newspapers got their wedding of the waters. My vote for the better ceremony would have gone to the first merchant who watched a loaded boat go past his wharf and didn’t bother to get up. He had missed that one. There would be another.
