I spent three years managing a distributed team at Stripe before begging my way back into an office, and I want to tell you something that will get me uninvited from every remote work conference on the circuit: the fifteen-minute accidental conversation in a hallway has done more for every product I've built than all the carefully scheduled Zoom calls in the world.

This isn't nostalgia. In 2019, I was the zealot telling everyone that office work was a relic, that we'd look back on commutes the way we look back on bloodletting. I'd read the Buffer reports—99% of remote workers want to keep working remotely, sure, because who wants to admit their "freedom" comes with invisible costs? I'd built the rituals. The donut app pairings. The virtual offsites with shipped boxes of cheeses and obscure board games. The "no meeting Wednesdays" that turned out to mean " meetings crammed into every other day plus Wednesday." Remote work doesn't eliminate the problems of office work; it transmutes them into forms that resist easy recognition.

Consider the bug that sat in Stripe's payment reconciliation system for eleven months in 2021. Eleven months. It was a rounding error, tiny, affecting maybe 0.003% of transactions. The engineer who eventually spotted it, Mira, had worked on that codebase for two years remotely from Boise. She'd walked past that logic a hundred times. The person who actually found it was a new hire, Wei, who'd been in the San Francisco office for three weeks. He didn't find it by deep analysis. He found it because he was stuck on a mutual friend's problem, three of them at a whiteboard at 7pm, one of those "hey while you're looking at that" moments that spread like mycelium through a building and never through a screen.

I'm not romanticizing the open plan. God no. The SF office had the terrible concrete everything that SoftBank money buys, and someone on the fourth floor played pan flute music I'm convinced was a hostile act. But the pan flautist was also the person who, when I was muttering about a Kafka consumer issue in the kitchen, said "oh, we fixed that in Treasury last month, talk to Rachel." That conversation didn't happen on Slack. I know because I checked, afterward, compulsively, wanting to prove my own theory wrong. Rachel and I were in three shared channels. We'd never once interacted about consumers.

The telemetry on this is garbage, which is itself revealing. Every pro-remote study measures hours "saved" from commuting, self-reported happiness, retention—you know what's hard to measure? The velocity of information transfer in an embodied space. The way a team develops what engineers at Bell Labs called "critical mass," that density where ideas combust without deliberate ignition. When I moved to a hybrid role and started visiting Stripe's remaining offices twice a month, I could feel the lag. Two days in, my brain would recalibrate to ambient input. I'd remember something about a colleague from the particular way they sighed at their monitor. Remote work demands you already know what you need to know before you ask.

The asynchronous evangelists have their god, and I have prayed at that altar. Write the long document! Make the Loom! Here's what nobody admits: the forty-five-minute explainer video is a tax on the recipient that the maker doesn't feel. I made twenty Looms in 2020. I know, because the analytics dashboard told me, that seven were watched to completion. The median watch time was four minutes. Meanwhile, the five-minute whiteboard session lives entirely differently—iterative, interruptible, accountable. You cannot quietly tab away from a person standing in front of you.

I know the counterarguments intimately because I deployed them myself. The talent pool! Yes, Stripe's remote expansion let us hire an engineer in Tallinn who we'd never have found otherwise. She was brilliant, and she left after fourteen months because she couldn't crack the social layer, couldn't get the discretionary context that let others make decisions she was structurally excluded from. The retention data on remote workers is actually more complicated than the LinkedIn triumphalists suggest; senior remote hires at every company I've since consulted for show faster initial attrition than their co-located peers, and slower attrition once they survive the two-year mark. The interpretation is obvious but unpopular: remote work has a brutal hidden filtering function for cultural integration that we call "self-sufficiency" to make it sound like a virtue.

Childcare? Disability access? Commute misery? These are real, and office-first is a cruel luxury for many. I don't argue for mandating presence. I argue that teams doing ambiguous, creative, rapidly evolving work—software engineering especially—pay a specific and largely unacknowledged tax for remote distribution. The tax isn't in sprint velocity, which you can game with JIRA hygiene. It's in the adjacent possible, in the ideas that never form because the conditions for their formation don't exist.

I'm in Shopify's office in Waterloo now, deliberately. The company went "digital by default" in 2020 and tries periodically to get me to comply. I comply selectively, flying to Seattle or Toronto for intense weeks, then retreating to write. This hybrid is its own kind of charade, I know. The remote members experience me as intermittently present, unreliable. The office members experience my absences. What I've learned is that the only honest remote work is fully distributed—everyone equally adrift—or deeply intentional about the losses. Most companies choose neither. They choose the language of flexibility while quietly promoting the people who show up, who get the shoulder-tap context, who exist in three dimensions to the decision-makers.

The best remote teams I've seen—GitLab, with their thousand-page handbook of asynchronous process, or the small consultancy Ken Muir runs from three time zones—achieve what they achieve through enormous, explicit, taxing investment in what offices do automatically. They're not better. They're compensating, heroically. The question isn't which mode is superior in ideal form. It's which mode's failures are visible enough to fix. Remote work's failures evaporate, attributed to individual communication styles, to "needing to be better at documentation," to personal failing rather than structural design.

I'll never convince the 99% from the Buffer survey. But if you've ever sat with a problem for three weeks, solved it in twenty minutes of accidental colocation, and genuinely tried to understand why—there's something specific and valuable happening in bodies sharing space, something we've barely begun to account for, something we're in danger of building our entire professional infrastructure atop while pretending it doesn't exist.
