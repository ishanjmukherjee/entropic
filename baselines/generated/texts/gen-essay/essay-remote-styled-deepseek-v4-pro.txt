I was in the middle of debugging a race condition in an embedded payment terminal back in 2011—a real hair-puller involving a baud rate mismatch that only manifested when the chip got warm—when Jeff, the hardware lead, leaned over my cubicle wall holding two oscilloscope probes and said, “You’ve been staring at the wrong register file for forty minutes.” He wasn’t smug about it. He just saw the column of hex on my screen and knew. I turned around. He pointed. The bug surrendered ten minutes later. You cannot replicate that on Slack. You cannot replicate it on a scheduled Zoom call where someone’s AirPods are dying and someone else is pretending their camera is frozen so they can eat a sandwich. The idea that remote work is better than office work for software teams is not merely wrong on the margins; it is a wholesale category error, a confusion of individual comfort with collective throughput, and it is costing us the very cognitive overflow that makes hard things possible.

Let me start with something specific that nobody in the remote-first cheerleading squad wants to talk about: the half-life of a question. In an office, when I hit a wall, the time between confusion and resolution is measured in seconds if the right person is within earshot. I once watched a junior engineer at Stripe, circa 2015, spend an entire afternoon trying to optimize a PostgreSQL query that was running 14 seconds on a cold cache. He rewrote the CTE three times, added a partial index, vacuumed the table manually. Nothing. At 4:15 PM, he muttered a curse loud enough that the DBA three desks down heard it through his headphones. The DBA, a man named Keith who had memorized the entire chapter on planner cost constants, swiveled around and said, “Have you checked your random_page_cost setting? We’re on SSDs now. Default’s insane.” The engineer changed one number from 4.0 to 1.1. Query time: 80 milliseconds. The entire transaction—frustration, utterance, swivel, insight, fix—took under 90 seconds. In a remote environment, that moment doesn’t happen. Instead, the engineer writes a carefully composed message in a public channel, waits 25 minutes for a reply, gets a well-meaning but incomplete suggestion because Keith is in a design review and can’t really focus, and then schedules a 15-minute pairing session for the next morning. The bug lives overnight. The deployment slips. The company pays real money for the latency, and nobody puts it on a slide.

Proponents will tell you that remote work is better because it allows for deep work, for uninterrupted stretches of flow. I understand the appeal. I’ve written some of my most elegant state machines alone at a cabin table with snow falling outside and nobody asking me to review a Figma mockup. But software teams are not collections of authors producing independent manuscripts. They are systems for building and maintaining a shared hallucination—the precise, unforgiving hallucination that is a large codebase. That hallucination degrades when we stop overhearing each other. I’m talking about the ambient technical backchannel that office opponents dismiss as “noise.” In 2018, I sat next to a platform team that was migrating a monolith to Kafka. I had nothing to do with their project. But over six weeks of overhearing their arguments—thin walls, open plan, no escape—I absorbed an enormous amount about partition strategies, about the exact failure mode where a consumer group rebalance cascades into a thundering herd, about why they chose a cleanup policy of compact rather than delete. I never attended a single standup. When my own team needed to integrate with that event bus three months later, I already knew the idioms, the gotchas, the person to ask about schema registry versioning. I had acquired it by osmosis, a channel that simply does not exist when your team is a grid of faces on a 13-inch laptop.

The remote absolutists point to productivity metrics. Lines shipped, pull requests merged, story points burned. These are vanity numbers. The real work of software engineering, the work that determines whether a company survives its next five years, is making decisions that prevent the system from collapsing under its own weight. Those decisions happen badly in scheduled meetings and brilliantly in hallway collisions. I was present at a startup in 2013 when an engineer, walking back from the kitchen with a mug of terrible Keurig coffee, stopped to glance at a whiteboard where our CTO had been diagramming a proposed database sharding scheme. The engineer tilted his head and said, “This assumes uniform distribution of tenant IDs, but our top three customers generate 60% of writes. You’re going to create a hotspot the size of the sun.” The CTO erased half the board. That conversation, unplanned and unrecorded, saved the company from a catastrophic launch-day outage. The engineer who spoke up was not on the infrastructure team. He had no ticket to review the architecture. He was just walking past.

The inconvenient truth is that remote work works wonderfully for the kind of work that can be specified in a ticket. And the more a company pretends that all work can be specified in a ticket, the more it drifts toward mediocrity, because the most valuable work is the work nobody knew needed doing until a human being with adjacent expertise happened to glance sideways. I’m not arguing for a return to miserable open-plan hell with no privacy and mandatory five-day butt-in-seat dogma. I’m arguing that we have been dangerously eager to optimize for individual convenience while pretending the collective intelligence stays intact. It doesn’t. It leaks out through the gap between the direct message and the delayed reply, between the carefully worded async update and the unguarded “wait, that’s not going to work” that saves a career. We are trading high-fidelity side-channel information for a high-resolution simulacrum of collaboration, and we’re calling it progress.
