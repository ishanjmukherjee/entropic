A log burns, a nail rusts, and a cake rises in the oven. In each case, atoms find new partners without losing their identities. The carbon in the log becomes part of carbon dioxide, but it remains carbon. Nuclear reactions are different. They can change an atom so fundamentally that it belongs to another element. To understand how people learned to make and use such changes, we have to begin with a discovery that overturned one of chemistry’s most useful assumptions: that atoms themselves could be taken for granted.

Ordinary chemistry takes place largely among electrons, especially those in the outer regions of atoms. When atoms share or transfer electrons, they can form bonds; when those bonds are broken and new ones form, the result is a chemical reaction. A nuclear reaction, by contrast, changes the tiny, dense nucleus at the atom’s center. The number of protons in that nucleus determines the element. Change that number and the element changes. Change only the number of neutrons and the element remains the same, but it becomes a different isotope. Isotopes of an element generally behave much alike in chemical reactions, even when their nuclei behave very differently.

The first clues came not from anyone trying to alter an atom, but from atoms that changed on their own. In 1896, Henri Becquerel found that uranium compounds could expose a photographic plate even when it was wrapped to keep out light. Marie and Pierre Curie investigated this radiation and identified two highly radioactive elements, polonium and radium. Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy later showed that radioactive substances could turn into other elements as they decayed. This was a striking departure from ordinary experience. A chemist could heat or dissolve a substance and change its form, but radioactivity was not a consequence of anything the chemist did to it. The change came from within the atom.

Rutherford’s scattering experiments helped establish that most of an atom’s mass and all its positive charge are concentrated in a very small nucleus. He went on to show that a nucleus could be changed deliberately: by bombarding nitrogen with particles emitted by a radioactive source, he produced oxygen. In 1932, James Chadwick identified the neutron. Because neutrons carry no electric charge, they can approach a positively charged nucleus without the electrical repulsion that a positively charged particle encounters. The neutron thus gave researchers a powerful new way to investigate and alter nuclei.

One result was so unexpected that the chemists who found it initially struggled to explain it. In 1938, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium among the products of experiments in which they exposed uranium to neutrons. Barium was far lighter than uranium. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained the result: the uranium nucleus had split. They called the process fission. The fragments and other products together weighed slightly less than the starting material. The difference in mass corresponded to energy, as Einstein’s equation \(E=mc^2\) predicts. Chemical reactions also involve tiny changes in mass, but the energy released per event in nuclear reactions is typically far greater.

Fission also releases neutrons, which can cause further fissions. In 1942, a team led by Enrico Fermi demonstrated a controlled, self-sustaining chain reaction in Chicago. The discovery had consequences that went well beyond the laboratory. Nuclear weapons devastated Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, making the human cost of the new technology unmistakable. In the following decades, engineers also used controlled fission to generate electricity. A nuclear power plant may seem unlike a coal-fired plant, but both use heat to make steam that turns a turbine. The crucial difference is where the heat comes from: splitting nuclei rather than burning fuel.

Other uses depend less on the energy of nuclear reactions than on our ability to detect what they produce. Doctors can follow a radioactive tracer through the body to learn how an organ is functioning. Archaeologists can estimate the age of once-living material by measuring how much carbon-14 remains in it. Fusion, in which light nuclei join, offers a different lesson. It powers the Sun, yet reproducing the process on Earth in a form that can reliably supply electricity remains difficult. Knowing that a reaction is possible is not the same as knowing how to put it to work.

Nuclear science did not make ordinary chemistry obsolete. The reactions in a kitchen or a living cell can still be understood without asking what happens inside a nucleus. What changed was the meaning of the atom itself. It was no longer merely the durable unit that chemists could count on from one reaction to the next. It also contained a structure with a history of its own, capable of changing spontaneously or under human direction. Learning to distinguish those two kinds of change gave us a better account of matter—and new choices about how to use it.
