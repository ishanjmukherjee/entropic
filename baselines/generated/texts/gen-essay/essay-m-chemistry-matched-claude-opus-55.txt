For most of history, the word "chemistry" described the most profound transformation of matter anyone could imagine. Wood became ash and smoke, iron became rust, and grape juice became wine. Yet in every one of these changes, something stayed stubbornly the same. The carbon in the burning log was still carbon when it drifted away as carbon dioxide. The iron in rust was still iron, merely bound to oxygen. Chemists could rearrange atoms in endless combinations, but they could not change what the atoms were. The alchemists' dream of turning lead into gold failed not for lack of effort but because ordinary chemistry cannot touch the place where an element's identity lives.

That place is the nucleus, a speck at the atom's center roughly a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. Ordinary chemical reactions involve only the electrons, the light particles that swarm around the nucleus. When two atoms bond, they share or exchange electrons, and the energy involved comes from the electromagnetic attraction between those electrons and their neighbors. These energies are modest, a few electron volts per bond. That is enough to warm a room or power a muscle, but not enough to disturb the nucleus, which is held together by a far stronger force acting over far shorter distances.

A nuclear reaction reaches inside that core. It changes the number of protons and neutrons, and because the number of protons defines an element, a nuclear reaction can turn one element into another. The energies involved are typically millions of electron volts per reaction, roughly a million times what a chemical bond releases. This difference in scale explains why a lump of uranium the size of a golf ball can yield as much energy as thousands of tons of coal. It also explains why nuclear processes went undetected for so long. They do not happen in a laboratory flask or a cooking fire. They require either the slow, spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei or collisions violent enough to overcome the electrical repulsion that keeps nuclei apart.

The first clue came by accident. In 1896, Henri Becquerel found that uranium salts fogged photographic plates kept in a dark drawer. Marie and Pierre Curie soon showed that this strange emission, which Marie named radioactivity, came from the atoms themselves and was unaffected by heating, dissolving, or chemically combining them. They isolated two new elements, polonium and radium, whose radiation was fierce. Then, in 1901 and 1902, Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy reached a startling conclusion: radioactive atoms were spontaneously turning into atoms of different elements. Soddy reportedly cried out, "This is transmutation!" Rutherford warned him not to use the word, lest they be dismissed as alchemists.

Rutherford went on to map the atom itself. In 1911 he showed that nearly all of its mass sat in a tiny, dense nucleus, and in 1917 he became the first person to deliberately cause a nuclear reaction, firing alpha particles into nitrogen gas and knocking out protons, turning nitrogen into oxygen. The alchemists' goal had been achieved, though only a few atoms at a time and with no commercial value. Meanwhile, Einstein's 1905 equation E = mc² offered a way to understand the enormous energies involved. Careful measurements showed that the products of a nuclear reaction weigh slightly less than the ingredients, and the missing mass reappears as energy.

The decisive tool arrived in 1932, when James Chadwick discovered the neutron. Having no electric charge, the neutron could slip into a nucleus without being repelled. Enrico Fermi and his colleagues in Rome bombarded element after element with neutrons and produced a host of new radioactive isotopes. In late 1938, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium among the products of bombarded uranium, a result that made no sense until Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch explained it. The uranium nucleus had split nearly in half. They called the process fission. Crucially, each split released additional neutrons, which could go on to split other nuclei, so a self-sustaining chain reaction was possible.

What followed came with astonishing speed and under the shadow of war. On December 2, 1942, beneath the stands of a squash court at the University of Chicago, Fermi's team achieved the first controlled chain reaction. Less than three years later, atomic bombs destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki, demonstrating the new chemistry's terrible power. Peaceful uses soon followed. Reactors began generating electricity in the 1950s, and today they supply roughly a tenth of the world's power. Radioactive isotopes became indispensable in medicine, allowing doctors to image organs and destroy tumors, and in science, where radiocarbon dating unlocked the timelines of archaeology.

The opposite process, fusion, joins light nuclei instead of splitting heavy ones. Hans Bethe showed in 1939 that fusion powers the stars, and humans first unleashed it in the hydrogen bomb in 1952. Taming fusion for electricity has proved far harder, though in 2022 researchers at the National Ignition Facility finally produced more energy from a fusion reaction than the lasers delivered to the fuel.

In little more than a century, humanity learned to work not just with atoms but within them. This new kind of chemistry brought both the deepest dangers and some of the greatest promises of the modern age.
