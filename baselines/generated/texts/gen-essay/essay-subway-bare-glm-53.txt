The cost of building a subway mile varies dramatically around the world. Recent research comparing hundreds of urban rail projects found that New York's Second Avenue Subway cost roughly $2.6 billion per mile, while comparable tunnels in Paris, Seoul, and Copenhagen were built for a fraction of that—often $100 to $600 million per mile. A tunnel in Singapore or Madrid can cost a tenth of what the same tunnel costs in Boston or Sydney. These differences are not explained by geology, density, or the intrinsic difficulty of digging; they are explained by institutions, politics, and the accumulated habits of the construction industry itself.

The first and most important factor is how projects are procured and managed. In countries with low costs, such as Spain, South Korea, and Denmark, transit agencies typically retain a strong, experienced in-house staff of engineers and project managers who design much of the work themselves and write contracts they understand. They use designs that have been built before, standardizing stations, tunnel-boring techniques, and signage across entire networks. When a city builds its fifth subway line using the same station template as its fourth, contractors know what they are doing, suppliers can prefab components cheaply, and lawsuits are rare. In high-cost countries, by contrast, agencies often hollow out their technical expertise and outsource design and management to consulting firms. Paradoxically, this outsourcing does not save money; consultants bill generously, and no single actor is accountable for the whole project. The absence of a stable public client with deep institutional memory means every project is invented from scratch.

Contract structure matters enormously. Wealthy English-speaking countries have embraced what is known as "risk transfer": the public authority tries to pass every conceivable risk—geological surprises, utility relocation, delays—to the contractor, who then prices that risk into the bid with a hefty premium, or litigates over it later. Add to this the American habit of "design-build" packages combining unlike work, change orders issued mid-construction, and contractors who bid low expecting to profit through disputes, and costs escalate. Low-cost countries allocate risk to whoever can best manage it: the agency handles coordination with utilities and utilities relocation, while the contractor digs. Madrid's metro expansions, delivered quickly and cheaply in the 1990s and 2000s, were built with relatively simple contracts, open-book pricing, and an owner who audited aggressively but kept disputes out of court.

Labor is another major driver, though not in the way often assumed. Opponents of transit spending frequently blame union wages, and pay rates do matter, but research suggests that the number of workers and the hours they spend are more decisive. American and British projects staff far more people per-mile than Spanish or Korean ones, in part because work rules and staffing requirements—mandated by agencies themselves or negotiated politically—require more supervision, more inspection, and redundant crews. Overbuilt stations also inflate labor needs: New York stations are cavernous, with entrances oriented to individual property owners rather than to the street grid, and each requires extensive custom architecture, mechanical systems, and community outreach. A basic cut-and-cover station in Madrid or Seoul serves just as many riders at a fraction of the cost.

Political institutions play a subtle but powerful role. Where a single accountable agency controls the project from design through operation, and where that agency has built lines continuously for decades, learning curves are captured. Where projects are episodic—decades between lines, different agencies for planning, funding, construction, and operation—each generation of officials repeats the mistakes of the last, and each election brings new demands for modifications, local hiring, or community accommodation that add cost. In the United States, federal and state funding formulas can actually reward higher costs, since money flows to projects that generate jobs and spending regardless of efficiency. Environmental review and litigation add years of delay; each year of delay carries interest costs and inflation that compound into billions.

Finally, there is the question of standards and their interpretation. Safety and accessibility requirements are laudable, but how they are implemented varies widely. The same fire-safety codes can be satisfied with modest ventilation plants or with vast underground complexes; the same accessibility mandate can mean simple elevators or elaborate mezzanines. High-cost countries often interpret regulations conservatively, layering requirements the regulations never strictly demanded, because no engineer wants personal liability for a judgment call. Low-cost countries interpret codes sensibly and standardize solutions.

The encouraging lesson is that expensive countries are not doomed by geography or labor markets. Spain, Italy, and South Korea are wealthy, high-wage democracies with strong unions and strict safety laws, and they build subways cheaply. The difference lies in institutional choices: keeping expertise in-house, standardizing designs, writing simple contracts, staffing projects leanly, and building continuously rather than episodically. These are reforms any country could adopt—if its political system can summon the discipline to do so. The scandal of subway costs, in other words, is not that tunnels are expensive, but that in some places we have chosen, through accumulated practice, to make them so.
