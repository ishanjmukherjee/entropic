I think Patricia Highsmith's Those Who Walk Away is her best novel, and I have almost never encountered anyone who agrees with me, or who has even read it. The 1967 edition sits on my shelf with a cover price of $5.95, a garish painting of Venice in winter, and a spine cracked at pages 87 and 203 where I have returned too often. The plot sounds, on description, like a self-parody of her work: Ray Garrett, a young American widower, travels to Venice after his wife Peggy has killed herself, or tried to—Highsmith keeps this ambiguous in a way that should feel sloppy but doesn't—and there encounters his father-in-law, Ed Coleman, who blames him for the death and, in the novel's sixth page, shoots him. Ray survives. What follows is a prolonged stalking across Venice and eventually Paris and Tangier, with Ray refusing to report Ed to the police, refusing to quite leave, refusing to explain himself to anyone including the reader.

The Critical Quarterly reviewed it in 1968 as "another exercise in the Ripley manner," which misses the point so precisely it almost interests me more than an accurate review would. The Tom Ripley novels—The Talented Mr. Ripley, Ripley Underground, the rest—operate through action, through schemes and their escalation, through Ripley's increasingly baroque financial and social improvisations. You can summarize them. Those Who Walk Away cannot be summarized because almost nothing happens, or rather, what happens is repetitive to the point of ritual: two men in rooms, two men on vaporettos, two men not quite looking at each other across restaurants. The shooting in the opening chapter is the only violence in the novel. The rest is atmosphere and avoidance.

I read it first in 2003, in a graduate seminar on "criminal modernism" that spent thirteen weeks on The Big Sleep and The Ministry of Fear and nothing by Highsmith at all. I found it remaindered in a Milwaukee bookstore, the stamp of some failed rental library on its title page, and bought it because I had loved Ripley but found the sequels diminishing. What I discovered—and I remember this precisely, the February afternoon, the radiator clanking in my apartment on Murray Avenue—was something closer to Bergman than to Hammett. The novel's Venice is not the Venice of plot. Ray visits no tourist sites, makes no romantic discoveries. He stays in cheap hotels. He eats badly. The light is "the color of old handkerchiefs," a phrase I have never forgotten, the kind of observation that comes from someone who actually spent January in Cannaregio rather than reading other novels about Venice. Highsmith lived there, off and on, from 1962 to 1967, and the book's geography is exacting: the specific bar where Ray encounters a minor character, the Calle de la Maddalena, the vaporetto stop at San Alvise. I have checked these myself, in 2019, and they are correct.

But this is not why the novel matters. What Highsmith understood, and what almost no crime writer has understood since, is that guilt does not require a crime. Ray Garrett may or may not have driven his wife to suicide. He does not know. The novel's refusal to clarify this is not coyness but something closer to phenomenological honesty. Most of us do not know the consequences of our actions; we suspect, we fear, we half-remember. Ed Coleman's pursuit of Ray is equally opaque to himself. He does not want to kill him, exactly—the opening shooting is almost accidental, a gesture that surprises them both. He wants something Ray cannot give, something that may not exist: confirmation that Peggy's death was Ray's fault, which would make it comprehensible, which would make it someone else's. The two men circle each other not because the genre demands it but because they have become necessary to each other, the only witnesses to an event neither understands.

The novel's reception makes a kind of sad sense. Highsmith's biographer, Joan Schenkar, dismisses it in eleven lines. The New York Times called it "a disappointment" and complained of its "slightness of incident." Contemporary readers wanting Ripley's sociopathic competence found instead a protagonist who cries without knowing why, who wanders into relationships he does not want, who lets himself be followed. There is no triumph in Those Who Walk Away, no clever resolution. The final chapter finds Ray in Tangier, Ed having finally abandoned his pursuit, working at something unspecified, living in a room he describes as "adequate," thinking of Peggy without conclusion, without catharsis. The last sentence is: "He went out and bought a newspaper." That is all.

I have taught this novel twice, both times to students expecting crime fiction and receiving something else entirely. The first time, in 2011, a student named Diane Wegner became furious with it, wrote a paper arguing that Highsmith had "forgotten to write an ending," and I found I could not disagree with her without disagreeing with her entire premise about what novels are for. The second time, in 2019, a quiet student who never spoke in discussion wrote me, years later, to say she had reread it during her divorce and understood something she couldn't articulate. I think what she understood is that the novel is one of the few honest accounts we have of living with events that do not resolve, that do not become stories we tell ourselves, that remain simply present in their incomprehensibility. Ray Garrett walks away from nothing and into nothing. The title is bitter irony, or perhaps just bitter. Highsmith lets him survive not because survival is meaningful but because it is simply what happens, the body continuing, buying newspapers, occupying rooms.

I do not know how to convince someone to read this novel. The copies available online are often ex-library, brittle, the acid paper browning. It has never been filmed; it would be unfilmable. The pleasures it offers are those of exactitude and removal, of watching a writer commit to an emotional register so narrow it feels, at times, like a single note held too long. But I have read it perhaps fifteen times, and each time I notice something new: the way Ray never uses his first name, the precise number of days between major encounters, the economies of the hotels. In 1967 Highsmith was forty-six, already known for Ripley, commercially successful enough to take risks, and she produced this strange, unlovable, necessary book. I keep hoping it will find its readers, though I also suspect, cynically, that its obscurity is part of what protects it from misreading. You cannot make Those Who Walk Away into something else. It will not cooperate. It walks away from genre, from resolution, from meaning, and in doing so achieves something I have found nowhere else in her work or in the work of her contemporaries: a genuine indifference to being understood, which turns out, finally, to be its own form of hospitality. You stay with it not because it welcomes you but because it makes no claim upon you at all, and something in that release, that absolute absence of demand, allows a kind of attention that more obliging novels foreclose. At least, this has been my experience. I have no conclusion to offer. I went out and bought a newspaper.
