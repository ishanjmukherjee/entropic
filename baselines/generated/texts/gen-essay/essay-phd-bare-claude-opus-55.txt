The first problem you choose as a PhD student will feel enormously consequential, and in one sense it is: it shapes your first few years, your early publications, and the skills you build. But it matters less than you fear, and the habits you form while choosing it matter more than the choice itself. What follows is advice I wish someone had given me, offered with the caveat that every field and every advisor is different.

Start by accepting that you will not find the perfect problem by thinking hard in a quiet room. New students often spend months reading papers, waiting for an idea to arrive fully formed. Reading is necessary, but understanding a field from the inside comes mostly from doing things: reproducing a result, running an experiment, working through a proof line by line, building a tool someone else described. You learn where the soft spots are by pressing on them. Many good problems begin as an annoyance, a step that doesn't work as advertised or an assumption that seems arbitrary once you touch it. You will only notice these things if your hands are dirty.

Second, prefer problems where you can make progress soon. A good first project has a short path to a first result, even a small one. This is not about being unambitious. Early momentum teaches you how research actually goes: how long things take, what your advisor responds to, what writing a paper involves. A student who spends three years on a single grand question without intermediate results often finishes with neither the grand answer nor the skills that would have come from completing smaller things. Look for problems with a ladder: a modest version you could solve in a few months, and harder versions that open up if the modest one works.

Third, ask why the problem hasn't been solved already. This question is the single most useful filter I know. Sometimes the answer is that it's genuinely hard and many smart people have failed, which should make you cautious. Sometimes it's that nobody cares, which should make you ask whether you care for good reasons. The best answers point to something you have that others lack: a new technique, a dataset, a combination of skills from two fields that rarely talk, or simply a recent development that changed what is possible. If you can articulate your unfair advantage, you have a strong candidate.

Fourth, care about the answer. Research involves long stretches of failure, and curiosity is what carries you through them. Strategic considerations such as what is fashionable, what gets funded, and what leads to jobs are real and you shouldn't ignore them. But a problem you chose only for strategic reasons becomes very heavy around month eight, when nothing is working. Ask yourself whether you would want to know the answer even if you weren't the one who found it. If yes, that is a good sign.

Fifth, be wary of fashion, but don't be contrarian for its own sake. Crowded areas move fast, and a first-year student competing with large, well-resourced groups on the same obvious next step will often lose the race. At the same time, popular areas are popular partly because they are fertile. The sweet spot is often adjacent to the crowd: a question the hot topic raises but that most people are too busy to examine, or an old problem that new methods suddenly make tractable.

Sixth, use your advisor well. Your advisor has seen many projects succeed and fail, and their sense of what is feasible is worth a great deal. Bring them specific ideas rather than asking what you should work on, because concrete proposals get concrete feedback. Listen carefully when they are skeptical, but recognize that advisors are sometimes wrong, especially about ideas that cut against their own work. Over time, the goal is to develop your own taste, and that means occasionally pursuing something they are lukewarm about, ideally in a small, low-cost way first.

Seventh, give yourself permission to abandon problems. Sunk costs are seductive. If you have worked on something for months and the core idea isn't panning out, it is often right to salvage what you can, write up what you learned, and move on. Abandoning a problem is not failure. It is part of the job, and most successful researchers have a graveyard of abandoned projects. What you want to avoid is drifting: neither committing fully nor letting go. Set checkpoints for yourself and make honest decisions at each one.

Finally, remember that the PhD is about becoming a researcher, not just producing a thesis. The most valuable thing you leave with is not any particular result but the ability to find good problems on your own. Every project you choose, finish, or abandon trains that judgment. Pay attention to what worked and why. Keep a notebook of ideas, including bad ones. Talk to people outside your immediate area. Over a few years, you will find that the problems start finding you, and that is the real sign you have learned the craft.
