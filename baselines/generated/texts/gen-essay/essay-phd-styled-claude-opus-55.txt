Every first-year I've ever advised has arrived carrying a copy of Richard Hamming's "You and Your Research," usually printed, sometimes annotated in two colors of ink. Hamming told the Bellcore audience in 1986 to ask what the important problems in your field are, and then to ask why you aren't working on them. It is a magnificent talk. It is also, for a person in the first eighteen months of a PhD, close to the worst advice available, and I'd like to explain why before you spend a year proving it to yourself the way I did.

My own first problem was "make distributed training robust to stragglers." I chose it because it was important, which it was. Google had papers on it. So did three groups at Berkeley. I spent my first fall reading forty-one of those papers (I kept a spreadsheet, which I still have, with a column titled "why this doesn't solve it" that gets progressively more unhinged), and my first spring building a simulator that modeled stragglers as a heavy-tailed delay distribution I had essentially made up. By the following September I had a method that beat the baselines on my simulator and lost to them on the department's real cluster, a grim little 32-GPU machine named kestrel that everyone hated. I could not tell you why. That was the real failure. Not that the idea didn't work, but that I had no way to find out what was wrong, because I didn't understand any single layer of the system well enough to see which one was lying to me.

So here is my thesis, stated as bluntly as I can: your first problem should be one where you are fairly confident you already know the answer. Not certain. Seventy percent, say. You should be able to write down, before you start, the plot you expect to produce, and the work should consist of finding out whether that plot is real.

People hear this as timidity. It isn't. The point of a first problem is not the result; almost nobody's first paper matters, including the ones that win awards. The point is to build, in your own hands, the full loop of research: predict, measure, get surprised, find the bug, find out the bug was actually the finding, write it up, get rejected by a reviewer who has clearly read only the abstract, rewrite, resubmit. You cannot learn that loop on a problem where every outcome is equally plausible to you, because then nothing is a surprise. Surprise is the only signal you have that your model of the world is wrong, and a problem you have no model of cannot surprise you. It can only fail to work.

The second paper I wrote, the one that actually got me through, came out of this. I was annoyed that kestrel's job times varied by as much as 30% between identical runs, and I was pretty sure it was the network. I spent six weeks proving it was the network. It was not the network. It was that two of the nodes had been installed with a different BIOS power setting by a sysadmin who'd since left, and they throttled under sustained load. That is not an important problem in Hamming's sense. But finding it required me to learn how to instrument every layer of the stack, and once I could do that, the straggler problem I had abandoned became tractable in about four months, because now I could see.

A few practical corollaries. Prefer problems with a fast inner loop. If an experiment takes three days, you will get perhaps a hundred iterations in your first year, and you will waste sixty of them on typos. If it takes ten minutes, you get thousands, and judgment comes from volume. Prefer problems where you own the measurement. Someone else's benchmark is someone else's assumptions, and you will spend months discovering them. Prefer problems your advisor finds slightly boring. This sounds perverse, but an advisor who is thrilled by your problem will want to steer it, and you need, at least once, to be the person in the room who knows the most about something, even if the something is BIOS power states.

And avoid, at all costs, the problem that is a famous open question with your name attached as an afterthought. A student in my cohort spent three years on a proposed proof technique for a well-known lower bound. She is brilliant. She left with a master's degree and an aversion to the entire field that I don't think has faded. The problem was important, and it ate her.

Hamming, I'd point out, was talking to people who already had their loops. His audience at Bellcore had years of practice; they knew how to get surprised productively, and the risk for them was that they would spend their competence on trivia. That is a real risk, and it will become your risk around year three. At that point, by all means, go reread the printed copy with the two colors of ink. You will find it hits differently, because you'll finally have something to aim. Until then, pick the small problem you think you understand, and let it show you how wrong you were about it.
