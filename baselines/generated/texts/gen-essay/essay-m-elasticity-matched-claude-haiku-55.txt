Economists like to say that demand curves get flatter over time, and the phrase sounds technical until you watch it play out. Tomorrow, you cannot do much about the price of gasoline. Next year, you can buy a more efficient car, move closer to work, or take the train. Over a decade, cities reorganize around cheap or expensive fuel. The same is true of supply. A wheat farmer facing a price spike cannot plant more wheat until the next season. A mine can take years to open. A factory can take longer. Elasticity is less a fixed property of a good than a function of the clock.

This matters because scarcity is almost always temporary and local, and the mismatch between how fast prices move and how fast capacity can respond creates opportunity. Some businesses exist in that gap. They do not produce much of anything in the ordinary sense. Instead, they hold capacity that sits idle most of the time and sell access to it when everyone else suddenly wants it.

Consider the electricity grid. Demand for power is famously inelastic in the short run. When a heat wave hits on a July afternoon, air conditioners run whether or not the wholesale price has tripled, because households rarely see real-time prices and cannot respond in minutes. Supply is also rigid. Large baseload plants, like nuclear and coal, take hours or days to start and are cheapest to run continuously. The system is therefore balanced by a small fleet of "peaker" plants, often natural gas turbines that cost little to build, can start in minutes, and run only a few hundred hours a year. Their owners earn most of their revenue in a handful of extreme hours. Their business is not generating electricity so much as being available when the rest of the system cannot be.

Ride-hailing offers a faster version of the same logic. A driver's supply is fixed in the moment: the number of cars on the road at 11 p.m. on a Saturday is set by decisions made hours earlier. Demand, by contrast, can spike when a concert ends or rain begins. Surge pricing is a crude but effective way to lengthen the supply curve over a short window, nudging drivers to log in, pulling some riders out of the market, and rationing the rest. The platform profits not from owning cars but from the fact that the mismatch between fixed supply and sudden demand is valuable, and it knows how to price that mismatch.

Cloud computing has built an entire industry on this principle. Companies that buy servers must size them for their peak load, which means most of the time much of the hardware sits idle. Spot instances, which let customers rent unused capacity at deep discounts, exist because the provider can sell idle machines to someone who doesn't need them guaranteed. When a demand spike arrives, the spot price rises, and customers who need guarantees pay premium rates for reserved capacity. The provider is, in effect, running an options market on computing power. Its revenue comes from the difference between what it charges for guaranteed availability and what it would earn from idle hardware.

Hotels have practiced a version of this for decades. Revenue management, pioneered by airlines in the 1980s, treats a room on a given night as a perishable asset. Unsold inventory is worth nothing once the night passes, so prices rise as capacity fills and fall to attract last-minute buyers. The business works precisely because demand is elastic for some customers and inelastic for others, and the operator can separate them through fare rules, cancellation policies, and booking windows.

There is a pattern across these examples. The profitable firms are rarely those that produce the most. They are those that understand time. They keep reserve capacity, price it well, and accept that long periods of low utilization are the cost of being ready for short periods of high demand. This looks wasteful from the outside, and critics often call it rent-seeking. Sometimes it is. But the alternative is a world in which nobody builds the peaker plant, nobody keeps the spare server running, and the first heat wave simply goes dark.

The lesson, then, is not that scarcity is profitable but that the timing of scarcity is. Elasticity is a story about how quickly people and machines can adjust. The businesses that thrive in the gaps between those speeds are, in a sense, selling time: the time it takes for the rest of the economy to catch up.
