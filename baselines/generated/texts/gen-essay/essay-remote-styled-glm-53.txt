I've spent eleven years building software, and about six of those years were spent as part of a fully remote team, so when people ask me where I stand on the remote-versus-office debate, I expect them to be surprised when I say the office was better. Not marginally better. Meaningfully, expensively better, in ways that took me years to even notice because remote work is seductive in exactly the way bad habits are seductive: every individual day feels fine.

The remote years weren't bad years. I lived in Lisbon for a while, worked from a rented apartment in Alfama with a view of a church bell tower, shipped code that I'm proud of. The company was well-run, the processes deliberate, the documentation culture genuinely strong. If remote work were going to work anywhere, it would have worked there. And on paper it did work: our cycle time on tickets was short, our release cadence brisk, retrospectives well-attended. We measured everything. That was part of the problem—we could only measure what we could see.

Here's what nobody puts in a dashboard: the question you don't ask. At the office, in 2019, I was wrestling with a nasty cache-invalidation bug at a payments company in midtown, the kind where writes to a Postgres replica would succeed and then evaporate into the void. I spent two days on it. On the second afternoon, our DBA, a laconic guy named Victor, walked past my desk, glanced at my screen for maybe four seconds, and said, "Is that a connection pooler in front of the replica?" It was. PgBouncer, misconfigured in transaction mode. Four seconds. Two days of my time in exchange for half a glance, and Victor wasn't even trying—he was just walking to the kitchen.

You cannot schedule that. You cannot put it in a Slack channel, because the whole reason it happened is that I didn't know I had a question to ask. The knowledge that Victor had was not "how to fix cache invalidation," it was a vast, unlabeled map of the system's failure modes, and the bug triggered a pattern-match in his head that my head didn't have the data for. The economics of colocated work are the economics of ambient pattern-matching, and they are wildly lopsided: tiny costs (walking past a desk) against enormous savings (two days of engineer time, and I was not cheap).

The standard rebuttal is documentation. Write it down, the remote advocates say, and to be fair, the remote company tried. We had a Confluence graveyard of some 3,000 pages. I know because I counted them once during a boring retrospective. The problem is that what Victor knew could not have been written down, because Victor himself didn't know he knew it. Tacit knowledge is called tacit for a reason. Our Confluence pages documented decisions—why we chose Kafka over RabbitMQ in 2021, what the incident response ladder looks like—but bugs don't read documentation. Bugs manifest in the seams between documented decisions, and the seams are only visible to people who have sat next to each other long enough to absorb each other's intuitions.

The second thing I'll say, and this is the part that gets me in trouble with friends in the industry: remote work is better for senior people and worse for everyone else, and most teams are mostly everyone else. My first two years as a junior engineer, in an office in Cambridge, I learned essentially by osmosis—overhearing the staff engineer gently dismantle someone's design proposal in a hallway, watching how my tech lead handled a 2 a.m. incident page with a customer screaming on the other end of a bridge call. None of that was in my job description. All of it made me an engineer. The juniors I mentored remotely got, in place of osmosis, scheduled things: onboarding documents, weekly one-on-ones, a Slack channel called #questions. They asked perhaps a tenth of the questions the juniors in my office years asked, because asking a question asynchronously means composing it, which means deciding it's worth composing, which mostly means deciding it isn't. Three years in, several of them were still writing code that a colocated junior would have grown out of in one. I don't blame them. Nobody taught them, because teaching, it turns out, is mostly ambient.

Now, the strongest remote counterargument is talent access, and it's real—my Lisbon team had a brilliant engineer in Novosibirsk whom no office could have hired. But people overestimate how often that matters. Most companies are not talent-constrained; they are coordination-constrained. When our team went remote, meetings didn't decrease, they metastasized: everything became a calendar event, because synchronous attention was now a scarce resource that had to be reserved, and people responded to scarcity by hoarding. We had a weekly "sync" that ran ninety minutes and accomplished roughly what ten hallway minutes used to. Multiply that across forty people.

There's also the artillery-shell pattern of information flow: async communication flattens everything into broadcast text, and every individual thinks twice before broadcasting. On the remote team, architecture debates went quiet, decisions got made in small DM threads instead of open channels, and dissent cratered—not because anyone was silenced, but because disagreeing asynchronously means writing a well-argued public message disagreeing with a colleague, and the emotional cost of that is much higher than the emotional cost of saying "hmm, I'm not sure about that" with your face at the edge of someone's monitor.

We haven't gone back yet at my current company, and I understand why: leases are expensive, commutes are miserable, some of my colleagues do genuinely produce more from home. I'm not arguing the office is better for every individual. I'm arguing it's better for the team, and teams are what ship software. The office is a tax every individual pays so that the collective can purchase something individuals can't buy alone: cheap, accidental, continuous exchange of knowledge. That tax used to be invisible because it was bundled with the commute and the lease and everything else we hated about 2019. Now we've unbundled it, and we're discovering, slowly and expensively, that the thing we thought was overhead was the product.
