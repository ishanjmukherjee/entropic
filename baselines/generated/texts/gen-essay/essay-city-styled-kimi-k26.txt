I moved to Chicago in February, which tells you almost everything you need to know about my judgment. I'd accepted a job at the Tribune without visiting after Labor Day, without understanding that the lake generates its own weather system, its own bitter mythology. My first morning, I stepped out of a sublet in Ukrainian Village and the wind hit me so hard my coffee cup cracked against the El platform railing. I stood there watching brown liquid freeze in grooves between the concrete. A man in a Bears hat looked at me and said, "New, huh?" Not a question.

I'd come from Austin, where February means bluebonnet speculation and maybe one last cold snap that everyone treats like a camping trip. I owned exactly one coat, a Patagonia down jacket I'd bought for a ski trip to Telluride I never actually took. Within three weeks its zipper had frozen so completely that I had to cut myself out of it with kitchen shears. I wore the ruined thing anyway, held together with duct tape, because I was too proud and too broke to admit I'd miscalculated so dramatically.

The job was why I'd come, ostensibly. Covering City Hall. But the real magnet was a woman named Maren who I'd met at a journalism conference in Phoenix the previous October. She'd described Chicago with such precision—the particular click of radiators in old brick buildings, the smell of chocolate from the Blommer factory that drifted over the West Loop on humid afternoons—that I convinced myself I was already in love with the city, not with her rendering of it. We dated for four months. She broke up with me in April, standing outside the Logan Square Blue Line station, because she said I'd started "performing Chicago" instead of living in it. She wasn't wrong. I had been taking notes on everything, storing observations like currency, waiting to cash them in for... what? A essay? A personality? I still have a voice memo from March 17th, two minutes of ambient sound from a Polish bar in Avondale where I drank alone and eavesdropped on men arguing about Solidarity. I haven't listened to it in years. I know what's on it: nothing useful, just the desperate archaeology of someone trying to belong too fast.

What no one tells you about moving to a new city is how comprehensively you will misunderstand the geography of your own loneliness. I thought I missed my friends in Austin, and I did, but what I actually missed was the version of myself that existed without effort. In Chicago, I had to rebuild everything from contingency. Where to buy decent coffee. Which branch of the public library had the least aggressive fluorescent lighting. A dry cleaner who wouldn't ruin my shirts—the single remaining professional wardrobe I possessed, since I'd spent my moving stipend on a mattress from Craigslist that turned out to have bedbugs. I remember the exact address: 1847 W. Grace Street, apartment 2R. I remember the smell of the pesticide, how it stayed in my clothes for weeks, how I walked into the Tribune newsroom smelling like a chemical fire and everyone pretended not to notice because we were all dying in our own ways.

The bedbugs forced a certain grim socializing. I started having drinks with other reporters not because I liked them especially but because I couldn't sleep in my apartment. A guy named Royko, no relation, covered the police beat and knew every all-night diner within precinct boundaries. We'd eat eggs at 2 AM while he explained the taxonomy of Chicago corruption, how the 1st Ward was different from the 11th, which aldermen you could call directly and which required intermediaries. I retained almost none of it. But I started to recognize faces at the Billy Goat Tavern. The bartender, a Serbian woman named Dragana, would ask about my apartment without expecting an answer. She'd just slide my Old Style and turn back to her crossword. That recognition—being seen without being demanded—became the foundation of something I couldn't name at the time.

I stayed six years, longer than I'd planned, shorter than the lifers expected. I left for a job in New York, which is its own complicated confession. But what I understand now, what I couldn't have articulated while duct-taping that coat or sleeping in Royko's living room, is that moving to a new city doesn't transform you. It reveals which parts of you were never as fixed as you believed. I thought I was adaptable. I was merely desperate. I thought I was sociable. I was merely absent options. The city doesn't care about your origin story. Chicago in February will kill you indifferently, and the miracle is that some part of you learns to find that indifference almost comforting.

I went back last October for a friend's wedding. The chocolate smell from Blommer hit me outside the Robey Hotel, and I stopped on the sidewalk like a tourist. Dragana still worked the Billy Goat, though her crossword had become a phone game. She didn't remember my name. But she looked at me for a long moment and said, "You got better clothes now." The old coat was long gone, abandoned in a donation bin during my second summer, when I'd finally understood enough to understand what I didn't need anymore.
