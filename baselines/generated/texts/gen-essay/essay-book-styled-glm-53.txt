The first copy of A Meaningful World I ever saw was face-down on a remainder table outside a bookshop in Santa Fe, marked down to six dollars. Wendell Berry's blurb was on the back, which is how I justified buying a book by two people I'd never heard of. That was 2011. I've probably bought and given away a dozen copies since, and I remain convinced that this book — by the mathematician Benjamin Wiker and the literary critic Jonathan Witt — is one of the most underrated works of the last twenty years, and underrated for a very particular reason: it made the philosophical case that would have unsettled both its enemies and most of its friends.

The book's subtitle is Why the Universe Is Not a Fluke or a Frivolity — terrible subtitle, marketing suicide — and its central move is to take the "fine-tuning" argument that religious apologists love and drag it somewhere more interesting. The standard fine-tuning argument runs like a casino在手: if the gravitational constant were off by a hair, no stars; if the strong force were slightly different, no chemistry; therefore, design. Wiker and Witt start there but insist the argument doesn't end at the level of physics. Their claim is that the world is "designed" all the way down and, crucially, all the way up — into Shakespeare, into the periodic table, into Euclid. Meaning, they argue, isn't a veneer painted on neutral matter. It's.structural. The book is organized as an ascent: mathematics, then chemistry, then biology, then the human arts, with chapters on the periodic table as an "elegant musical score" and on Hamlet as proof that genius doesn't reduce toadaptation.

Now, I should confess my biases. I'm a lapsed physicist who spent nine years in condensed matter and now teach a Great Books seminar to engineering students who arrive convinced that beauty is a matter of opinion. I read Wiker and Witt the way a drowning man reads a Coast Guard manual. So take my enthusiasm with whatever salt you like. But here is the thing that keeps me coming back: the chapter on the periodic table is the single best piece of science writing I know of that almost no one has read.

Wiker and Witt walk through Mendeleev's 1869 table and dwell on his counterintuitive act of leaving gaps. Mendeleev didn't just organize the sixty-three known elements; he left blank spaces — shortages — and predicted the properties of the missing ones. For gallium, discovered in 1875, he predicted a density of 5.9; the measured value came in at 5.91, after a lab in Paris initially mismeasured it at 4.7 and Mendeleev wrote a letter telling them their sample was impure. He was right. The kicker is that Lecoq de Boisbaudran named the element after France, and there's a running apocryphal joke that he was really naming it after himself (le coq, the rooster). Wiker and Witt use this to make a point about the deep coherence of chemistry: the table isn't a filing cabinet, it's a score, and the gaps in it are as revealing as the notes. I've taught that chapter to sophomores who had memorized the periodic table for AP chemistry and never once been asked to consider it as evidence that the universe has a taste.

The chapter on mathematics arrives at much the same place via G.H. Hardy. They quote Hardy's A Mathematician's Apology — the 1940 essay where an atheist mathematician flat-out says that mathematical beauty and mathematical truth are, at the level of practice, inseparable, and that he did mathematics because it was beautiful. Hardy is the crucial witness precisely because he's a hostile one. Wiker and Witt's method throughout the book is to assemble exactly these witnesses: Hardy against himself, Crick's "astonishment" at the genetic code against Crick's philosophical materialism, Hamlet against every theory that reduces the play to status display or mate selection. They call this reading the world "rhetorically," which is an unfortunate word for something quite precise: the claim that the universe isn't just lawful but eloquent, and that eloquence is a property of the thing, not of the observer.

Why do I think it's underrated rather than just obscure? Because of what happened when it was noticed. The book came out from InterVarsity Press in 2006 and was immediately filed under Intelligent Design, largely because William Dembski had blurbed it and Wiker had associations with the Discovery Institute. The New York Times review effectively dismissed it as stealth apologetics; reviewers on the science side largely never got past the publisher's catalog. Meanwhile the readers who should have loved it — the ones who've kept Marilynne Robinson and James Wood and The Tree of Knowledge in print — never heard of it. It got orphaned from both audiences. The religious readers wanted courtroom arguments they could deploy; the literary readers wouldn't touch anything with that blurbed genealogy. So it sold to a few thousand homeschooled teenagers and a few dozen seminarians, and vanished into remainder bins in Santa Fe.

And that's a loss, because the book's argument, whatever you think of its conclusions, is one of the few serious attempts in print to build a single account of meaning across nature and art. The anthropology of it — that human beings are the creatures in whom the universe's coherence becomes explicit — is Augustine as much as it's anyone. When I taught the Hamlet chapter last spring, one of my students, a materials science major named Priya, argued the whole book was just the pathetic fallacy with footnotes. Fine. Disagree with it. But the book wants you to disagree with it at full strength, on real ground: Mendeleev's letter to Paris, Hardy's essay, the density of gallium at 5.9. It doesn't hide behind vagueness, which is more than I can say for most of the science-and-meaning books that get reviewed everywhere. The others gesture; this one argues.

Six dollars on a remainder table. If you see it, buy it, and argue with it allnight.
