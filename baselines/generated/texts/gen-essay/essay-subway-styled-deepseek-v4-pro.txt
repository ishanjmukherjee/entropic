I first understood the cost disease not from a white paper but from a single number: $3.5 billion. That’s what New York’s Metropolitan Transportation Authority spent to extend the Q train 1.5 miles under the Upper East Side, a project that took from 2007 to 2017 to complete and involved mining about 180,000 cubic yards of rock. Divide it out and you get roughly $2.3 billion per mile. It was a figure so grotesque I assumed it was a typo. Then I looked at the Madrid Metro. Between 1999 and 2003, Madrid built 47 miles of new tunnel and 38 stations for around $5.3 billion total. That’s about $113 million per mile. You don’t need a degree in civil engineering to know something has gone catastrophically wrong when one city is spending twenty times more to do the same basic thing: dig a hole, line it with concrete, lay track, and wire a third rail.

The standard explanation is the cost of labor, the density of utilities, or geology. These are real but they are not the driver of the gap. Manhattan’s mica schist is actually famously excellent for tunneling; it’s self-supporting, almost a gift from the Ice Age. Madrid’s tunnels under the historic city center passed within three feet of building foundations in loose alluvial soil. The difference isn’t what is under the ground. It’s what is above it: a political and managerial culture that has, over a half century, turned infrastructure from a public good into a machine for extracting rents, generating litigation, and avoiding decisions.

Let’s start with stations. In New York, the Second Avenue Subway stations are caverns, some 1,000 feet long with full-length mezzanines, their cavernous volumes blasted out of bedrock in a mining technique that requires enormous, custom-built ventilation systems. When I visited the 72nd Street station for the first time, my neck craned upward at a cathedral ceiling high enough to accommodate a four-story building. It was beautiful and absolutely unnecessary. In Madrid, stations are functional, with a standard length of 115 meters and a simple, utilitarian box design refined over hundreds of iterations. By reusing a single station design, Madrid didn’t have to reinvent fire-suppression systems, passenger flow models, or structural engineering from scratch on every single site. New York custom-designs its stations the way a fussy architect details a bespoke townhouse, and every bespoke detail is a vector for delay, cost overruns, and a phalanx of consultants billing at $400 an hour.

Those consultants are the second part of the disease. The MTA’s capital projects are managed by sprawling, low-accountability networks of private contractors and sub-consultants who bid on a system designed more for legal liability distribution than for building things. I once spent an afternoon with a tunnel engineer who’d worked on both the Paris Métro expansion and Boston’s Big Dig, and he told me something that stopped me cold. On the French project, the client—the state-owned RATP—maintained a permanent, elite in-house engineering corps that had generational knowledge of the soils of the Île-de-France. They oversaw contractors with real authority because they understood the work at a granular level. On the Big Dig, the state had outsourced its very ability to think. There was no one on the public payroll who could staunchly challenge a contractor’s change order for additional ground freezing because the institutional knowledge had been fired, retired, or scattered to the very consulting firms now billing the public back for it at triple the cost. When you don’t know how to build something yourself, you will pay whatever you are asked to pay.

And then there is the third and most toxic cost: the deflection of political responsibility. In most high-cost Anglophone cities, the planning process isn’t a process; it is a gauntlet of lawsuits and community-board vetos dressed up as democratic engagement. In 2017, I read through the environmental impact statement for a proposed bus lane in a small American city and found, on page 743, a detailed analysis of the shadow the lane might cast on a particular species of butterfly. Seven hundred and forty-three pages for a bus lane. In Spain, the central government overrides local objections for projects of national significance, using a streamlined administrative law process that has its own authoritarian discomforts but which gets tunnels open in four years instead of two decades of litigation. The trade-off is stark: you can have a system where every neighbor has infinite veto power and projects cost $2.3 billion a mile, or you can have functional public transit. You cannot have both.

The core of the argument is not technical; it is a sociological observation about what systems are designed to do. The Paris, Madrid, and Seoul systems are designed to move people. The American and British systems, in their high-cost iterations, have been redesigned into jobs programs, oversized monuments, and outdoor courts for legal stalling. The infrastructure itself becomes a secondary byproduct of the actual purpose, which is the distribution of funds to as many politically connected hands as possible, spread out over the longest possible political cycle, with the maximum plausible surface-level consultation that absolves every elected official from ever having to make a final, irrevocable decision. When I see a price tag of $7 billion for a three-stop extension, I no longer see steel and concrete. I see a society that has become existentially incapable of saying “enough,” drawing a line on a map, and just digging. Until that changes, the cost isn’t the problem. The cost is just the invoice for a deeper failure of nerve.
