Every new PhD student is eventually handed Richard Hamming's 1986 talk "You and Your Research," usually by someone older who means well. The part everyone remembers is Hamming sitting down at the chemists' table in the Bell Labs cafeteria and asking what the important problems in their field were, then coming back later to ask why they weren't working on them. It's a great story. I also think it's bad advice for a first-year student, and I say that having followed it for fourteen months.

My important problem was the crystal structure of a bacterial multidrug efflux pump. Nobody at any lunch table would have disputed its importance. Here is what working on it looked like. I grew twelve liters of E. coli and spent three days purifying the protein in the cold room. Then I set up 96-well sitting-drop trays across a detergent screen and waited between two and six weeks. When something that looked like a crystal appeared, I looped it, froze it, and sent it to a beamline at Argonne. There it diffracted to 8 Å, smeared in one direction, which tells you roughly that you have a protein and not much else. Then I changed the detergent, or chopped eleven residues off the C-terminus, and started again.

One turn of that crank took about six weeks. In fourteen months I got ten turns. That means I was told I was wrong ten times, each time in the same uninformative way.

So here is my advice, and it's narrower and less noble than Hamming's. Choose your problem by its loop time, the interval between having an idea and learning that it's wrong. Give that more weight than importance, more than your advisor's enthusiasm, and more than whether the eventual paper lands somewhere glossy. A problem with a one-day loop gives you several hundred chances to be wrong over a PhD. A problem with a six-week loop gives you maybe forty, and you'll waste the first ten learning to pipette.

The reason is that a first-year student cannot judge importance. You think you can, because you've read the reviews and can recite the open questions, but that's borrowed judgment, and most of it is borrowed from your advisor. I'll be blunt about what advisors call important. Disproportionately, they mean the problems their lab has failed to crack for a decade. That's why those problems are still sitting there for you. Hamming was addressing people who already had taste. You don't have it yet, and you only get it by making predictions and being corrected, over and over, until your guesses improve. Taste comes out of the number of times you've been wrong, so a slow problem starves you of exactly the thing you came to graduate school to acquire.

What rescued me was a postdoc two benches down. She got tired of my sighing and showed me a thermal stability assay. You mix your protein with a dye and heat it on the qPCR machine nobody uses in the mornings, and you watch for the temperature at which it falls apart. (The standard dye, SYPRO Orange, is useless here because it lights up the detergent micelles. You need CPM, which reacts with buried cysteines as they become exposed. I lost a week learning that.) I could run ninety-six conditions before lunch. By four o'clock I knew whether a given lipid, ligand, or mutation made the pump sturdier or flimsier.

In my first month with that assay I was wrong more often than in the entire previous year, and it was the best month of my degree. I started having opinions. I would bet myself that cardiolipin would add four degrees, and then I'd find out. The thesis I eventually defended was about which lipids hold that pump together, a question no one would have named at Hamming's table. A construct that came out of that work later diffracted to 3.4 Å. That happened for the student who came after me.

The obvious objection is that some of the best science is inherently slow, such as longitudinal cohorts, gravitational wave detectors, and anything involving mice. That's true. I'd still tell you not to make the slow thing your problem, or else to find the fast loop hiding inside it. There is almost always one, whether it's a simulation, a pilot assay, or a toy version that runs in ten minutes rather than nine days. If you can't find one, treat that as information about the project.

Measuring loop time before you commit is easy, though almost nobody does it. Don't ask the advisor, who will describe the project as it exists in the grant. Ask the fourth-year student what they found out last week. Ask it exactly that way and watch their face. If the answer is a result, even a dull one, good. If the answer is "we're still optimizing," ask how long they've been optimizing. Then ask to see their notebook from month fourteen and count the entries that end in a number.

Mine had ten.
