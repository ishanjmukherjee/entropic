I stood there holding a box cutter, suddenly aware that I didn’t know a single person within a three-hundred-mile radius. The silence was different here. In my old town, quiet felt like a blanket. Here, it felt like an echo.

I had moved for a reason that sounded good when I explained it to friends back home: opportunity, fresh start, expanding horizons. But standing in an apartment where the light fell wrong through the windows, I understood I had traded the known for the possible, and the possible has a terrifying way of being indistinguishable from the empty.

The first weeks were a study in orientation. I learned the geography of my new life by getting lost repeatedly. I discovered the corner store sold terrible coffee but was owned by a man who would later remember my name. I found the park where old men played chess on Sundays, their concentration so complete it felt like architecture. I began to measure time not by days but by small recognitions — the barista who no longer needed my order, the neighbor who waved first, the moment I stopped automatically locking my doors at certain intersections because I’d learned which shadows were just shadows.

In my old life, I had been busy with the machinery of existing.
