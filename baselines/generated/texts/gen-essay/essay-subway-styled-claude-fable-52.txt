The number I can't stop repeating at dinner parties is this: Phase 1 of the Second Avenue Subway, three stations and about three kilometers of tunnel on the Upper East Side, cost roughly $1.7 billion per kilometer. Madrid's MetroSur, a 40-kilometer loop with 28 stations that opened in 2003 after less than four years of digging, cost roughly what New York paid for two of those stations. That is the price of two stations in one city against a whole line in the other.

People hear this and reach for their favorite villain. Conservatives say unions. Progressives say consultants and profiteering contractors. Engineers say Manhattan schist, or the spaghetti of unmapped utilities under an old city. Lawyers say environmental review. I have spent an embarrassing number of evenings in the Transit Costs Project's database and the case studies that Alon Levy, Eric Goldwyn, and their colleagues at NYU built on top of it, and I've come to think that almost every one of these explanations is true and almost none of them is the cause.

Take them in turn. Stockholm pays Scandinavian wages to a fully unionized workforce and is extending its metro for something like a seventh of New York's unit cost. Rome is threading Line C through two and a half thousand years of archaeology, where a tunnel boring machine can't pass a station box without someone finding a barracks from Hadrian's time, and it still comes in far below anything built in America this century. Istanbul tunnels through miserable geology in an earthquake zone, cheaply. Paris has older utilities than New York and a legal system that does not lack for process. Yes, New York's tunnel crews were overstaffed. The Times found some 25 workers on boring machines that run with fewer than ten elsewhere, and about 200 people on East Side Access whose jobs nobody could identify. That is real money. It does not get you to a factor of ten.

My thesis is that expensive countries are the ones where the public agency no longer knows how to build a subway, and has arranged to pay other people to know for it. Everything else follows from that.

When Manuel Melis ran Madrid's expansion in the 1990s, he had a few dozen engineers on the public payroll who had built tunnels themselves. They chose the construction method. They standardized the stations, plain boxes dug down from the street, and they broke the work into contracts small enough that a mid-sized firm could bid. They made design decisions in days because the person deciding understood the drawing in front of him. Milan does something similar through MM, an engineering company the city owns. In those systems the state is a client with opinions.

The MTA managed Second Avenue with a thin layer of in-house staff over a thick layer of consultants. A client like that cannot say no, because it lacks the standing to know when no is the right answer. So every internal department that wanted its own crew room, its own storage, its own separate back-of-house space got one, and the stations ballooned. The cavern at 96th Street is well over twice the length of the platform it serves. The mezzanines run the full length of the stations, magnificent and empty. Nobody decided that New York needed the largest subway stations in the world. They accreted, because every request was reasonable on its own and no one in the building had both the authority and the technical confidence to refuse.

The same weakness explains the mining. Cut-and-cover stations are far cheaper than caverns blasted out from below, but they mean tearing up an avenue for a couple of years, and that means a fight with merchants and co-op boards. A confident agency takes the fight, compensates people, and gets it over with. A timid one buys its way around the conflict by going deep, then pays again in escalators, ventilation, and decades of maintenance. It also explains the contracts. An agency that can't evaluate a bid on technical merit falls back on lowest price, the low bidder makes its margin on change orders, and each change order goes to a consultant for review. Boston's Green Line Extension reached a $3 billion estimate with about half a dozen full-time agency staff overseeing it. The fix was mostly hiring people and deleting station architecture, and it knocked off roughly $700 million.

The strongest evidence for my view is that the disease travels with the management style. Toronto built the Sheppard line in the 1990s at unremarkable cost. Since then Canada has adopted the British fashion for outsourced project delivery, with its special-purpose agencies and layers of advisors, and the Ontario Line is now priced near a billion Canadian dollars per kilometer. The rock under Toronto did not change, and neither did the unions.

This is an uncomfortable conclusion for Americans, because the cure is the thing we have spent forty years being told is the sickness. The cure is a larger, better-paid, permanent public engineering staff, with the power to overrule both the fire marshal's wish list and the neighborhood's. I'd pay a chief engineer a million dollars a year without blinking. The stations at 72nd and 86th Streets cost us roughly 1,500 times that.
