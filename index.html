how to read this

Underneath, a language model is a next-word predictor trained on a large slice of the public internet. For every token of a text (a word or a piece of one), we ask how much probability the model gave to the token actually written, given everything before it. That probability is turned into surprisal: 0 bits means the model was certain, and each extra bit means half as likely. A token at 10 bits was a one-in-a-thousand guess.

Single tokens are noisy: any name or number is a surprise. So the page shades passages of one to three sentences by their average surprisal, which is steadier and easier to read. Hover or tap a passage to see its average and the tokens that drove it. Tick "per token" to shade every token on its own.

Red is text the model saw coming. Where a page is mostly red, it restates what the model already knows, and a reader could have got it by asking one. Green is where the writer adds something the model had no way to anticipate: a claim, a detail, a turn of phrase.

The colours are set against the reference texts for the kind of writing picked above the chart. Full red is where the most predictable tenth of model-written passages sit. Full green is where the most surprising tenth of passages by people sit. The neutral point is the value that best separates the two.

Caveats worth keeping in mind. Names, numbers and typos are surprising without being insightful, so read green as "information", not "quality". Text the model has read before comes out red whoever wrote it: Dickens and Shakespeare score lower than anything a model wrote for this study. Kind matters more than authorship, so a poem should be compared with poems and code with code. And later parts of a text are judged knowing the earlier parts, so a term you coined turns red once you have used it a few times.

This is not a detector. A low score does not show that a model wrote something, and a high one does not show that a person did. Models coached on voice land much closer to people than models given a one-line prompt, and the chart shows how much the groups overlap.

The idea follows Gwern's advice on writing for LLMs: the text worth publishing is the text a model could not have produced on its own.

about the reference texts

Writing by people comes in two sorts. New pieces were published after the scoring model was trained, from August 2026 on, so it cannot have read them; that makes them the fair comparison for something you wrote yourself. Older pieces, from before July 2025 and sorted by how well known they are, show what happens when the model has seen a text: the more famous, the closer to zero.

Writing by models comes from several families (Moonshot, DeepSeek, Zhipu, Alibaba, Mistral, Meta, Google, OpenAI and Anthropic), each given the same prompts once, with no picking among outputs. Some prompts were a single line; others coached the model on voice or craft. The prized rows hold model writing that people singled out as good, most of it old enough for the scoring model to have read.

Every text is measured on its opening 3,000 characters, about 500 words, so the marker for your text uses its opening too. The scoring model is , given the text with no instructions and no chat formatting. That setup was chosen by a preregistered test, and the plan for the reference texts was written down before any were scored.

The study is unfinished. Academic papers, and the essays written by GPT, Gemini, Grok and Claude Opus, have been collected but not scored yet, so they are missing from the chart.

Nearly every text in the chart is also on the shelf: click its dot, or its entry in the list, to see the heatmap. Each links to its source, and only the opening that was scored is shown. Song lyrics from commercial releases, books still in copyright and studio screenplays are counted in the chart but not reproduced; their dots are hollow and do not open.

settings and privacy

Texts from the shelf were scored ahead of time and are loaded from this site. A text you paste is sent to a small relay, which passes it to Featherless for scoring. Neither this site nor the relay stores it; the relay only counts characters to keep spending in check.

Scoring runs on a small shared budget, which caps each text at about 10,000 words and can run out for the day. If it does, or you want longer texts, paste your own Featherless API key below. It is kept only in this browser and is sent only to Featherless.
